Think about the cast list taped to the drama-club door after auditions. Everyone crowds in and scans for one thing: their own name. You do not read the list. You hunt for a word. If the list had you down as "the tall kid from third period" instead of your name, you would walk right past it — even though you are on it.

A recruiter's first pass works like that. The last lesson showed that she checks your page against a short, named list — the posting's basic qualifications — and that the check happens fast and happens first. This lesson is about what has to be true of the page for that check to succeed. The words the posting uses have to actually appear, attached to real evidence, in places a fast reader will look.

For a GNC posting, the basic qualifications often read something like this: **control systems, orbital mechanics, classical dynamics, aerodynamics, sensors and actuators, modeling and simulation, and the languages C++ and Python.** You may already have real work behind several of these, from this course or from your own projects. So the question here is narrower than "do you have the skill?" It is: does the *word* for that skill, attached to something checkable, actually sit on the page? A reader cannot give you credit for knowledge she cannot see named.

## What "encoding" means

To **encode** a basic qualification means to write it on the page in the field's own words — the same words the posting uses — attached to a specific piece of your work that someone could check. Not a paraphrase you like better. Not a general description that leaves the reader to work out the connection.

Here is the difference. Say you built a simulation of a spinning spacecraft and tuned a feedback law so it holds steady. That *is* control systems work. So the phrase "control systems" should appear on the page near it. If you only describe what the code does in everyday words — "made the spacecraft stop wobbling" — the reader, scanning for "control systems", has to make the connection in her head. That extra step is exactly what a fast first pass has no time for.

This is also what a **[[keyword scan|keyword-scan]]** needs — a search, by eye or by software, for particular words. The literal word has to be there.

::: key
Encoding a basic qualification means the posting's own word for it appears on the page, attached to something specific you actually did — not implied by a description that leaves the reader to make the connection herself.
:::

### How each of the seven shows up

The seven items differ in how easily they appear in self-directed work.

- **Orbital mechanics, classical dynamics, and modeling and simulation** tend to show up naturally in a simulation-heavy project. (**Classical dynamics** is Newton's laws applied to moving and spinning bodies; **modeling and simulation** is building a computer model of a system and running it forward in time.)
- **Control systems** and **[[sensors and actuators|sensors-actuators]]** show up when a project has an estimator, a controller, or real hardware. Sensors measure what the vehicle is doing; actuators push it.
- **Aerodynamics** — how air pushes on a moving body — shows up only when flight through an atmosphere is part of what you built: an ascent, an entry, or at least an atmosphere model.
- **[[C++ and Python|cpp-python]]** are not subject areas at all. They are the two programming languages the posting names directly. You encode them by stating them plainly and truthfully, ideally showing which one did the heavy lifting in which project.

## Two places encoding happens

One mention of a keyword does less work than two mentions that back each other up in different parts of the page. The two places that matter most are the skills section and the project bullets. They do different jobs.

- A **skills section** is a **claim**. In the most scannable form on the page, it tells the reader which named areas you have worked in.
- A **project bullet** — one line under a project, describing what you did — is **evidence**. It shows one specific instance of that area, in enough detail that the reader believes the claim instead of merely noticing it.

Each one alone has a weakness.

A skills section with no bullet behind it anywhere on the page is an unchecked list. It is plausible, but the reader has nothing to weigh it against another candidate's identical list.

Strong bullets that never use the posting's words fail the other way. The evidence is real. But a reader scanning for "control systems" or "orbital mechanics" may not connect a well-written, everyday-language sentence back to the term she is checking — especially on a fast first pass.

So the strongest resumes do both, for every basic qualification with real evidence behind it. The skills line names it. At least one bullet elsewhere proves it with a concrete result. The claim tells her where to look; the evidence rewards her for looking.

::: warning A skills list with no bullet behind it is a claim, not evidence
Writing "orbital mechanics" under Domains costs nothing and proves nothing on its own. If nothing else on the page shows it — a specific project with a specific result — a careful reader either ignores the line or, worse, asks about it in an interview and finds there is little to say. List only a domain you can immediately back with one real bullet.
:::

## The skills section: grouped lists, not proficiency bars

A skills section works best as a few plain categories, each followed by a short list separated by commas. For example:

- **Languages:** the programming languages you have used.
- **Domains:** the subject areas, in the posting's words.
- **Tools:** the libraries and software you work with.

What it should not be is a single wall of words, or — worse — a set of **proficiency bars**: little graphics of filled and empty dots, or a bar with a percentage, next to each skill.

### Why the bars fail

Picture a video-game character sheet: Strength 4 of 5, Speed 3 of 5. In a game, those numbers mean something, because the game defines them. On a resume, four filled dots out of five next to "Python" is a number with no scale behind it. Filled by whom? Measured how? Against what standard? Nobody knows.

So the reader cannot weigh it the way she weighs a bullet that states a measured result, because there is nothing behind the dots to check. Worse, it invites one of the hardest interview questions to answer well: "What makes this a four and not a five?"

A grouped text list makes no such claim. It says you have worked with something, and it leaves the proving to the bullets — which is exactly where proving belongs.

There is a second, more mechanical reason, covered fully in the lesson on **[[applicant tracking systems|ats]]** later in this module. A graphic is not text. Anything shown only as a picture is invisible to software that reads a resume as a stream of characters. A plain grouped list is text all the way through, so a human reader, a keyword search, and any software between the two can all read it, with no extra effort from you.

::: key
A skills section works as a small number of grouped text lists — Languages, Domains, Tools — never as proficiency-bar graphics or percentage claims. Those assert a number nothing on the page can back up, and they are invisible to any system that reads the document as text.
:::

## Keyword-matching software, and what you can honestly plan around

Some employers' application systems run automatic keyword matching or filtering on a resume before any person opens it. Others send every application straight to a human reader with no automatic filter at all. Practice varies by employer and by system. This module cannot state it as a fixed fact about any one company's process. And for any given posting, you have no reliable way to know in advance which kind of system, if any, stands between your submission and its first human reader.

That sounds like a problem, but it sorts itself out. The same plain, specific, field-standard words that help a human confirm a basic qualification quickly are exactly what a simple keyword match needs: the literal word, present in the document as text.

So there is no separate strategy for "writing for software". Write in the field's real terms. Attach them to real evidence. Keep the document as plain text, not graphics. Then a human reader, and whatever automatic step might stand in front of her, can each find what they are looking for.

::: example Encoding "control systems", weakly and strongly
**Weak.** The summary paragraph mentions an interest in "control theory and dynamical systems" inside a sentence about career goals. No project bullet anywhere describes a controller, a gain, or a stability result.

Run the first pass. The reader scanning for "control systems" may notice the phrase. But she has nothing to weigh it against. The word appears unattached to evidence, so a careful reader gives it no credit.

**Strong.** Two changes.

1. The Domains line under Skills reads: "control systems ([[PID, LQR|pid-lqr]]), orbital mechanics, classical dynamics."
2. Under Projects, one bullet reads: "Designed and tuned a PID attitude controller for a rigid-body 3-axis simulation; achieved [[settling time|settling-time]] under 4 seconds for a 30-degree slew with steady-state pointing error held below 0.5 degrees."

Now the term appears twice, in two forms that back each other up:

- a named **claim** in Skills;
- a specific, checkable **instance** in Projects, with a controller type and measured results attached.

**Sanity check:** the numbers make sense together. A 30-degree turn that settles in under 4 seconds and holds within 0.5 degrees means the leftover pointing error is less than 2% of the size of the turn ($0.5/30 \approx 1.7\%$). That is the kind of figure an interviewer can ask about — and you can answer.
:::

::: example A grouped skills section, and what it replaces
**Before**, as proficiency bars:

"Python ●●●●○ · C++ ●●●○○ · MATLAB ●●●○○ · Control Theory ●●○○○"

That is four unexplained scores, no grouping, and nothing a reader can check for herself.

**After**, as grouped text:

- Languages: Python, C++
- Domains: control systems, orbital mechanics, classical dynamics, modeling and simulation
- Tools: [[NumPy, SciPy, CVXPY|python-tools]], [[Git|git]]

Go line by line.

- **Languages** names the two languages the posting asks for, and claims nothing more.
- **Domains** uses the posting's own words where they are accurate: four of the seven basic qualifications appear word for word.
- **Tools** groups related libraries and software.

**Sanity check:** nothing here claims a precision the candidate cannot defend. The actual proving — how well she does control systems work — is left to the project bullets a few lines further down the page.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
A candidate's summary paragraph mentions "orbital mechanics" once, as part of a sentence about her interests. No bullet anywhere on the page describes an orbits-related project. Has she encoded this basic qualification? Explain.
:::

::: answer
Not fully. Encoding means the posting's word appears *attached to real, checkable evidence*, not merely mentioned.

A single mention inside an interest statement is a claim with nothing behind it. A careful reader either ignores it or, if she asks about it in an interview, finds little concrete to discuss. The word needs a bullet somewhere that shows specific orbital-mechanics work with a result attached.
:::

::: check
Apart from the fact that parsing software cannot read a graphic, what is the core problem with a proficiency-bar skills list — the "four filled dots out of five" format?
:::

::: answer
The number has no defined scale behind it. Four out of five by what measurement, set by whom, compared with what standard? The reader has nothing to check it against — unlike a bullet reporting a measured result, which says what was done and how it was verified.

It also invites one of the hardest follow-up questions to answer credibly — "why a four and not a five?" — with no evidence on the page to answer it.
:::

::: check
A posting lists "aerodynamics" as a basic qualification. A candidate has a project with an atmospheric entry and a drag model, but her bullet only says "simulated a vehicle falling through the atmosphere", and the word aerodynamics appears nowhere. What should she change, and why?
:::

::: answer
She should rewrite the bullet — and ideally add an entry under Skills — to use the field's real term: aerodynamics, or a specific part of it such as drag modeling or atmospheric flight. "Falling through the atmosphere" is an everyday paraphrase.

The underlying work does not change. What changes is whether a fast reader, scanning for the posting's own word, can find it attached to real evidence without having to make the connection herself.
:::

::: check
Why does this lesson recommend encoding an important basic qualification in two places — the skills section and a project bullet — instead of treating one as enough?
:::

::: answer
The two places do different jobs.

- The skills section is a scannable **claim**: it tells the reader quickly which named areas you have worked in.
- A project bullet is **evidence**: one specific, checkable instance, detailed enough to be believed.

A claim with no evidence is easy to doubt. Evidence that never uses the exact keyword may slip past a fast reader's eye on the first pass. Together, the claim tells her where to look and the evidence rewards her for looking.
:::

::: check
Why can't a candidate design her resume around the behavior of one company's keyword-matching software? What should she do instead?
:::

::: answer
Practice on automatic keyword matching varies by employer and by system. For a given posting she has no reliable way to know which kind of system, if any, stands between her submission and a human reader. Designing around a guess about one system means designing around information she does not have.

Instead: plain, specific, field-standard words attached to real evidence, kept as text rather than graphics. That serves a human reader and any keyword system equally well, so no separate strategy is needed.
:::

## Summary

| Basic qualification | What it usually looks like in self-directed work | Where it gets encoded |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Control systems | A controller or estimator gain tuned against a stability or performance target | Skills: Domains line; a controller or estimator bullet |
| Orbital mechanics | Two-body propagation, orbital elements, maneuver planning | Skills: Domains line; a propagation or maneuver bullet |
| Classical dynamics | Rigid-body kinematics and dynamics, often inside a 6-DOF simulation | Skills: Domains line; a simulation bullet |
| Aerodynamics | Atmosphere modeling, drag, ascent or entry flight | Skills: Domains line; an ascent or entry bullet |
| Sensors and actuators | IMU, star tracker, reaction wheels, thrusters, real or simulated | Skills: Tools or Domains line; a sensor-fusion or actuation bullet |
| Modeling and simulation | Any verified numerical model with a stated way of checking it is correct | Skills: Domains line; nearly every project bullet |
| C++ and Python | The languages the work was written in, stated plainly and truthfully | Skills: Languages line; named per project in Projects |
| Skills format | Grouped text lists: Languages, Domains, Tools | Never proficiency bars or percentages |

The next lesson takes up the bullets themselves: the exact structure — action, method, measured result — that turns a listed skill into evidence a reader believes, worked through on real anchor projects from this course.

::: context keyword-scan Searching for a word, not reading
A **keyword** is a word or short phrase that someone is searching for. Press Ctrl+F in a document, type "orbital", and every match lights up — that is a keyword scan by software. A recruiter's eye does the same thing on paper. Either way, the search finds the exact word, or close variants of it, and nothing else. A search for "control systems" does not light up "made the spacecraft stop wobbling", however true that sentence is.
:::

::: context sensors-actuators Sense, decide, push
Every guided vehicle runs the same loop. **Sensors** measure what is happening: an **IMU** (inertial measurement unit, said "I-M-U") senses rotation and acceleration; a **star tracker** is a small camera that works out which way the spacecraft points by recognizing star patterns. A computer decides what to do. **Actuators** carry it out: **reaction wheels** spin up to turn the spacecraft the other way, and **thrusters** fire short jets of gas.

::: context cpp-python Two languages, two jobs
C++ is said "C plus plus"; the "++" is a joke from the language itself, where `++` means "add one", so it is "one better than C". Python is named after the comedy group Monty Python, not the snake. In GNC work they tend to split the labor: Python for analysis, plotting and quick experiments, and C++ for code that must run fast and predictably, such as flight software and large simulations. A posting that names both usually wants to see real work in each.
:::

::: context ats The software in front of the reader
An **applicant tracking system**, or **ATS**, is the software many companies use to collect applications. It turns your file into text, splits it into fields such as name, education and skills, and puts it in a searchable list for recruiters. If a step goes wrong — say a picture has no text in it — whatever was in that picture never reaches the list. Lesson five of this module covers how to format a file so this pipeline reads it correctly.

::: context pid-lqr Two controllers worth naming
**PID** (said "P-I-D") stands for proportional-integral-derivative. It pushes back in proportion to the current error, to the error built up over time, and to how fast the error is changing. It is by far the most common kind of controller in industry. **LQR** (said "L-Q-R"), the linear-quadratic regulator, picks its gains by minimizing a cost that trades error against effort. Naming the type of controller is a small detail that tells a GNC reader you made a specific design choice. Both come back in the control track of this course.
:::

::: context settling-time What "settles in 4 seconds" means
Command a spacecraft to turn 30 degrees. Its pointing angle rises, may overshoot a little, and wobbles down toward the target. The **settling time** is how long until it enters a narrow band around the target and stays there. The **steady-state error** is whatever gap is left after that. Both are standard measures of how well a controller performs.

::: context python-tools The Python toolbox
**NumPy** (said "num-pie") gives Python fast arrays and matrix math. **SciPy** builds on it with standard scientific tools: integrators, optimizers, interpolation and statistics. **CVXPY** lets you write a convex optimization problem in a form close to the math and hands it to a solver; it is a natural fit for guidance problems like fuel-optimal landing, which this course reaches in the guidance track. Listing tools like these tells a reader which parts of the Python world you actually work in.
:::

::: context git Saving every version
**Git** is a version-control program: it records every change to a set of files, who made it and why, so you can go back to any earlier version or combine work from several people. Almost every software team uses it, and GitHub is a website built around it. Listing Git on a resume says you can work the way an engineering team works — small, described changes, never one giant file called "final_final_v3".
:::
