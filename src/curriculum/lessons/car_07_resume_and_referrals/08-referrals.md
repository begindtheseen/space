Imagine a new kid wants to join a pickup soccer game at recess. If they walk up alone, the captains may not even notice them. If a player the captains already trust says, "She's good, I've played with her," the captains will at least watch her kick the ball. That word from a friend does not make her a better player. It gets her a fair look — and the friend's good name is now tied to how she plays.

That is a **referral**: a current employee of a company passing your application to the hiring team and putting their name on it. People often talk about referrals as a golden ticket, the one move that turns an unseen application into an offer. It is not. Believing it is leads to two bad habits this lesson exists to prevent: begging near-strangers for a favor they have no real basis to give, and, if a referral does come through, assuming it did more than it did.

So this lesson treats referrals the way the rest of the module treats everything: mechanically and honestly. You will learn exactly what you are asking for, what it is worth, who can give it, and how someone with no industry network yet can earn one.

## What a referral mechanically does

Start with who can refer. At most companies, a referral comes from a **current employee**, who submits you through the company's own [[internal referral system|referral-system]]. A friend who used to work there, or someone at a partner company, can introduce you to people — but cannot, as a rule, file the referral. So the person you ask should work there now.

A referral does two things:

1. It moves your application into a channel a specific person is likely to read, instead of leaving it as one of many look-alike applications in a general queue.
2. It attaches the referrer's own standing to your application. That is a form of **[[vouching|vouching]]** — putting your own reputation behind a claim about someone else.

Together, these mean someone has a personal reason to give your application real attention: a colleague they know put a name on it. That is a genuine, useful mechanism. And it is the *whole* mechanism. It is not magic, and it does not change a single word of what your application says.

## What a referral does not do

A referral does not waive a qualification the posting states directly. If a role requires a specific degree, a certification, or an [[export-control eligibility|export-control]] status, a referral does not make that optional. It gets your application read by someone with a reason to read carefully — and a careful read of an application that misses a stated requirement still finds it missing.

A referral also does not set your **level** (how senior the job you are offered is) or your **compensation** (what you are paid). And it does not guarantee an interview, let alone an offer. It raises the odds your application is actually looked at. That is real, but it has limits.

Here is a less comfortable point. A referral from someone who cannot speak to your work — a distant acquaintance, a friend of a friend, someone you swapped a few messages with once — carries little real weight. The referrer has nothing to vouch for except your own say-so. It can also cost you. Asking someone to stake their name on work they have never seen puts them in an awkward spot. Whether they decline or agree reluctantly, it is a poor foundation for whatever relationship you had before.

::: warning A referral is not a finish line
Treating a referral as the goal, instead of as one way to get a strong application read more carefully, leads candidates to under-invest in the resume and evidence the referral was always going to be judged against. The referral changes how promptly and carefully your application is read. It never changes what your application needs to say for itself.
:::

::: key
A referral moves your application into a queue a human reads, and attaches a colleague's reputation to it. It does not override a basic qualification, does not set level or compensation, and does not guarantee an interview — and a referral from someone who cannot speak to your work is close to worthless, however it was obtained.
:::

## Why asking a stranger rarely works, and what does instead

Picture this message, sent to someone the sender has never interacted with: "I saw you work at a company I'm interested in, could you refer me?"

Look at it from the other side. It asks the reader to take a real risk with their reputation, based on almost no information, in exchange for nothing. They cannot tell this message from the same one sent to fifty other people that week. Saying no costs them nothing. Saying yes costs a little **[[social capital|social-capital]]** — the goodwill and trust they have built up with coworkers. So it is a reasonable request to decline, and many people do. Not because they are unhelpful, but because the request gives them nothing to act on.

What works instead has one common thread: **give the other person something real to respond to**, instead of asking them to trust your word alone. There are three levels of this:

- **A real connection.** Even a small one — a shared class, a shared open-source project, a conversation that actually happened — is a foundation a cold message does not have.
- **Something they can check.** Attach one concrete project they can look at. Now they can evaluate you instead of merely trusting you.
- **Visibility before you ask.** The strongest version, which the next lesson builds in full, is to be seen doing real, checkable technical work in places that person already sees — before you ever need anything. Then, when you ask, they have their own evidence of your work, not only your word.

The two questions that matter — does this person know you, and have they seen your work? — make a [[simple grid|referrer-grid]] for judging who to ask.

## How to ask: six things a strong request contains

A referral request that is easy to say yes to is **forwardable**: the referrer could paste it straight into their company's system, or forward it to the hiring manager, without writing a word of their own. It contains six things, briefly:

1. **The connection.** How you know them, or why you are writing to them in particular. A real connection, never an invented one.
2. **The exact requisition.** The specific posting, by title and ID — not "something in GNC". A specific ask is one they can act on right away instead of having to go find.
3. **Two or three sentences of fit.** Concrete, and tied to a real, named project — not a statement of how much you love space.
4. **A link to one anchor project.** Your single strongest, most relevant one. Not your whole portfolio: asking someone to review everything you have ever built asks for far more of their time than the request should cost.
5. **Your resume, attached.** Built and formatted the way this module taught, so everything they need is in the same message.
6. **An explicit easy out.** Plain permission to say no, or not to reply at all.

Items 2 to 6 are the core. Item 1 is what gives a message to a near-stranger any footing. Put together, the whole request should cost the referrer under five minutes: read it, glance at the project, forward it.

That last item deserves a reason, not only a place on the list. Turning down a request carries a small social cost by default. It can feel like letting someone down, or like an exchange that now needs an awkward reply. An **easy out** removes that cost: it says, in so many words, that no reply is a perfectly fine outcome. And that makes a *yes* easier too, because the request no longer feels like an obligation the reader has to actively refuse. Nobody feels [[cornered|easy-out]].

::: key
Ask short, specific, and forwardable. Name the requisition, give two sentences of fit, attach the resume, link one project, and include an explicit easy out; state your real connection first. Never ask a stranger to vouch for competence they have not seen — ask them to pass on a submission. The easy out lowers the social cost of declining, and in doing so makes a genuine yes easier as well.
:::

::: example A referral request, weak and strong
**Weak:** "Hi! I saw you work at a company I'm really interested in. I'm looking for opportunities in aerospace and would love it if you could refer me sometime. Let me know!"

Check it against the six items:
- connection — not stated (if one even exists);
- requisition — none named;
- fit — nothing about the sender's actual work;
- project link — none;
- resume — none;
- easy out — none. "Let me know!" asks for a reply either way.

Score: 0 of 6. Even a reader who wanted to help has nothing to forward.

**Strong:** "Hi — we were both in the same online numerical methods study group last spring. I'm applying for the GNC Engineer, Powered Descent req (ID listed on the posting). I built a [[lossless-convexification|lossless-convexification]] descent-guidance solver and validated it against a 5,000-case [[Monte Carlo|monte-carlo]], which is linked below along with my resume. If you're able to pass this along, I'd appreciate it — and no worries at all if the timing or fit isn't right, I know these things are a real ask."

Check it the same way:
- connection — the study group, real if modest;
- requisition — named exactly;
- fit — one specific, checkable project, with a number;
- project link — "linked below";
- resume — attached;
- easy out — "no worries at all if the timing or fit isn't right".

Score: 6 of 6.

**Sanity check on length.** The strong message is 80 words, comfortably under a 150-word limit, and reads in well under a minute. Add a glance at the project and a forward, and the referrer's cost stays under five minutes. Short, and yet it carries far more than the weak one.
:::

::: example What a referral changes, and what it does not
Three candidates apply for the same requisition.

**Candidate 1** has a referral, but her resume misses a basic qualification the posting states directly. The referral works: she gets a careful read. Then she is not moved forward, because the referral could never change whether the stated requirement was met.

**Candidate 2** has no referral. Her resume plainly meets every basic qualification, backed by strong, verified project bullets. She is read in the general queue and moves forward on the strength of that evidence alone.

**Candidate 3** also plainly meets every basic qualification. She also has a referral from a colleague who has actually reviewed her open-source contribution to a project they both use. Her application is read promptly, by someone with a personal reason to pay attention, instead of waiting its turn in the general queue.

**What changed, and what did not.** Compare 2 and 3: same strong evidence, and the referral changed only *how quickly and carefully* it was read. Compare 1 and 3: both had referrals, and only the one whose evidence met the checklist went forward. The referral never changed what the resume had to say.
:::

## Building toward a referral before you need one

The strongest position to be in, when you do want to ask, is to already be a known, checkable person to the one you are asking. Not because you asked well — though asking well matters — but because there is already something real for them to point to.

That is not a trick, and it is not luck. It comes from steady, visible, genuine technical participation over time, in the same places the engineers you would ask actually show up — for example, fixing a real bug in an [[open-source project|open-source]] they use. The next lesson is about exactly that: where those places are, and what a self-taught candidate with no industry contacts can realistically do in each.

## Check yourself

::: check
Precisely what does a referral change about how an application is processed? State it as a mechanism, not a general impression.
:::

::: answer
A referral moves the application into a channel a specific person is likely to read, and attaches that person's own standing to it as a form of vouching. That makes it more likely to get real, careful attention instead of sitting as one of many look-alike submissions in a general queue. That is the entire mechanism. It does not alter the content of the application, or any requirement stated in the posting.
:::

::: check
Name two things a referral does not do, and explain why believing it does either of them can hurt a candidate.
:::

::: answer
A referral does not waive a qualification stated directly in the posting, and it does not guarantee an interview or an offer. (It also does not set level or pay.) Believing otherwise can hurt in two ways. First, she may apply to roles whose stated requirements she does not meet, expecting the referral to make up for it — and a careful read will still find the requirement unmet. Second, she may treat getting a referral as the finish line and stop investing in the resume and evidence that the referral was only ever meant to get read carefully, never to replace.
:::

::: check
Explain, mechanically, why a cold referral request to someone with no prior connection to the sender usually fails to produce a yes.
:::

::: answer
The request asks the recipient to take on a real, if modest, reputational risk — vouching for someone to their own colleagues — based on almost no information beyond the message itself. From their side, it looks the same as the identical message sent to many other people. Declining costs nothing. Agreeing spends a little real social capital for no clear return. So the request itself gives them little reason to say yes.
:::

::: check
List the six parts of a strong referral request, and explain specifically why the "easy out" is included as one of them.
:::

::: answer
A strong request (1) states the real connection to the recipient, (2) names the exact requisition, (3) gives two or three sentences of concrete fit tied to one named project, (4) links that one project, (5) attaches the resume, and (6) includes an explicit easy out. The easy out is there because turning down a request carries a default social cost: people may agree reluctantly, or avoid replying at all out of discomfort. Saying plainly that no reply is fine removes that cost — which makes a genuine, comfortable yes more likely, not less.
:::

::: check
A candidate obtains a referral from a distant acquaintance who has never seen any of her actual technical work. Is this referral easy to obtain, and is it strong evidence to the hiring team that eventually reads her application? Explain both parts.
:::

::: answer
It may well be easy to get, if the acquaintance is willing to do a small favor without much thought. But that ease is exactly why it is weak evidence. Part of a referral's value to a hiring team is the implied claim that someone with firsthand knowledge of the candidate's work is willing to put their name behind it. A referrer who has never seen the work cannot make that claim. So the referral acts more like a queue-jump than real vouching, and a careful hiring team is unlikely to weigh it as heavily as one from someone who can speak to the work directly.
:::

::: check
Why does this lesson recommend building visible technical presence "before you need it," rather than starting that effort at the moment you want to ask someone for a referral?
:::

::: answer
A referral request lands far better when the recipient already has independent, checkable evidence of the candidate's work, not only the candidate's own account of it in the same message as the ask. That independent evidence — a contribution they have seen, a project they can look at, a track record that existed before any request — cannot be made up on the spot when a referral is needed. It has to exist already, so the visible participation that produces it has to start well before any specific ask.
:::

## Summary

| What a referral does | What a referral does not do |
| --- | --- |
| Moves the application into a channel a specific person reads | Waive a qualification stated directly in the posting |
| Attaches the referrer's own standing to the submission | Set level or compensation |
| Increases the odds the application receives real attention | Guarantee an interview or an offer |
| Works best from someone who has seen real evidence of your work | Carry much weight from someone who has never seen it |
| Comes from a current employee, asked with a short, forwardable, six-part request | Justify asking a stranger to vouch for work they have not seen |

The next lesson covers where that evidence and those relationships actually get built: the conferences, communities and open-source projects where GNC engineers really are, and what a self-taught candidate with no industry contacts can realistically do in each one.

::: context referral-system How a referral gets filed
Most larger companies give employees an internal tool, often built into the same applicant tracking system that holds cold applications. The employee enters your name and email, picks the requisition, attaches your resume, and adds a line or two about how they know you. Your application then carries a "referred by" tag the recruiter can see. Many companies also pay employees a **referral bonus** if someone they referred is hired and stays a while — which is one reason people are often willing to refer, and also a reason recruiters read a referral's "how do you know them" line closely.
:::

::: context vouching An old word for a promise
To **vouch** for someone is to say, "I will answer for this person." The word comes through Old French from the Latin *vocare*, "to call" — originally, calling someone to court to back up a claim. It is the same root as "voice" and "vocal". The key idea has survived: a voucher is not merely saying something nice. They are putting something of their own on the line. That is why vouching is only worth anything when the voucher actually knows what they are vouching for.
:::

::: context export-control A requirement no referral can move
On U.S. launch vehicles, one stated requirement that often appears in the posting is export-control eligibility. Under the export rules called **ITAR** (said "eye-tar"), SpaceX postings have required the applicant to be a U.S. citizen or national, a lawful permanent resident (**green card** holder), a refugee or an asylee — or to be eligible for the needed government authorization. This is a legal rule, not a company preference, so no employee's recommendation can change it. The first career module, on the ITAR gate, covers it in full.
:::

::: context social-capital Trust as a kind of savings account
**Social capital** is a name for the trust and goodwill a person has built up with the people around them. Think of it as a savings account. Every time a coworker delivers good work, keeps a promise or gives a fair recommendation, the balance goes up. Every time they vouch for someone who turns out badly — or pass along a flood of weak referrals — it goes down. When you ask for a referral, you are asking someone to make a small withdrawal on your behalf. Asking well means making sure it is a withdrawal they will be glad they made.
:::

An easy out takes the rudeness out of no. That leaves the reader free to decide on the merits — and a request with a named req, a real project and an attached resume usually has merits. ::: context lossless-convexification The descent-guidance trick in the example
A rocket landing on its engine has to solve an optimization problem fast: which thrust, pointed which way, gets it to the pad using the least fuel? The catch is a rule like "the engine cannot throttle below some minimum", which makes the problem **non-convex** — hard to solve reliably. **Lossless convexification**, developed by Behçet Açıkmeşe and colleagues, rewrites the problem into a **convex** one that a computer can solve quickly and reliably, and proves the answer is still right for the original problem — nothing is "lost". It comes back in the guidance part of this course.
:::

::: context monte-carlo Five thousand runs with the dice rolled
A **Monte Carlo** test runs the same simulation many times, each time with the uncertain inputs — wind, engine thrust, sensor noise, starting position — drawn at random within realistic limits. Then you look at the whole spread of results, such as how often the vehicle lands within a target distance. The name comes from the famous casino in Monaco, because the method runs on chance. Saying "validated against a 5,000-case Monte Carlo" tells an engineer you tested your solver against 5,000 randomized situations, not one hand-picked case.
:::

::: context open-source The best introduction there is
**Open-source** software is code whose source is public, so anyone can read it and suggest changes. Astrodynamics and estimation have real open-source projects, run by engineers who review every proposed change — called a **pull request** — before accepting it. When you submit a genuine fix and a maintainer reviews and merges it, that maintainer has now *seen your work*, firsthand, and can judge it. That moves them into the top row of the referrer grid. The next lesson covers how to do this well.
:::
