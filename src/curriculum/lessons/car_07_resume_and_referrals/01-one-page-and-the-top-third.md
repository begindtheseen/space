Picture a coach at team tryouts with a clipboard. On the clipboard is a short list: can this player dribble, pass, and run the length of the field. Sixty kids come through. The coach does not get to know each one. She watches for a few seconds, ticks the boxes she can see, and moves on. A kid who can dribble beautifully, but only does it at the far end of the field where she is not looking, gets no tick.

A **[[resume|resume-word]]** — a one-page summary of what you have done, sent with a job application — is read the same way. The first reader is often a **[[recruiter|recruiter]]**, a person whose job is to find and screen candidates, and who is usually not an engineer. She has the job posting's list of must-haves in her head, and she checks your page against it fast. Only if that first pass finds what it is looking for does the page get a second, slower read.

You may be writing this resume with no GNC job title to lean on and no degree in the field. That is a real constraint. This module treats it as a writing problem you can solve, not a reason to apologize on the page. The goal is to make a technical reader confident, from the page alone, that you can do guidance, navigation and control work. Confidence gets built in an order: shape first, then content. This lesson is the shape. It fixes three things the rest of the module writes into: one page, newest things first, and your strongest evidence placed where a fast reader looks.

## How long the first look lasts

Start with the reader, because everything else follows from her.

The **first pass** is the quick scan that decides whether your resume gets read properly. It takes **well under a minute**. In that time the reader is not asking "is this a brilliant engineer?" She is asking a narrower, more mechanical question: for each item on the posting's list, is there something on this page, in words I recognize, that plausibly satisfies it?

That list is the posting's **[[basic qualifications|basic-quals]]** — the short, named set of must-haves, such as control systems, orbital mechanics and classical dynamics. It is not a vague wish. It is a checklist, and the first pass is a check against it.

So a brilliant bullet about a verified 6-DOF simulation that sits at the bottom of a second page, under an unrelated retail job, might as well not exist. The reader never got there.

::: key
A recruiter's first pass takes well under a minute, and it checks the resume against the basic-qualification list. So every basic qualification has to be visible and verifiable near the top of the page — not buried in the last bullet of a project on page three.
:::

Treat that first pass as a design target, not a mystery about how one person reads. Individual readers differ in habits and speed, and you cannot design around any one person's quirks. What does not change is the posting's own list. That list is the fixed target every version of your resume is checked against. The goal set for this module is a page a non-specialist can check against the basic qualifications in about 30 seconds.

There is a simple test, and the exercises in this module ask you to run it. Hand the page to a friend who knows nothing about GNC. Put the posting's basic-qualifications list beside it. Watch how long it takes them to find evidence for each line, and where they get stuck. If they cannot find your evidence for "control systems" without you pointing at it, the page is not done yet — however good the project behind it really is.

## Why one page

Think of packing for a trip with one small backpack. You cannot take everything. So you lay it all out and choose: what do I really need? The small bag forces the choice, and you end up with a better-packed bag than someone who threw everything into a suitcase.

One page works the same way. It is not a formality or an old tradition. It is a **[[forcing function|forcing-function]]** — a limit that makes you do something useful you would otherwise skip. If everything has to fit on one page, you must decide, item by item, what earns the space. That decision is the exact skill this module teaches: knowing which piece of evidence best proves you can do the work, and cutting the rest.

A two-page resume from an early-career engineer is usually not evidence of more accomplishment. It usually means the choosing never happened. Then the reader has to do your sorting for you — at the exact moment you need her doing something else, which is checking whether you clear the basic qualifications.

There is a real exception, and it is worth naming so you do not misapply the rule. A senior engineer with a long list of published papers, several programs that made it to flight, or a decade of jobs may honestly need a second page. The amount of real, distinct evidence has outgrown one page. That is not the position of a self-taught candidate without an industry GNC role yet. For you, one page is not a limit to fight. It produces a better resume than an unlimited one would, because it makes you lead with your strongest three or four pieces of evidence instead of listing everything you ever touched.

::: key
Resume length for an early-career engineer: one page. A second page signals an inability to prioritize, unless you are senior with a long publication or program record. The limit is a feature: it forces you to choose your strongest evidence.
:::

## Reverse chronological, not grouped by skill

**Reverse chronological** means newest first. (*Chronological* means "in time order", from the Greek word *chronos*, time; *reverse* flips it.) Your most recent project comes before an older one. Your current status comes before an earlier one. It is how a news feed is ordered, and it is the order a reader expects.

There is another resume shape you should rule out on purpose rather than by accident. A **[[functional resume|functional-resume]]** groups everything under skill headings — "Simulation Experience", "Programming Experience" — instead of listing dated entries. It exists mainly to help someone with gaps in their work history avoid drawing attention to their timeline. It is a familiar idea, and it is the wrong tool here, for two separate reasons.

**First, it makes the reader do unfamiliar work.** A reader who has looked at hundreds of resumes expects dated entries, newest first, under a few standard sections. A functional layout breaks that expectation. That does not read as creative. It reads as unfamiliar, and an unfamiliar shape is slower to scan — the opposite of what you want from a page whose first job is to be scanned fast.

**Second, it solves a problem you do not have.** A functional resume is built for hiding a timeline. Hiding is not your problem. Your problem is that your strongest evidence is a project, not a job, and it needs to be the first thing a reader sees. You fix that by choosing *where each section sits on the page* — the next part of this lesson — not by giving up the dated shape readers already know how to read quickly.

Notice what "reverse chronological" does and does not decide. It sets the order of entries *inside* each section. It does not say which sections appear, or in what order they run down the page. That second question — what belongs in the top third — is the one that does the real work for someone in your position.

## The top third

Picture the page cut into three horizontal bands. The **[[top third|top-third]]** is the top band: the part a reader's eyes land on first, before she decides whether to keep going. Everything below it gets read only if the top third has already earned enough confidence. That makes it the most valuable space on the page. So it is worth saying exactly what goes there.

### The header block

The **header block** is the few lines at the very top with your name and how to reach you. Keep it small on purpose:

- your name;
- one email address;
- one phone number, if you want a call to be possible;
- a city and state or region, not a full street address;
- a small number of links: a **[[GitHub|github]]** profile or other code-hosting page, and, if you have one, a single portfolio or project site.

Five icons for five different platforms do not add credibility. They add clutter the reader has to sort through to find the one link that matters.

### The identity line

Right below the header comes one line that says who you are in terms a GNC reader recognizes. This is the **identity line**. It is not a mission statement about your passion for space. It is a factual sentence anchored to real evidence: the kind of work you have built, and the kind of role you want next.

This line does real work. Before the reader sees anything else, it tells her which lens to read the rest of the page through.

### The strongest section

Then your strongest section begins — immediately. Not after a paragraph of preamble. Not after a section that says less about your ability than the one behind it.

For the reader this module is written for, that almost always means a **Projects** section, with one short line of education right above it, starting right there in the top third. If you have an Experience section listing unrelated jobs, it still belongs on the page, but lower down, because it carries less of the specific evidence a GNC reader is checking for. Later lessons build what a strong Projects section and identity line contain. This lesson's job is only to fix where they sit.

::: key
The top third is the header block, a one-line identity statement, and the start of your strongest section. For a candidate without an industry GNC role, that strongest section is Projects, and it begins right away — not after unrelated jobs, and not after a paragraph explaining your journey.
:::

::: warning A generic objective statement is not an identity line
"Seeking a challenging position where I can grow my skills and contribute to a dynamic team" describes almost any candidate for almost any job — which means it describes none of them. It sits on the most valuable line of the page and gives the reader nothing she can check. Replace it with one factual sentence naming your field and your strongest evidence, or cut it and let the header and your first project speak first.
:::

::: example Same facts, reordered
A candidate has one real **anchor project** — a project strong enough to build the whole resume around: a verified [[6-DOF|six-dof]] launch-vehicle simulation with a documented [[dispersion campaign|dispersion]]. She also has eighteen months in an unrelated retail job.

**Her draft, top to bottom:**

1. Header.
2. A paragraph headed "Objective" about her enthusiasm for aerospace.
3. Skills: a list of tools with no context.
4. Education.
5. Experience: the retail job, described in detail.
6. At the bottom of a second page, under a heading called "Other", three thin lines about the simulation.

Now run the first pass. The reader looks for control systems, orbital mechanics, dynamics, modeling and simulation. The top third holds an enthusiasm paragraph and a bare tool list. The evidence is on page two. She finds nothing to tick.

**The revision changes no facts — only order and placement:**

1. The header stays.
2. The objective paragraph becomes one identity line: "Built and verified a 6-DOF launch-vehicle simulation with a Monte Carlo dispersion campaign; seeking a guidance, navigation and control role."
3. Education shrinks to a single line.
4. Projects, led by the simulation with its strongest bullets, starts right after that — inside the top third.
5. Skills moves below Projects as a short, grouped list.
6. Experience, with the retail job in one honest line, moves to the bottom of what is now a single page.

**Sanity check:** did anything get hidden? No. The retail job is still there and still true. It has moved to where its weight for a GNC posting belongs — below the project that does the real work of the page. And the same reader, running the same first pass, now meets the simulation in the first few lines.
:::

::: example One identity line, weak and strong
**Weak:** "Aspiring aerospace engineer seeking an entry-level opportunity to apply my skills and grow professionally."

Test it: could this sentence sit, unchanged, at the top of thousands of other resumes? Yes. So the reader learns nothing from it that she did not already know from the fact that someone applied.

**Strong:** "Self-taught GNC engineer; built and verified a 6-DOF launch-vehicle simulation and a consistency-checked attitude estimator; seeking a guidance, navigation and control role."

Run the same test. This sentence could not sit on thousands of other resumes, because it names two specific things. In one line the reader now knows:

- the field (GNC);
- two concrete, checkable claims to look for as she reads on (the simulation and the estimator);
- which kind of role to read the rest of the page against.

**Sanity check:** the strong line is not more enthusiastic than the weak one. It is more *specific* — and specificity is what a scanning reader can actually use.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
One candidate has four years of relevant industry experience and two conference papers. Another has no industry GNC role and one strong project. Both are thinking about a two-page resume. Why is that reasonable for one and not the other?
:::

::: answer
One page is a forcing function: it makes a candidate choose her strongest evidence instead of listing everything.

The senior candidate has enough distinct, real evidence — a longer work record and published papers — that fitting it honestly may truly need a second page. The extra length reflects real accomplishment, not a skipped choosing step.

The candidate with no industry role and one project has much less material. A second page for her would not mean more evidence. It would mean padding, or a failure to choose — at the exact moment the reader needs the page to be quick to check.
:::

::: check
A candidate has organized her resume under headings like "Simulation Experience" and "Programming Experience" instead of dated entries, because she thinks this shows off her skills more directly. Give two separate reasons this works against her.
:::

::: answer
**First:** this functional layout breaks the reader's expectation of how a resume is organized. An unfamiliar shape is slower to scan, which is the opposite of what a page meant to be checked quickly should do.

**Second:** functional layouts exist mainly to hide an employment timeline. That is not her problem. Her problem is getting her strongest evidence — a project — to appear where the reader looks first. She solves that by placing sections well inside a normal reverse-chronological resume, not by abandoning the shape readers already know how to read fast.
:::

::: check
Define "the top third" exactly. Why is it not the same thing as "whatever section happens to come first on the page"?
:::

::: answer
The top third is the header block, the one-line identity statement, and the beginning of the single strongest section. It is the band of the page the reader's attention lands on before she decides whether to keep reading.

"Whatever comes first" treats section order as an accident of habit or template. The top third is a deliberate choice: you decide which section carries the most evidence for *this* reader, and you put that section there on purpose.
:::

::: check
A candidate's header lists a full home address, a personal blog, a photography portfolio, a Twitter handle, an Instagram handle and a GitHub profile. What is wrong with it, and what should replace it?
:::

::: answer
The header spends the most valuable space on the page on links a GNC reader has no reason to open. They hide the one link that matters — the GitHub profile, where the checkable technical work lives. A full street address adds nothing that a city and region do not already say.

Replace it with: name, one email, one phone number if wanted, city and region, and a small number of links that bear on GNC work — usually a code-hosting profile and, if it exists, one portfolio or project site.
:::

::: check
A reader has the posting's basic-qualifications list and your resume. Roughly how long does her first pass last, and what exactly is she doing in that time? What does that mean for where your evidence goes?
:::

::: answer
Well under a minute. She is not forming an overall opinion of your talent. She is going down the basic-qualifications list and, for each item, looking for something on the page — in words she recognizes — that plausibly satisfies it.

That means every basic qualification needs visible, checkable evidence near the top of the page. Evidence buried at the bottom, or on a second page, may never be reached in that first pass, and a qualification she cannot find is one she cannot tick.
:::

## Summary

| Element | Where it goes | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First pass | Well under a minute, against the basic-qualification list | Every basic qualification must be visible and checkable near the top |
| Page length | One page (a second only for a long, senior record) | Forces a choice of strongest evidence instead of listing everything |
| Order inside sections | Reverse chronological: newest first | Matches what readers expect, so the page scans fast |
| Header block | Top of the page, small | Name, one email, one phone, city and region, a few relevant links |
| Identity line | Right below the header | One factual sentence: field, strongest evidence, target role |
| Strongest section | Starts right after the identity line | For this reader, Projects — the top third is where it counts most |

The next lesson stays in the top third but turns to content: how to make sure the exact words a posting's basic qualifications use — control systems, orbital mechanics, classical dynamics, aerodynamics, sensors and actuators, modeling and simulation, C++ and Python — actually appear on the page where a fast reader will recognize them, and how the skills section carries its share of that work without a proficiency-bar graphic.

::: context resume-word A French word for "summary"
*Résumé* is French for "summary", from a verb meaning "to sum up". In the United States it names the short, targeted document you send with a job application, and it is usually written without the accent marks. Much of the rest of the world says **CV** instead, short for the Latin *curriculum vitae*, "the course of a life". In American usage a CV is a longer, complete record — common in academia, where it lists every paper and talk. For an engineering job application in the US, you want the short one.
:::

::: context recruiter The person with the clipboard
A **recruiter** is the person at a company whose job is to find, screen and move candidates through hiring. Recruiters usually are not engineers, and each one handles many openings at once. That shapes how they read: not "is this person good at Kalman filters?" but "can I find each must-have from this posting on this page?" Some recruiters specialize in technical roles and learn the vocabulary well. You should still write so that someone who does not know GNC can tick your boxes without help.
:::

::: context basic-quals Must-haves and nice-to-haves
Most engineering postings split their requirements in two. **Basic qualifications** are the must-haves: a degree type, years of experience, named subject areas, programming languages. Missing one usually ends the application. **Preferred qualifications** are the nice-to-haves that make a candidate stand out. The first pass is a check against the basic list, which is why this module builds the whole page around making each basic item easy to find.

::: context forcing-function A limit that makes you do the right thing
**Forcing function** is a design term for a constraint that makes a good action happen whether or not anyone remembers to do it. A car that will not shift out of park unless your foot is on the brake is one. The one-page limit does the same thing for a resume: you cannot finish the page without deciding what matters most. Engineers meet the same idea all the time — a mass budget on a spacecraft forces the team to argue about which instrument is really worth its kilograms.
:::

::: context functional-resume Two shapes side by side
A **chronological** resume lists dated entries, newest first, under a few standard headings. A **functional** resume groups claims under skill headings and pushes dates to the side or drops them. The functional shape hides a timeline. It also breaks the dated pattern readers scan for, and it separates each claim from the project that proves it.

::: context github Where your code lives in public
**GitHub** is a website that stores code **repositories** — a project's files together with the full history of every change. Engineers use it to share work and review each other's code. For a candidate without an industry job, a public repository is the strongest proof on the page, because a reader can open it and see the code, the tests and the commit history for herself. That is why it earns a place in a small header when a photography site does not.
:::

::: context six-dof Six ways a vehicle moves
A rigid vehicle can move in six independent ways: slide forward and back, left and right, up and down (three **translations**), and turn in pitch, yaw and roll (three **rotations**). Each is a **degree of freedom**, so a **6-DOF simulation** tracks all six at once. That matters because for a real rocket they affect each other: tilting the vehicle changes where the thrust pushes it. You build one of these in the simulation track of this course, and it becomes a natural anchor project.
:::

::: context dispersion Running the flight thousands of times
A single simulated flight uses one set of exact numbers. Real flights never match them: engines vary a little, winds change, sensors have small errors. A **dispersion campaign** — also called a **Monte Carlo** analysis, after the famous casino in Monaco, because it runs on random numbers — flies the simulation thousands of times, each with slightly different random errors drawn from realistic ranges. The result is a spread of outcomes, such as a cloud of landing points, that tells you how far off things could plausibly land. Teams use that spread to decide whether a design has enough margin.
:::
