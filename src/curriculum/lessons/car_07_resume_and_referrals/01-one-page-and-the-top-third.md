You are writing a resume with no GNC job title to lean on and no degree in the field to shortcut the conversation. That is a real constraint, and this module treats it as a solvable writing problem rather than a reason to apologize on the page. The whole module is about making a technical reader confident, from the document alone, that you can do guidance, navigation and control work — and confidence is built in a specific order: shape first, then content. This lesson is the shape.

A resume is not read the way an essay is read. It is scanned once, fast, against a checklist the reader already has in her head — the basic qualifications named in the posting — and only kept for a second, slower look if that first pass finds what it is looking for. Nothing you write later in this module matters if the page is organized so that evidence never reaches that first pass. A brilliant bullet about a verified 6-DOF simulation, sitting at the bottom of a second page under an unrelated retail job, might as well not exist. This lesson fixes the shape the rest of the module writes into: one page, ordered newest to oldest, with the strongest evidence positioned where a fast reader actually looks.

## Why one page

One page is not a formality and not an arbitrary tradition. It is a forcing function. If everything you want a reader to know about you has to fit in one page, you cannot include everything — you have to decide, item by item, what earns the space. That decision is exactly the skill this module is teaching: knowing which piece of evidence best proves you can do the work, and cutting the rest. A two-page resume from an early-career candidate usually is not evidence of more accomplishment; it is usually evidence that the prioritization step never happened, and the reader is left doing your triage for you, at the exact moment you most need her doing something else — checking whether you clear the basic qualifications.

There is a real exception, and it is worth naming so you do not misapply the rule: a senior engineer with a long publication record, multiple shipped programs, or a decade of employment history may reasonably need a second page, because the volume of legitimate, distinct evidence has grown past what one page can honestly hold. That is not the position of a self-taught candidate with no industry GNC role yet. For this reader, one page is not a limitation fought against — it is the constraint that produces a better resume than an unconstrained one would, because it forces you to lead with your strongest three or four pieces of evidence instead of listing everything you have ever touched.

::: key
One page is a forcing function, not a formality: it makes you choose your strongest evidence instead of listing everything, and for a candidate without an industry GNC role or a long record, a second page usually signals that the choosing never happened.
:::

## Reverse chronological, not grouped by skill

There is another resume shape worth naming so you can rule it out deliberately rather than by accident: the functional or skills-grouped resume, which organizes content under headings like "Technical Skills" or "Simulation Experience" rather than under dated entries. It exists mainly to help a candidate with employment gaps or an unrelated work history avoid drawing attention to a timeline. It is a familiar idea, and it is the wrong tool here for two separate reasons.

First, a functional resume asks a reader to do unfamiliar work. A reader who has looked at hundreds of resumes has an expectation for how one is organized — a stream of dated entries, newest first, inside a small number of standard sections. A functional layout breaks that expectation, and breaking it does not read as creative; it reads as unfamiliar, and unfamiliar shapes are slower to scan, which is the opposite of what you want from a document whose first job is to be scanned fast. Second, and more specific to this reader: a functional resume solves the problem of hiding a timeline, and hiding is not your problem. Your problem is that the strongest evidence you have — a project, not a job — needs to be the first thing a reader sees. That is solved by where a section sits on the page, which the next part of this lesson covers, not by abandoning the dated, chronological shape a reader already knows how to read quickly.

Reverse chronological means each section, internally, lists its own entries newest first — your most recent project before an older one, your current status before an earlier one. It does not by itself say which sections appear, or in what order they appear down the page. That second question — what belongs in the top third — is the one that actually does the work for a candidate in your position.

## The top third

Picture the page in three horizontal bands. The top third is the header block, a one-line identity statement, and the beginning of whichever section carries your strongest evidence. Everything below that band gets read only if the top third has already done its job — earned enough confidence that the reader keeps going. That makes the top third the highest-value real estate on the page, and it is worth specifying exactly what belongs there.

The header block is small on purpose: your name, one email address, one phone number if you want a call to be possible, a city and region rather than a full street address, and a small number of links — a GitHub or code-hosting profile and, if you have one, a single portfolio or project site. Five icons for five different platforms do not add credibility; they add clutter a reader has to sort through to find the one link that actually matters.

Immediately below the header, a single line states who you are in terms a GNC reader recognizes — not a mission statement about your passion for space, but a factual identity anchored to real evidence: the kind of work you have built and the kind of role you want next. This line is doing real work: it tells the reader, before she reads anything else, what lens to read the rest of the page through.

Then the strongest section begins — immediately, not after a paragraph of preamble and not after a section that says less about your capability than the one behind it. For the reader this module is written for, that almost always means a Projects section, paired with one brief line of education context, starts right there in the top third. An Experience section listing unrelated jobs, if you have one, still belongs on the page — but lower, because it carries less of the specific evidence a GNC reader is checking for. Later lessons in this module build exactly what a strong Projects section and a strong one-line identity statement contain; this lesson's job is only to fix where they sit.

::: key
The top third is the header block, a one-line identity statement, and the start of your strongest section. For a candidate without an industry GNC role, that strongest section is Projects, and it begins immediately — not after unrelated employment, not after a paragraph explaining your journey.
:::

::: warning A generic objective statement is not a substitute for an identity line
"Seeking a challenging position where I can grow my skills and contribute to a dynamic team" describes almost any candidate for almost any job, which means it describes none of them. It occupies the single most valuable line on the page and returns nothing a reader can check. Replace it with one factual sentence naming your domain and your strongest evidence, or cut it entirely and let the header and the first project speak first.
:::

## What a first pass is actually checking

A posting's basic qualifications are not vague expectations — they are a short, specific, named list: control systems, orbital mechanics, classical dynamics, and so on. A reader's first pass through your resume is, mechanically, a check of that list against your page: for each named item, is there something here, in language she recognizes, that plausibly satisfies it. That is a narrower and more mechanical task than "is this an impressive engineer," and it happens before the more interesting question is even reachable. Treat that first pass as a design target for the page, not as a mystery about how any particular person reads: build the top third so that a reader unfamiliar with your specific projects can locate evidence for each named qualification quickly, without hunting.

This is the same test the exercises in this module ask you to run on your own draft directly: hand the page to someone who does not know GNC, hand them the posting's basic-qualifications list beside it, and watch how long it takes them to confirm each line and whether they get stuck anywhere. Individual readers vary in habit and pace, and you cannot design around any one person's idiosyncrasies — what does not vary is the posting's own list, which is the fixed target every version of your resume is checked against. Design toward that list. If a friend running this test cannot find your evidence for "control systems" without you pointing at it, the page has not done its job yet, regardless of how good the underlying project actually is.

::: example A page before and after reordering, same underlying content
A candidate has one real anchor project — a verified 6-DOF launch-vehicle simulation with a documented dispersion campaign — and eighteen months in an unrelated retail job. Her current draft opens with a header, then a paragraph headed "Objective" describing her enthusiasm for aerospace, then a Skills section listing tools without context, then Education, then Experience with the retail job described in detail, and finally, at the bottom of a second page, three sparse lines about the simulation project under a heading called "Other."

Nothing about the underlying facts changes in the revision — only order and placement. The header stays. The objective paragraph is replaced by one identity line: built and verified a 6-DOF launch-vehicle simulation with a Monte Carlo dispersion campaign; seeking a guidance, navigation and control role. Education becomes a single line. The Projects section, led by the simulation project with its strongest bullets, starts immediately after that line — in the top third. Skills moves below Projects as a compact, categorized list. Experience, including the retail job described in one honest line, moves to the bottom of the now-single page. The retail job has not been hidden or misrepresented; it has been placed where its evidentiary weight for a GNC posting actually belongs, well below the project that does the real work of the page.
:::

::: example One identity line, weak and strong
Weak: "Aspiring aerospace engineer seeking an entry-level opportunity to apply my skills and grow professionally." This sentence contains no information specific to the candidate — it could sit at the top of thousands of other resumes unchanged, and a reader gains nothing from reading it that she did not already know from the fact that a resume was submitted at all.

Strong: "Self-taught GNC engineer; built and verified a 6-DOF launch-vehicle simulation and a consistency-checked attitude estimator; seeking a guidance, navigation and control role." In one line, a reader now knows the domain, has two concrete, checkable claims to look for as she reads on, and knows what role family to read the rest of the page against. The second version is not more enthusiastic than the first — it is more specific, and specificity is what a scanning reader can actually use.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
A candidate with four years of relevant industry experience and two conference papers is considering a two-page resume. A candidate with no industry GNC role and one strong project is considering the same. Explain why the same choice is reasonable for one and not the other.
:::

::: answer
The one-page rule is a forcing function that makes a candidate choose her strongest evidence rather than list everything. The senior candidate has enough distinct, legitimate evidence — a longer employment record, published work — that fitting it honestly may genuinely require a second page; the volume reflects real accomplishment, not a skipped prioritization step. The candidate with no industry role and one project has far less material, so a second page for her would not reflect more evidence — it would reflect padding, or a failure to choose, at exactly the moment the reader needs the page to be easy to check fast.
:::

::: check
A candidate has organized her resume under headings like "Simulation Experience" and "Programming Experience" rather than as dated entries, believing this highlights her skills more directly. Give two separate reasons this works against her.
:::

::: answer
First, this functional layout breaks a reader's expectation for how a resume is organized, and an unfamiliar shape is slower to scan — the opposite of what a document meant to be checked quickly should do. Second, functional layouts exist mainly to obscure an employment timeline, which is not her actual problem; her problem is making her strongest evidence, a project, appear where a reader looks first. That is solved by section placement within a normal reverse-chronological resume, not by abandoning the chronological shape readers already know how to read.
:::

::: check
Define "the top third" precisely. Why is it not the same thing as "whatever section happens to be first on the page"?
:::

::: answer
The top third is the header block, a one-line identity statement, and the beginning of the single strongest section — the specific band of the page a reader's attention lands on before she decides whether to keep reading. It is not just "whatever comes first" because that framing treats section order as arbitrary; the top third is a deliberate claim about which section carries the most evidentiary weight for this reader, specifically, and puts that section there on purpose, rather than by habit or by the order sections are conventionally listed elsewhere.
:::

::: check
A candidate's header lists a full home address, a personal blog, a photography portfolio, a Twitter handle, an Instagram handle, and a GitHub profile. What is wrong with this header, and what should replace it?
:::

::: answer
The header is spending the highest-value space on the page on links a GNC reader has no reason to open, which delays or obscures the one link that matters — the GitHub profile, where verifiable technical work actually lives. A full street address adds nothing a city and region does not already convey for this purpose. The header should hold name, one email, one phone number if wanted, city and region, and a small number of links directly relevant to GNC work — typically a code-hosting profile and, if it exists, one portfolio or project site.
:::

::: check
A candidate wants to keep the sentence "Seeking a challenging position where I can grow my skills and contribute to a dynamic team" as her identity line, arguing it shows enthusiasm. What should she do instead, and why?
:::

::: answer
She should replace it with one factual sentence naming her domain and her strongest concrete evidence — for example, stating that she built and verified a specific project and is seeking a specific role family. The original sentence is generic enough to apply to nearly any candidate for nearly any job, so a reader gains no information from it; a scanning reader needs specificity she can check against the rest of the page, not a statement of enthusiasm that every other applicant's resume could also carry unchanged.
:::

## Summary

| Element | Where it goes | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Page length | One page (second page only for a long, senior record) | Forces a choice of strongest evidence instead of listing everything |
| Section order | Reverse chronological within each section | Matches a reader's existing expectation, so the page scans fast |
| Header block | Top of the page, small | Name, one email, one phone, city/region, a few relevant links |
| Identity line | Directly below the header | One factual sentence: domain, strongest evidence, target role |
| Strongest section | Starts immediately after the identity line | For this reader, that is Projects — the top third is where it counts most |

The next lesson stays in the top third but moves to content: how to make sure the exact vocabulary a posting's basic qualifications use — control systems, orbital mechanics, classical dynamics, aerodynamics, sensors and actuators, modeling and simulation, C++ and Python — actually appears on the page in a form a fast reader recognizes, and how the skills section carries its share of that work without leaning on a proficiency-bar graphic.
