This module has covered building the document, submitting it, and earning the kind of visible presence that makes a referral possible over time. This lesson closes it with two more immediate tools available to someone actively searching right now: reaching out directly to people on LinkedIn rather than only waiting to be found, and knowing what to actually do when a first attempt at a specific company or team does not lead anywhere. Both are about initiative — but initiative done carelessly costs more than it earns, in both cases, which is exactly why each deserves a precise rather than a general treatment.

## LinkedIn outreach done well

A message that gets read carefully and responded to genuinely does two specific things: it references something real and public about the recipient's actual work — a paper, a talk, a specific project, a specific post they wrote — rather than opening with a generic, templated connection request that could be sent to anyone in the field. And it asks one concrete, answerable question, rather than an open-ended invitation like "would you be open to a chat sometime." A specific question is easy to answer in two minutes from a phone; an open-ended one asks the recipient to do the work of figuring out what you actually want and to find time for something with no defined shape.

It is worth being explicit about the last piece: do not ask for a referral or anything else on first contact. Let a real, even brief, exchange happen first. A message that leads with an ask before any actual interaction has occurred reads as transactional from the very first line, and it puts the recipient in the position of deciding whether to help a stranger before they have any real information about whether helping is warranted.

::: key
A strong LinkedIn message references something specific and real about the recipient's own work, asks one concrete question, and makes no ask on first contact. The specificity itself is the signal — it demonstrates the sender actually looked at what the recipient does, rather than sending the same message to many people at once.
:::

## LinkedIn outreach done badly

The failure pattern is the mirror image: the same templated note sent to dozens of engineers at a target company, an ask for a referral or a job front-loaded before any real exchange has taken place, and repeated follow-up messages when the first one goes unanswered. Each piece compounds the others. A templated message is recognizable as templated the moment its content could apply to any recipient in the same role, and a request that arrives with no prior interaction attached to it reads as a transaction rather than a conversation. Repeated follow-ups on an unanswered templated message do not read as persistence — they read as pressure, and they make declining, or continuing to ignore, easier to justify rather than harder.

The underlying mechanism is not mysterious and does not depend on any statistic about response rates: a message that clearly cost the sender nothing to write — because it was not written for this specific recipient at all — tends to be met with the same minimal effort it was sent with, if it is met with any response at all. A message that visibly cost the sender real attention to research and write tends to be read as what it is: an attempt at an actual, specific conversation.

::: warning Volume and pressure work against you, not for you
Sending the same message to many people, and following up repeatedly when ignored, does not increase your odds by increasing your reach — it makes each individual message easier to recognize as generic, and repeated unsolicited follow-up reads as pressure rather than genuine interest.
:::

::: example A LinkedIn message, generic and specific, to the same recipient
Generic: "Hi! I'd love to connect and learn more about your experience at your company. Also, if there are any GNC openings, I'd really appreciate a referral!" This message could be sent to any engineer at any company with the name changed, asks for a favor before any exchange has occurred, and gives the recipient nothing specific to respond to beyond a vague offer to "learn more."

Specific: "Hi — I read your team's recent conference paper on attitude-estimator consistency testing. I ran a similar NEES/NIS analysis on a quaternion EKF I built and found a very similar overconfidence issue traced to an under-tuned process-noise term. Curious whether that's a pattern you've seen show up often in practice, or whether it was more specific to your particular sensor setup." This message references a real, specific piece of the recipient's actual work, connects it to a real, specific piece of the sender's own work, and asks one focused, answerable question — with no ask attached at all.
:::

## Recruiters reaching out, and replying to them

LinkedIn is also a channel where a company's own recruiters sometimes reach out directly, or can be reached, about a specific opening. A recruiter's message deserves a prompt, substantive reply — not necessarily an instant one, but not one left for weeks either. The reasoning here is a matter of process, not urgency for its own sake: a recruiter is typically managing multiple open requisitions and multiple candidates at once, and a clear, timely answer keeps you actively under consideration for a specific requisition rather than falling out of an unprioritized queue because a reply took too long to arrive. This is not a claim about how quickly any specific system penalizes a slow reply — it is a plain fact about how a person juggling many simultaneous conversations is more likely to keep moving forward with whichever ones are easiest to keep moving.

## Reapplication cadence

Reapplying to a company or a specific team after an earlier rejection is normal and expected, not a breach of etiquette — provided something material has actually changed since the last attempt. A genuine change looks like a new anchor project completed since the last application, a degree milestone reached, relevant new employment, or a realization that a different role family within the same company actually fits your evidence better than the one you tried before. Any of these gives a reader something new to evaluate that was not there the first time.

Two failure modes sit on either side of this and are worth rejecting explicitly. Reapplying immediately with an unchanged application signals nothing new and asks the same reader's attention to be spent twice on the same evidence that did not succeed the first time. Waiting a fixed period — six months, a year — as though the calendar itself were the relevant variable, misses the actual criterion entirely: what matters is whether new evidence exists, not how much time has passed since the old evidence was reviewed. A candidate who completes a strong new anchor project in two months has more standing to reapply than one who waits a full year and changes nothing.

::: key
Reapplying is normal and expected when something material has genuinely changed — a new anchor project, a degree milestone, relevant new employment, or a better-fitting role family. The calendar is not the criterion; new evidence is.
:::

One further, specific piece of advice: do not relitigate a previous rejection in a reapplication or in a note to a recruiter. Explaining why an earlier decision was wrong, even when the explanation is accurate, spends the reader's attention on a question that is no longer live — the decision was already made — rather than on the new evidence that is actually the point of reapplying at all.

::: example Two reapplications, six weeks apart and five months apart
The first candidate is declined for a specific requisition and reapplies to the identical requisition three weeks later, having changed nothing about her resume, projects, or evidence. Nothing new exists for a reader to evaluate; the application is, in substance, the same one already reviewed and declined.

The second candidate is declined for a similar requisition and reapplies five months later, having completed a new anchor project — a consistency-checked attitude estimator with a documented NEES/NIS analysis — and finished a degree milestone in the interim. This reapplication genuinely differs from the first attempt: a reader now has new, real evidence to weigh that was not available the first time, regardless of how much or how little time has passed. The five-month gap itself is not what makes this reapplication reasonable — the new evidence inside it is.
:::

::: example Relitigating a rejection, and letting new evidence speak instead
Relitigating: a reapplication note opens with "I was surprised and honestly a bit frustrated not to move forward last time, especially given my qualifications — I'd like to understand what specifically was missing." This spends the reader's attention arguing about a decision that is already final, and puts the reader in the position of defending or explaining a past call rather than evaluating new evidence.

Letting new evidence speak: a reapplication note opens with "Since my last application, I've completed a consistency-checked attitude estimator project, which I've linked below along with my updated resume, and I'd like to be considered for the current opening on this team." This gives the reader something new and concrete to evaluate immediately, without spending any of her attention relitigating a decision that is no longer open for discussion.
:::

:::

::: answer
A templated message is recognizable as templated because its content could apply to any recipient in a similar role, which signals it cost the sender little to send; a request for a favor with no prior exchange attached reads as transactional rather than conversational. A recipient receiving a message that clearly required no specific attention to write has little reason to invest real attention in responding to it, independent of any particular response-rate figure.
:::

::: check
What specifically makes a LinkedIn outreach message "specific" in the sense this lesson uses the word, and why does that matter more than the message being polite or lengthy?
:::

::: answer
Specificity means the message references something real and identifiable about the recipient's own actual work — a paper, a talk, a particular project — connected to something concrete about the sender, and closes with one focused, answerable question. This matters more than politeness or length because it is the only part of the message that proves the sender actually engaged with who the recipient specifically is, rather than sending an interchangeable message to many people; politeness and length do not carry that same evidence.
:::

::: check
A recruiter messages a candidate about a specific open requisition. Why does this lesson recommend a prompt, substantive reply rather than a slower, more considered one?
:::

::: answer
A recruiter is typically managing multiple open requisitions and multiple candidates simultaneously, and a clear, timely reply keeps a specific candidate actively under consideration for a specific requisition rather than falling out of an unprioritized queue because a response took too long to arrive. This is a statement about how attention gets allocated across many simultaneous conversations, not a claim about a fixed deadline or penalty.
:::

::: check
State the reapplication rule in your own words: what actually licenses a reapplication to a company or team that previously declined a candidate, and what does not?
:::

::: answer
A reapplication is licensed by genuine new evidence since the previous attempt — a new anchor project, a completed degree milestone, relevant new employment, or a better-fitting role family than the one tried before. The mere passage of time does not license it by itself; a long wait with no real change offers a reader nothing new to evaluate, while a short wait containing genuine new evidence does.
:::

::: check
A candidate is declined for a requisition and reapplies to the identical requisition three days later, having changed nothing about her resume or projects. What is wrong with this, independent of how strong her original application actually was?
:::

::: answer
Regardless of the original application's quality, this reapplication presents a reader with the same evidence already reviewed and declined, asking for a second read of substantively the same material. There is nothing new for the reader to evaluate, so the reapplication cannot change the outcome even if the underlying application was genuinely strong — the problem is the absence of new evidence, not necessarily the quality of what already exists.
:::

::: check
Why does relitigating a past rejection in a reapplication note work against the candidate, even when her account of the earlier decision is entirely accurate?
:::

::: answer
Relitigating spends the reader's attention on a decision that is already final and not open for reconsideration, and it puts the reader in the position of having to defend or explain a past call rather than evaluate new evidence — which is the only thing that can actually change the outcome this time. Accuracy does not change this dynamic: even a fair complaint about an earlier decision directs attention away from the new evidence a reapplication needs to be built around, and toward a question the reader has no reason to reopen.
:::

## Summary

| Situation | Do | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reaching out on LinkedIn | Reference specific real work; ask one concrete question; make no ask on first contact | A templated message with an immediate referral or job ask |
| An unanswered outreach message | Leave it; move on to the next specific, well-researched contact | Repeated follow-up, which reads as pressure |
| A recruiter messages you | Reply promptly and substantively | Leaving a specific-requisition message unanswered for weeks |
| Considering a reapplication | Reapply once something material has genuinely changed | Reapplying unchanged, or waiting a fixed period with no new evidence |
| Writing a reapplication note | Lead with the new evidence and let it speak | Relitigating the earlier decision, even if your account of it is accurate |
