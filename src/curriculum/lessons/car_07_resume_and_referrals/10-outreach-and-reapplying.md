Imagine two letters arrive in your mailbox. One says "Dear Resident" and offers you a credit card. The other is handwritten, uses your name, and mentions the science fair project you did last spring. You throw the first away without opening it. You read the second one twice. Nobody had to tell you to — you could see at a glance which one somebody wrote *for you*.

The people you might contact in the space industry sort their messages the same way. This module has covered building the resume, sending it in, and earning the kind of visible presence that makes a referral possible over time. This last lesson adds two tools for someone searching *right now*:

- reaching out directly to people on **[[LinkedIn|linkedin]]** — a social network for work — instead of only waiting to be found;
- knowing what to do when a first try at a company or team goes nowhere, and when it makes sense to **reapply**.

Both are about taking initiative. And in both, initiative done carelessly costs more than it earns. That is why each gets a precise treatment here, not general advice.

## LinkedIn outreach done well

A message that gets read carefully, and gets a real answer, does two specific things.

1. **It points to something real and public about the recipient's own work.** A paper, a talk, a specific project, a post they wrote. It does not open with a **templated** connection request — one written once and sent to anyone in the field.
2. **It asks one concrete question someone can answer.** Not an open-ended "Would you be open to a chat sometime?"

Why does the question matter so much? A specific question can be answered in two minutes from a phone. An open-ended one makes the reader do the work: figure out what you actually want, then find time for something with no clear shape. Most busy people put that off, and "later" quietly becomes never.

There is a third rule, and it is worth stating on its own: **make no ask on first contact.** Do not ask for a referral, a job, or anything else in the first message. Let a real exchange happen first, even a short one. A message that leads with an ask reads as a transaction from its first line. It also forces the reader to decide whether to help a stranger before they know anything about whether helping makes sense.

::: key
LinkedIn outreach that works: a specific, short message referencing something they actually did, one concrete question, and no ask on first contact. The specificity itself is the signal — it shows the sender actually looked at what the recipient does, instead of sending the same message to many people at once.
:::

## LinkedIn outreach done badly

The failing pattern is the mirror image, and it has three parts:

- the same templated note sent to dozens of engineers at one target company;
- a request for a referral or a job up front, before any real exchange;
- repeated follow-up messages when the first one goes unanswered.

Each part makes the others worse. A templated message is recognizable the moment its content could fit anyone in the same role. A request that arrives with no earlier conversation reads as a transaction. And repeated follow-ups on an ignored template do not read as persistence — they read as pressure. They make it easier, not harder, for the reader to decline or keep ignoring you. Worse, people remember them.

The mechanism is not mysterious, and it does not rest on any statistic about reply rates. A message that plainly cost the sender nothing to write — because it was not written for this reader at all — tends to get the same small effort back, if it gets anything. A message that visibly took real attention to research and write tends to be read as what it is: an attempt at a real, specific conversation.

::: key
Mass-templated connection requests with an immediate request for a referral are ignored and remembered.
:::

::: warning Volume and pressure work against you, not for you
Sending the same message to many people, and following up again and again when ignored, does not raise your odds by widening your reach. It makes each message easier to spot as generic, and repeated unsolicited follow-up reads as pressure, not genuine interest. If a well-researched message goes unanswered, let it go and put the effort into your next specific contact.
:::

::: example A LinkedIn message, generic and specific, to the same recipient
**Generic:** "Hi! I'd love to connect and learn more about your experience at your company. Also, if there are any GNC openings, I'd really appreciate a referral!"

Check it against the three rules:

- It could go to any engineer at any company with the name swapped. Nothing refers to the reader's work.
- It asks a favor before any exchange has happened.
- It gives the reader nothing specific to answer — only a vague wish to "learn more".

It fails all three.

**Specific:** "Hi — I read your team's recent conference paper on attitude-estimator consistency testing. I ran a similar **[[NEES/NIS analysis|nees-nis]]** on a quaternion EKF I built and found a very similar overconfidence issue traced to an under-tuned process-noise term. Curious whether that's a pattern you've seen show up often in practice, or whether it was more specific to your particular sensor setup."

Check it against the same rules:

- It names a real, specific piece of the reader's work — the paper.
- It connects that paper to a real, specific piece of the sender's own work.
- It ends with one focused question that has two possible answers, and asks for nothing else.

It passes all three. It is also only a few sentences long.
:::

## Building a target list, not a mailing list

Outreach done well is slow, so you cannot do it with hundreds of people. You do it with a short, chosen list. A good size to start is **twenty people**, drawn from four kinds of contact:

- **engineers** working in your target **[[role family|role-family]]** — the group of related jobs your resume is tailored for;
- **recruiters** who post openings in that family;
- **conference presenters** whose papers relate to your anchor projects (the last lesson showed where to find them);
- **maintainers** of open-source projects in the field.

For each person, write down two things: *why they are on the list*, and *what you would actually have to say to them*. Then comes the hard, honest step. Anyone for whom the second answer is "nothing" comes off the list. If you have nothing specific to say, any message you send them will be a template, and you already know how templates land.

What is left is a **[[contact plan|contact-plan]]** that is not spam: a short list of people, each with a real reason to talk.

## Recruiters reaching out, and replying to them

LinkedIn is also a place where a company's own **[[recruiters|recruiter]]** — the people whose job is to find and move candidates through hiring — sometimes contact you, or can be contacted, about a specific opening.

When a recruiter messages you, give a **prompt, substantive reply**. Not necessarily within the hour, but not weeks later either. The reason is how their work runs, not urgency for its own sake. A recruiter is usually juggling several open **requisitions** (specific open positions) and many candidates at once. A clear, timely answer keeps you actively in the running for that requisition. A slow one risks you slipping to the bottom of a pile nobody is sorting.

This is not a claim that any system penalizes a slow reply after a set number of days. It is a plain fact about people: someone handling many conversations at once keeps moving the ones that are easiest to keep moving.

## Reapplication cadence

**Cadence** means the rhythm or timing of something — here, how often and when you apply again.

Reapplying to a company or team after an earlier rejection is **normal and expected**. It is not bad manners — provided something **material** (meaningful, not cosmetic) has changed since your last try. A real change looks like one of these:

- a new **anchor project** finished since the last application;
- a **degree milestone** reached;
- relevant **new employment**;
- realizing that a **different role family** at the same company fits your evidence better than the one you tried.

Any of these gives a reader something new to judge that was not there the first time.

There are two ways to get this wrong, one on each side:

- **Reapplying at once, unchanged.** It shows nothing new, and it asks the same reader to spend attention twice on the same evidence that already did not succeed.
- **Waiting a fixed time — six months, a year — as if the calendar were what counts.** This misses the real test. What matters is whether new evidence exists, not how much time has passed since the old evidence was read. A candidate who finishes a strong new anchor project in two months has more reason to reapply than one who waits a full year and changes nothing.

::: key
Reapplying is normal and expected. What matters is that something material has changed: a new anchor project, a completed degree milestone, a relevant job, or a different role family that actually fits. Resubmitting an unchanged application signals nothing. The calendar is not the criterion; new evidence is.
:::

One more specific piece of advice: **do not [[relitigate|relitigate]] a previous rejection** — do not argue the old decision again — in a reapplication or in a note to a recruiter. Explaining why an earlier decision was wrong, even when you are right, spends the reader's attention on a question that is closed. The decision is already made. The reader's attention belongs on your new evidence, which is the whole point of reapplying.

::: example Two reapplications, three weeks apart and five months apart
**The first candidate** is declined for a specific requisition. Three weeks later she reapplies to the identical requisition. She has changed nothing: same resume, same projects, same evidence.

What is new for a reader to judge? Nothing. In substance it is the same application that was already reviewed and declined.

**The second candidate** is declined for a similar requisition. Five months later she reapplies. In between she has finished a new anchor project — a consistency-checked attitude estimator with a documented NEES/NIS analysis — and reached a degree milestone.

What is new? Two real pieces of evidence that did not exist the first time.

Sanity check — is it the five-month gap that makes the second one reasonable? No. Had she finished the same project in five weeks, she would have had the same standing. The gap itself does nothing. The **[[new evidence inside it|reapply-timeline]]** is what counts.
:::

::: example Relitigating a rejection, and letting new evidence speak instead
**Relitigating:** a reapplication note opens with "I was surprised and honestly a bit frustrated not to move forward last time, especially given my qualifications — I'd like to understand what specifically was missing."

What does this ask the reader to do? Argue about a decision that is final, and defend or explain an old call. None of that is judging new evidence.

**Letting new evidence speak:** a reapplication note opens with "Since my last application, I've completed a consistency-checked attitude estimator project, which I've linked below along with my updated resume, and I'd like to be considered for the current opening on this team."

What does this one ask the reader to do? Look at something new and concrete, straight away. None of her attention goes to reopening a decision that is no longer open.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
Explain, as a mechanism rather than a statistic, why a templated LinkedIn message asking for a referral on first contact tends to fail.
:::

::: answer
A templated message is recognizable as a template because its content could apply to anyone in a similar role, which shows it cost the sender little to send. A request for a favor with no earlier exchange reads as a transaction, not a conversation. A reader who gets a message that plainly needed no attention to write has little reason to put real attention into answering it. That holds whatever the reply-rate figures happen to be.
:::

::: check
What specifically makes a LinkedIn outreach message "specific" in the sense this lesson uses the word, and why does that matter more than the message being polite or lengthy?
:::

::: answer
A specific message refers to something real and identifiable in the recipient's own work — a paper, a talk, a particular project — connects it to something concrete about the sender, and ends with one focused, answerable question. This matters more than politeness or length because it is the only part of the message that proves the sender engaged with who this recipient is, rather than sending an interchangeable message to many people. Politeness and length carry no such evidence: a template can be both.
:::

::: check
A recruiter messages a candidate about a specific open requisition. Why does this lesson recommend a prompt, substantive reply rather than a slower, more considered one?
:::

::: answer
A recruiter is usually managing several open requisitions and many candidates at once. A clear, timely reply keeps the candidate actively under consideration for that requisition, instead of slipping down an unsorted queue because the answer took too long. This is about how a person's attention gets shared across many conversations at once, not a claim about any fixed deadline or penalty.
:::

::: check
State the reapplication rule in your own words: what actually licenses a reapplication to a company or team that previously declined a candidate, and what does not?
:::

::: answer
Real new evidence since the last attempt licenses it: a new anchor project, a completed degree milestone, relevant new employment, or a better-fitting role family than the one tried before. The passage of time alone does not. A long wait with no real change gives a reader nothing new to judge, while a short wait that contains real new evidence does.
:::

::: check
A candidate is declined for a requisition and reapplies to the identical requisition three days later, having changed nothing about her resume or projects. What is wrong with this, independent of how strong her original application actually was?
:::

::: answer
However good the original was, this reapplication shows the reader the same evidence that was already reviewed and declined, and asks for a second read of the same material. There is nothing new to evaluate, so it cannot change the outcome even if the original application was strong. The problem is the absence of new evidence, not necessarily the quality of what is already there.
:::

::: check
Why does relitigating a past rejection in a reapplication note work against the candidate, even when her account of the earlier decision is entirely accurate?
:::

::: answer
It spends the reader's attention on a decision that is final and not open for reconsideration. It also puts the reader in the position of defending or explaining a past call instead of judging new evidence — and new evidence is the only thing that can change the outcome this time. Being accurate does not change this. Even a fair complaint pulls attention away from the new evidence the reapplication should be built around, toward a question the reader has no reason to reopen.
:::

## Summary

| Situation | Do | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reaching out on LinkedIn | Reference specific real work; ask one concrete question; make no ask on first contact | A templated message with an immediate referral or job ask |
| Choosing whom to contact | A list of about twenty: engineers, recruiters, presenters, maintainers — each with a real reason to talk | Anyone you would have nothing specific to say to |
| An unanswered outreach message | Leave it; move on to the next specific, well-researched contact | Repeated follow-up, which reads as pressure |
| A recruiter messages you | Reply promptly and substantively | Leaving a specific-requisition message unanswered for weeks |
| Considering a reapplication | Reapply once something material has really changed | Reapplying unchanged, or waiting a fixed period with no new evidence |
| Writing a reapplication note | Lead with the new evidence and let it speak | Relitigating the earlier decision, even if your account of it is accurate |

::: context linkedin What LinkedIn is
LinkedIn is a social network built around work instead of friends and photos. Each person has a profile that reads like an online resume: jobs, schools, skills, projects. People "connect" with colleagues, post about their work, and send each other messages.

It launched in 2003 and has been owned by Microsoft since 2016. Recruiters use it heavily to search for candidates, which is why a clear, honest profile that matches your resume matters. Most engineers you might want to reach have a profile there, even if they rarely post.
:::

::: context nees-nis What NEES and NIS check
Both are ways to test whether a filter is honest about its own uncertainty. A filter doesn't only guess where the spacecraft is; it also says how sure it is.

- **NEES** (often said "neez"), the normalized estimation error squared, compares the filter's real errors to the uncertainty it claims. You can only compute it in simulation, where the truth is known.
- **NIS** ("N-I-S"), the normalized innovation squared, does the same using the gap between each measurement and what the filter predicted it would be — so it works on real data too.

"Overconfident" means the filter claims to be surer than it really is. A **quaternion EKF** — an extended Kalman filter that tracks orientation with four numbers called a quaternion — is one of this course's anchor projects, and the Kalman filter module teaches both tests in full.
:::

::: context role-family Role families, again
Lesson 6 of this module introduced the role family: a group of related jobs that care about the same evidence. Entry and landing work, estimation and orbit determination, and GNC infrastructure are examples of families.

A target list built around one family keeps your outreach consistent. The engineers you contact, the recruiters you follow, and the papers you read all point the same way as the resume you will eventually send. That is what lets a single conversation turn into a useful next step.
:::

::: context recruiter What a recruiter actually does
A **recruiter** finds candidates for open positions and moves them through the hiring steps: screening resumes, setting up interviews, passing along offers. An **in-house** recruiter works for the company itself. An **agency** recruiter works for an outside firm that the company pays to find people.

Each recruiter usually has several requisitions open at once, each with its own pile of candidates. That is the picture behind the advice to reply promptly: you are one of many threads in a busy day, and the threads that get clear answers are the ones that keep moving. The next module walks through that pipeline stage by stage.
:::

::: context relitigate Where "relitigate" comes from
To **litigate** means to fight a case in court; it comes from the Latin *lis*, a lawsuit. To **relitigate** is to drag a case that was already decided back into court and argue it all over again.

Courts mostly refuse to do that — once something is decided, it stays decided, so everyone can move on. Hiring works the same way. The earlier decision is closed. What is open is a new application, judged on what it contains now.
:::
