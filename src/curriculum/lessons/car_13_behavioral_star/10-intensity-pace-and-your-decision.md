There are two decisions being made in this process and only one of them gets a structure. Theirs has rounds, questions and a defined end. Yours has whatever attention you have left over, usually arrives in the week you get an offer, and is made under exactly the conditions — time pressure, relief, sunk effort — that make people decide badly.

This lesson is about the second decision. It covers what culture-fit questions are actually probing, stated as a mechanism rather than as folklore; the three questions that tell you most about pace; how to read the evidence you can gather; and why the decision has to be made before the offer arrives rather than after.

::: key
Cultural fit is two-way. The pace and hours are genuine tradeoffs. Use the round to gather information for your own decision: what a normal week looks like, how often crunch happens, and what happened the last time a deadline slipped.
:::

## The asymmetry is smaller than it feels

Sitting in the round, the power looks entirely one-sided. Some of that is real — they have many candidates and you have few processes — and some of it is an artefact of the format, which is a sequence of people asking you questions.

Three things push back. The cost of a bad match is paid by both sides, and it is not small for either: an engineer who leaves in eight months has cost the team a hiring cycle and their own time, which is why the previous lesson's mismatch argument is not only about you. You hold information the other side cannot get anywhere else — what you will actually tolerate, what you actually want to be doing in two years. And this is the last cheap moment to ask: after you accept, every question you did not ask becomes a fact you have to live with.

There is also the point the previous lesson made. The expectation about extended hours is published. A condition that has been disclosed is one you are entitled to ask detailed questions about, and doing so reads as someone taking the term seriously rather than as someone looking for an exemption.

## What culture-fit questions are actually probing

The folklore version is that these questions are about likeability — whether you would fit in socially, whether the people in the room warm to you. Treated that way they become an exercise in impression management, which is both unpleasant and unreliable.

The mechanism is more specific and more useful. An organisation knows the conditions it imposes on the people inside it: a particular pace, a particular amount of ambiguity, a particular way that disagreements are conducted, a particular expectation about who owns a problem when it falls between two teams. Every one of those conditions produces behaviour, and the behaviour varies by person. A culture question is a question about *what you predictably do when that condition arrives*.

That reframe does three things.

It tells you what the material is. Your answers come from the same place as everything else in this module: specific past behaviour under the relevant condition. "How do you handle ambiguity?" is answered with what you did the last time a requirement did not exist, not with a self-description.

It tells you why self-description fails. "I thrive in fast-paced environments" is a claim about a disposition, unfalsifiable, and indistinguishable from the same sentence said by someone who does not. The version with information in it is the instance: here is a fast period I was in, here is what I did, here is what it cost.

And it tells you what you can learn from the questions themselves. The conditions an organisation probes for are conditions it believes it has. A round that asks repeatedly about ambiguity, ownership across boundaries and recovering from being wrong in public is telling you something about the environment, and you are allowed to notice.

What this module does not tell you is who asks these questions in any given process, how the answers are recorded, or how they are weighed. Where you need that, ask the recruiter.

## The three questions

Card-carrying versions of the three questions are worth knowing exactly, because each one targets something a general question cannot reach.

**What does a normal week look like?** This establishes the baseline. Without it, everything else is uncalibrated — you cannot interpret "we had a busy month" without knowing what an unbusy one is. Ask it of engineers rather than of the process, and ask about *their* last few weeks rather than about the team in general, because a general answer tends to come back as a description of policy.

**How often does a crunch period happen?** This is the frequency, and it is the number that converts a disclosed condition into a lived one. A stated willingness to work extended hours when needed to meet critical deadlines means something very different at twice a year than at three weeks in four. Nothing in this module tells you which it is, anywhere, and you should be suspicious of any source that tells you confidently without saying how it knows.

**What happened the last time a deadline slipped?** This is the most informative of the three and the one candidates leave out. The first two questions describe a schedule; this one describes behaviour under stress, which is what you are actually trying to find out. Who absorbed it. Whether scope moved or people did. Whether anyone said in advance that it was going to slip, and what happened to them. Whether the review afterwards looked for a cause or for a person. A team answers this question with specifics or it does not, and either response tells you something.

## Reading the evidence you can gather

**Engineers on the team** are the best source for the baseline and for the last-slip story, because they are describing their own experience. Ask for concrete periods, not for assessments.

**The recruiter** is the right person for anything about process and policy, as this track's screens module sets out.

**Public review sites** need a specific reading protocol, and this module's own resource note gives it: employee reviews are self-selected in both directions, so read them for recurring specifics about pace rather than for a verdict. A single furious review and a single glowing one are equally uninformative. What is worth something is a detail that recurs across many reviews written years apart, particularly a mundane one — the shape of a typical week, how a particular phase of a program goes, what people say they were surprised by. The overall rating is the least useful thing on the page.

**Triangulate rather than accumulate.** A specific fact that appears independently in an engineer's account, a review from two years ago and something in the posting is worth more than ten strong opinions pointing the same way.

::: warning Do not decide after the offer
The moment an offer exists, the question changes shape. It stops being *do I want this* and becomes *am I going to turn this down*, which is a different question with a different answer, asked under time pressure, after months of effort, with a deadline attached and other people's expectations in play. Whatever you conclude then will be reasoned backwards from a decision you have mostly already made.

Write your criteria down before the process finishes, while nothing is at stake. Then the offer is compared against something rather than assessed on its own, and you will know whether you are changing your mind or merely wanting the thing.
:::

## Doing the arithmetic honestly

This is a tradeoff, not a moral question. Intensity buys things that are real: rate of learning, proximity to hardware that flies, a compressed number of years of exposure that can take a decade elsewhere. It costs things that are also real. Neither the person who takes it nor the person who does not is making a mistake, and framing it as a test of seriousness is how people end up somewhere that does not suit them.

Three questions to answer on paper, before you need the answers.

**What am I optimising for, and over what period?** "Two years of the steepest learning I can get, accepting that my life outside work will be thin, in exchange for being able to work on flight software afterwards" is a coherent position. So is "I want work I find interesting and a life I can sustain indefinitely, and I will take longer to get where I am going." What is not coherent is a position with no time horizon in it.

**What would I have to keep, whatever else goes?** Be concrete: sleep below a certain amount, a relationship, a training schedule, a treatment regime, time with a child. This is the list that converts vague unease into a testable question.

**What would tell me it is not working, and by when?** Deciding in advance what the warning signs are makes them visible later. It is the same discipline as fixing a pass criterion before the campaign runs, applied to yourself.

## If you find a mismatch during the process

Three legitimate moves, and one that is not.

You can **say it**, which is often better than it sounds — a specific requirement stated early can sometimes be accommodated, and asking costs nothing you had.

You can **withdraw**, which is a normal thing to do and does not burn a bridge if done promptly and courteously.

You can **decide the tradeoff is worth it anyway**, with your eyes open and a horizon attached. That is a real choice, and it is different from drifting into it.

What is not legitimate — because it is the mismatch the previous lesson described, entered deliberately — is accepting while planning to renegotiate the terms later. That plan does not usually work, and it puts you in the eight-month resignation with less standing than if you had said nothing.

::: example Two candidates, the same three questions
Both ask the same three questions of an engineer on the team. The answers below are illustrations of what informative and uninformative answers sound like — they are not reports of what any particular team said, and nothing in this module tells you which kind you will get.

**Candidate A asks them as a checklist:**

*"What is the work-life balance like here?"* — a question that invites a summary. An honest answer to it is a generality, because the question asked for one. Whatever comes back is uncalibrated: she has no baseline against which to read *it is intense but manageable*.

*"Do you work a lot of weekends?"* — answerable with yes or no, and the yes-or-no carries no frequency and no shape.

*"Is the culture good?"* — unanswerable in the negative by anyone still employed there, which makes the answer worthless regardless of its content.

**Candidate B asks the same three areas as questions about specific experience:**

*"What did your last three weeks actually look like — when did you get in, when did you leave, and was that typical?"*

An answer with information in it sounds like: "Two of them were normal, in around nine, out around seven. The third I was here until eleven three nights running because we were chasing a regression in the guidance build before a test. That is roughly the pattern — a few heavy weeks around milestones and normal ones otherwise." An answer without information sounds like: "It varies a lot, but I would say it is manageable."

*"How often do you get a period like that, over a year?"*

Informative: "Four or five, clustered around vehicle milestones, and they last one to three weeks." Uninformative: "It depends on the program."

*"When a deadline last slipped, what happened?"*

Informative, and this is the one worth listening hardest to: who knew first, whether they said so early, what happened to the person who said so, whether scope moved or hours did, and what the review afterwards was looking for. Uninformative: "We do not really let things slip."

**What B has that A does not:** three calibrated data points from one conversation, and the ability to compare them against the next engineer's. She also asked in a way that is easy to answer honestly, because none of her questions required the other person to make a judgement about their own employer — they asked for facts about a specific recent period. That is the difference between a question that produces information and one that produces a position.
:::

::: example A decision record written before the offer exists
Written in week three of the process, before any outcome is known. It is a private document; nobody else sees it.

**What I am optimising for, and for how long.** The next two to three years: the steepest slope I can get on real flight-relevant GNC, close to hardware that actually flies. I am willing to have a thin life outside work for that period. I am not willing to treat it as indefinite, and if I am still doing it at five years without having decided to, that is a failure of attention rather than a choice.

**What I keep regardless.** Six hours of sleep as a floor, not an average. The Sunday call with my mother. Enough exercise that my back does not go. Two evenings a week are already committed and are not negotiable.

**What I will accept, explicitly.** Cancelled plans. Poor cooking. Very little reading that is not work. Several heavy weeks a year, and a deadline period where I am effectively unavailable to everyone.

**What I want to know before I say yes.** What a normal week looks like for two engineers, not one. How many heavy periods a year and how long they run. What happened the last time something slipped — specifically whether the person who flagged it early was treated as helpful or as a problem. Whether the team has anyone with caring responsibilities and how that works in practice, which is the most reliable single indicator I can think of for whether the stated flexibility is real.

**What would tell me it is not working, and by when.** If at six months I have not learned anything I could not have learned elsewhere, the trade is not being paid. If the heavy periods have been more than half the weeks, the frequency was misdescribed and I should say so rather than absorb it. If I have dropped two of the four things on my keep list for more than a month, I am past the boundary I set and I should notice that as data rather than as weakness.

**Why this is written down now.** Because in the week an offer arrives I will not be able to tell the difference between wanting the job and having concluded it is right, and I want something to compare against that was written by someone with nothing at stake.
:::

:::

::: check
Why is "what happened the last time a deadline slipped" more informative than either of the other two questions?
:::

::: answer
Because the other two describe a schedule and this one describes behaviour under stress, which is the thing you are actually trying to learn. It surfaces who absorbed the slip, whether scope moved or people did, whether anybody said in advance that it was coming, what happened to that person, and whether the review afterwards was looking for a cause or for someone to hold responsible. It is also hard to answer in generalities: either specifics arrive or they do not, and the absence of specifics is itself a data point. The other two questions calibrate the baseline and the frequency, which you need in order to interpret this one.
:::

::: check
Why does this lesson insist you write your criteria down before an offer exists, given that you will know more later?
:::

::: answer
Because the question changes shape when an offer arrives. *Do I want this* becomes *am I going to turn this down*, asked under a deadline, after months of effort, with other people's expectations attached — and reasoning conducted in that state tends to be constructed backwards from a conclusion already reached. Writing the criteria while nothing is at stake gives you an artefact produced by someone disinterested, and turns the later decision into a comparison rather than an assessment. It also makes it possible to tell the difference between changing your mind, which is legitimate, and wanting the thing, which is not a reason.
:::

::: check
How should you read employee reviews on a public site?
:::

::: answer
For recurring specifics about pace, not for a verdict. Reviews are self-selected in both directions — people write them when they are unusually angry or unusually pleased — so the distribution is not a sample of anything and the overall rating is the least informative element on the page. What carries information is a mundane detail that recurs independently across many reviews written years apart: the shape of a typical week, what a particular program phase does to a schedule, what people say surprised them. Then triangulate against what engineers tell you directly and against the posting. One specific fact confirmed by independent sources is worth more than a dozen strong opinions.
:::

::: check
A candidate discovers mid-process that the pace is not something she is willing to accept. What are her options, and which one is not available?
:::

::: answer
She can say so and ask whether the specific thing she needs can be accommodated, which sometimes it can and which costs nothing she had. She can withdraw, which is a normal, unremarkable act if done promptly and courteously. Or she can decide the tradeoff is worth it anyway, with a time horizon attached and with her eyes open — a real choice, quite different from drifting into it. What is not available is accepting while intending to renegotiate the terms later: it is the mismatch the previous lesson described, entered on purpose, and it usually ends in the same resignation with less standing than saying nothing would have given her.
:::

::: check
Why does this lesson refuse to treat intensity as a test of seriousness?
:::

::: answer
Because it is a tradeoff with real value on both sides, and framing it as a character test corrupts the decision. Intensity genuinely buys a steeper learning slope, proximity to hardware that flies, and a compression of exposure that can take much longer elsewhere; it genuinely costs time, attention and a certain amount of everything outside work. Which side of that is right depends on what someone is optimising for and over what period, and both answers are coherent. The failure is not choosing either one — it is choosing without a horizon, so that a bargain made for two years silently becomes the shape of a decade.
:::

## Summary

| Question | What it establishes | Ask whom |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What did your last three weeks look like | The baseline, without which nothing else can be read | Engineers, about themselves |
| How often does a heavy period happen | The frequency that turns a disclosed condition into a lived one | Engineers, over a year |
| What happened the last time a deadline slipped | Behaviour under stress: who absorbed it, whether scope or people moved | Engineers; listen hardest here |
| Process, policy, timelines | What is formal rather than experiential | The recruiter |

Culture questions are not about likeability: they ask what you predictably do under conditions the organisation knows it imposes, which means they are answered with the same specific past behaviour as everything else in this module. Write your own criteria down before the offer exists.
