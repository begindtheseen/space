Think about tryouts for a sports team. The coach watches you and decides whether you make the team. But you are watching too. How hard are practices? Do the older players look happy or worn out? If you make it, you show up all season.

An interview works the same way. Two decisions are being made, and only one gets a structure. The company's has rounds, questions and a clear end. Yours gets whatever attention is left over. It usually arrives in the week you get an **[[offer|job-offer]]** — under exactly the conditions that make people decide badly: time pressure, relief, and months of effort already spent.

This lesson is about your decision. It covers what **culture-fit** questions — questions about whether you will work well in the company's way of doing things — are really testing; the three questions that tell you most about **pace**, meaning how fast and how hard people work there; how to read the evidence you can collect; and why you decide what you want *before* the offer arrives.

::: key
Cultural fit is two-way. The pace and hours are genuine tradeoffs. Use the round to gather information for your own decision: what a normal week looks like, how often crunch happens, and what happened the last time a deadline slipped.
:::

## The power gap is smaller than it feels

In the interview, the power looks one-sided. Some of that is real: they have many candidates, and you have a few interviews going. Some is a trick of the format — a line of people asking *you* questions.

Three facts push back.

**A bad match hurts both sides.** An engineer who quits after eight months has cost the team a whole hiring cycle and cost themselves eight months. That is why the previous lesson's warning about a mismatch is not only about you.

**You hold information nobody else has:** what you will really put up with, and what you want to be doing in two years.

**This is the last cheap moment to ask.** After you accept, every question you did not ask becomes a fact you live with.

And the extended-hours expectation is printed in the posting. A condition stated openly is one you may ask about in detail; asking shows you take it seriously.

## What culture-fit questions are really testing

The popular belief is that these questions are about being liked. Believe that, and the interview becomes a performance of likeability — unpleasant, and unreliable.

The real mechanism is more useful. An organization knows the conditions it puts its people under:

- a particular pace;
- a particular amount of **ambiguity** — not knowing exactly what the goal or the rules are;
- a particular way of handling disagreements;
- a particular answer to "whose problem is this?" when a problem falls between two teams.

Each condition produces behavior, and the behavior differs from person to person. So a culture question asks *what you reliably do when that condition shows up*.

That view does three things for you.

**It tells you what to answer with:** specific past behavior under that condition, like everything else in this module. "How do you handle ambiguity?" is answered with what you did the last time a requirement did not exist — not with a description of yourself.

**It tells you why describing yourself fails.** "I thrive in fast-paced environments" is a personality claim. Nobody can check it, and it sounds the same from someone who does not thrive. The version with information in it is an instance: here is a fast period I was in, what I did, and what it cost.

**It lets you learn from the questions.** The conditions an organization asks about are the ones it believes it has. A round that asks again and again about ambiguity, owning problems across team lines, and recovering from being wrong in public is telling you about the place. You are allowed to notice.

What this module does *not* tell you is who asks these questions in a given process, or how answers are recorded and weighed. For that, ask the **[[recruiter|recruiter]]**.

## The three questions

Each reaches something a general question cannot. Learn them exactly.

**What does a normal week look like?** This sets the **baseline** — the ordinary level you compare everything else against. "We had a busy month" means nothing until you know what a quiet month is. Ask engineers, not the process, and ask about *their own* last few weeks. A question about "the team" tends to come back as a description of policy.

**How often does a crunch period happen?** A **[[crunch|crunch]]** is a stretch of extra-long hours to hit a deadline. This gets the **frequency**, which turns a stated condition into a lived one. "Willing to work extended hours when needed to meet critical deadlines" means something very different at twice a year than at three weeks in four. This module does not tell you which it is anywhere — and be suspicious of any source that says so confidently without saying how it knows.

**What happened the last time a deadline slipped?** A deadline **slips** when the work is not done by the planned date. This is the most revealing question, and the one candidates skip. The first two describe a schedule; this one describes behavior under stress:

- Who absorbed the slip?
- Did the **scope** — the amount of work promised — shrink, or did people work longer?
- Did anyone warn early, and what happened to that person?
- Did the **[[review afterward|post-mortem]]** look for a cause, or for someone to blame?

A team answers this with specifics or it does not. Either way, you learn something.

## Reading the evidence you can gather

**Engineers on the team** are the best source for the baseline and the last-slip story, because they describe their own lives. Ask for concrete periods, not opinions.

**The recruiter** is the right person for process and policy, as this track's screens module explains.

**Public review sites** such as Glassdoor need a special reading, and this module's resource note gives it: employee reviews are **[[self-selected|self-selection]]** in both directions, so read them for recurring specifics about pace, not for a verdict. One furious review and one glowing one tell you equally little. What counts is a detail that keeps coming back across reviews written years apart — especially a boring one: the shape of a typical week, how a program phase goes, what surprised people. The star rating is the least useful thing on the page.

**Triangulate, do not pile up.** To **[[triangulate|triangulate]]** is to check one fact from several independent directions. A specific fact that shows up separately in an engineer's account, a two-year-old review, and the posting beats ten strong opinions pointing the same way.

::: warning Do not decide after the offer
Once an offer exists, the question changes shape. *Do I want this?* becomes *am I going to turn this down?* — a different question with a different answer, asked under time pressure, after months of **[[effort already spent|sunk-cost]]**, with other people's hopes in play. Whatever you conclude then is reasoned backward from a decision you have mostly already made.

So write your criteria down before the process finishes, while nothing is at stake. Then the offer is compared against something. And you can tell whether you are changing your mind — which is fine — or only wanting the thing.
:::

## Doing the arithmetic honestly

This is a tradeoff, not a test of character. Intensity buys real things: fast learning, being close to hardware that flies, and packing into a few years experience that can take a decade elsewhere. It costs real things too. Neither the person who takes the deal nor the one who turns it down is making a mistake. Treating it as a test of seriousness is how people end up somewhere that does not suit them.

Answer three questions on paper, before you need the answers.

**What am I optimizing for, and over what period?** "Two years of the steepest learning I can get, accepting a thin life outside work, so that afterward I can work on flight software" is coherent. So is "interesting work and a life I can keep up forever, even if I get there slower." What is *not* coherent is a position with no **[[time horizon|time-horizon]]** — no stated length of time.

**What must I keep, whatever else goes?** Be concrete: a minimum of sleep, a relationship, a training schedule, a medical treatment, time with a child. This list turns vague unease into a question you can test.

**What would tell me it is not working, and by when?** Deciding the warning signs in advance makes them visible later. It is the same discipline as fixing a **[[pass criterion|pass-criterion]]** before a test campaign runs, applied to yourself.

## If you find a mismatch during the process

Suppose that partway through, you find the pace is not something you can accept. You have three honest moves.

**Say it.** A specific need stated early can sometimes be met, and asking costs nothing you had.

**Withdraw.** That is normal, and done promptly and politely it burns no bridge.

**Decide the tradeoff is worth it anyway** — eyes open, with a time horizon attached. That is a real choice, very different from drifting into it.

What is *not* honest is accepting while planning to renegotiate later. That is the previous lesson's mismatch, entered on purpose. It rarely works, and it lands you in the eight-month resignation with less standing than if you had said nothing.

::: example Two candidates, the same three questions
Both ask an engineer on the team about the same three areas. The answers are made-up illustrations of useful and useless answers, not reports of what any real team said.

**Candidate A asks them as a checklist.**

*"What is the work-life balance like here?"* This invites a generality. "Intense but manageable" — compared with what?

*"Do you work a lot of weekends?"* Yes or no carries no frequency and no shape.

*"Is the culture good?"* Nobody still working there can easily say no, so the answer is worthless.

**Candidate B asks about specific experience.**

*"What did your last three weeks actually look like — when did you get in, when did you leave, and was that typical?"*

Useful: "Two were normal — in around nine, out around seven. The third I was here until eleven three nights running, chasing a **[[regression|regression]]** in the guidance build before a test. That is the pattern: a few heavy weeks around milestones, normal ones otherwise." Useless: "It varies, but it is manageable."

Check it: nine to seven is about 10 hours; nine to eleven at night is about 14. Concrete enough to compare with the next engineer.

*"How often do you get a period like that, over a year?"*

Useful: "Four or five, clustered around vehicle milestones, lasting one to three weeks." Useless: "It depends on the program."

Check it: 4 periods of 1 week is 4 weeks; 5 periods of 3 weeks is 15 weeks. Out of 52, that is roughly 8 to 29 percent of the year — a range she can hold against her own limits.

*"When a deadline last slipped, what happened?"*

Useful — listen hardest here: who knew first, whether they said so early, what happened to them, whether scope or hours moved, and what the review looked for. Useless: "We do not really let things slip."

**What B has that A does not:** three calibrated data points she can compare with the next engineer's. Her questions were also easy to answer honestly: none asked the engineer to judge their own employer, only for facts about a recent period. That is the difference between a question that produces information and one that produces a position.
:::

::: example A decision record written before the offer exists
Written in week three of the process, before any outcome is known. Nobody else sees it.

**What I am optimizing for, and for how long.** The next two to three years: the steepest learning I can get on real flight-relevant GNC (guidance, navigation and control), close to hardware that flies. I accept a thin life outside work for that period, but not indefinitely. If I am still doing it at five years without having decided to, that is a failure of attention, not a choice.

**What I keep regardless.** Six hours of sleep as a floor, not an average. The Sunday call with my mother. Enough exercise that my back holds up. Two evenings a week already committed and not negotiable.

**What I will accept.** Cancelled plans. Poor cooking. Little reading that is not work. Several heavy weeks a year, and a deadline period when I am unavailable to everyone.

**What I want to know before I say yes.** What a normal week looks like for two engineers, not one. How many heavy periods a year, and how long. What happened the last time something slipped — especially whether the person who flagged it early was treated as helpful or as a problem. Whether anyone on the team has **[[caring responsibilities|caring-responsibilities]]**, and how that works in practice: the most reliable single test I know of whether stated flexibility is real.

**What would tell me it is not working, and by when.** If at six months I have learned nothing I could not have learned elsewhere, the trade is not paying. If heavy periods have been more than half the weeks, the frequency was described wrongly, and I should say so rather than absorb it. If I have dropped two of the four things on my keep list for more than a month, I am past my boundary, and I should treat that as data, not weakness.

**Why now.** In the week an offer arrives, I will not be able to tell wanting the job from having concluded it is right. I want something to compare against, written by someone with nothing at stake.

Sanity check against the first example: 8 to 29 percent heavy weeks sits well under this record's "more than half" line, so that team would pass this test.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
State, as a mechanism rather than as folklore, what a culture-fit question is testing — and say what follows for how you should answer one.
:::

::: answer
An organization knows the conditions it imposes — a pace, an amount of ambiguity, a way of handling disagreements, an expectation about who owns a problem that falls between teams. Each produces behavior that differs by person. A culture question asks what you reliably do when that condition shows up.

So you answer with specific past behavior under that condition, like every other answer in this module, not with self-description. "I thrive in fast-paced environments" cannot be checked and sounds the same from someone who does not thrive. A fast period you were really in, with what you did and what it cost, is evidence.
:::

::: check
Why is "what happened the last time a deadline slipped" more informative than either of the other two questions?
:::

::: answer
The other two describe a schedule; this one describes behavior under stress, which is what you want to learn. It shows who absorbed the slip, whether scope or people moved, whether anyone warned early and what happened to them, and whether the review looked for a cause or for someone to blame.

It is also hard to answer with generalities: specifics arrive or they do not, and missing specifics are data too. The other two questions set the baseline and frequency you need to read this one.
:::

::: check
Why does this lesson insist you write your criteria down before an offer exists, even though you will know more later?
:::

::: answer
Because the question changes shape when an offer arrives. *Do I want this?* becomes *am I going to turn this down?*, asked under a deadline, after months of effort, with others' expectations attached — and reasoning in that state tends to run backward from a conclusion already reached.

Criteria written while nothing is at stake come from someone with no stake in the outcome, and they turn the later decision into a comparison. They also let you tell changing your mind, which is legitimate, from wanting the thing, which is not a reason.
:::

::: check
How should you read employee reviews on a public site?
:::

::: answer
For recurring specifics about pace, not for a verdict. Reviews are self-selected in both directions — people write them when unusually angry or unusually pleased — so they are not a fair sample, and the overall rating is the least informative thing on the page.

What carries information is an ordinary detail that recurs independently across reviews written years apart: the shape of a typical week, what a program phase does to the schedule, what surprised people. Then triangulate it against what engineers tell you and against the posting. One fact confirmed by independent sources beats a dozen strong opinions.
:::

::: check
A candidate discovers partway through the process that the pace is not something she is willing to accept. What are her options, and which one is not available?
:::

::: answer
She can say so and ask whether her specific need can be accommodated; sometimes it can, and asking costs nothing she had. She can withdraw, which is normal if done promptly and politely. Or she can decide the tradeoff is worth it anyway, eyes open and with a time horizon — a real choice, unlike drifting into it.

What is not available is accepting while planning to renegotiate later. That is the previous lesson's mismatch entered on purpose, and it usually ends in the same resignation with less standing than saying nothing would have left her.
:::

::: check
Why does this lesson refuse to treat intensity as a test of seriousness?
:::

::: answer
Because it is a tradeoff with real value on both sides, and treating it as a character test spoils the decision. Intensity really buys a steeper learning curve, closeness to hardware that flies, and years of exposure packed into a short time. It really costs time, attention and a share of everything outside work.

Which side is right depends on what someone optimizes for and over what period; both answers are coherent. The failure is choosing without a horizon, so that a bargain made for two years quietly becomes the shape of a decade.
:::

## Summary

| Question or idea | What it establishes | Ask whom, or when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What did your last three weeks look like? | The baseline, without which nothing else can be read | Engineers, about themselves |
| How often does a heavy period happen? | The frequency that turns a stated condition into a lived one | Engineers, over a year |
| What happened the last time a deadline slipped? | Behavior under stress: who absorbed it, whether scope or people moved | Engineers; listen hardest here |
| Process, policy, timelines | What is formal rather than lived | The recruiter |
| Review sites | Recurring boring specifics, never the rating | Triangulate with engineers and the posting |
| Your decision record | What you optimize for, what you keep, what would tell you it is failing | Written before the offer exists |

Answer culture questions with specific past behavior, and use the round to gather evidence for your own decision, written down before the offer. The next lesson turns to the question that most clearly separates a candidate who has thought about the work from one who has not: why this company.

::: context job-offer What an offer actually is
A **job offer** is the company's formal "we want you," usually sent as a letter or email after the last interview. It states the job title, the pay, the start date and the main conditions. It almost always comes with a **deadline** — a date by which you must say yes or no, often only days or a week or two away. That deadline is part of why the offer week is such a bad time to start thinking: you are handed the biggest question of the whole process and a short clock at the same moment.
:::

::: context recruiter The person who runs the process
A **recruiter** is the company employee whose job is to find candidates and move them through the hiring process. They schedule the interviews, explain the steps, answer questions about pay and policy, and pass messages between you and the hiring team. They are usually not engineers, so they are the wrong person to ask what a normal week on the guidance team feels like. But they are exactly the right person to ask how the process works, how answers are used, and what the formal policies are.
:::

::: context crunch A word borrowed from video games
"Crunch time" is an old phrase for the tense last stretch before a deadline, and "crunch" is best known from the video-game industry, where teams often worked very long days in the weeks before a game shipped. The word spread to other kinds of engineering. In rocketry the deadline is often a physical event: a test firing, a vehicle shipping to the launch site, or a launch window that opens and closes on fixed dates set by orbits. A launch window does not move because the software is late, which is one reason crunch happens there.
:::

::: context post-mortem Looking back after something goes wrong
Engineering teams hold a review after a failure or a slipped deadline. Different teams call it a **post-mortem**, a retrospective or a failure review. The old phrase *post-mortem* is Latin for "after death" — it first meant a doctor's examination to find out why someone died. The question worth asking a team is what their reviews look for. A **blameless** review asks "what in our process let this happen?" A blaming one asks "who did this?" Teams that blame get fewer early warnings, because people learn to stay quiet.
:::

::: context self-selection Who bothers to write a review
Most employees never write a review. The ones who do are often the very unhappy and the very happy, so the reviews you see are not a fair sample of everyone who works there. Statisticians call this **self-selection**: the people chose themselves into the sample.

::: context sunk-cost Why spent effort pulls on you
Economists call money or effort that is already spent and cannot come back a **sunk cost**. The trap is letting it steer the next decision: "I have put four months into this process, so I should say yes." But those four months are gone whether you say yes or no. The only honest question is which future is better from here. People fall into this trap all the time — finishing a bad movie because they paid for the ticket. Writing your criteria down early is a defense against it.
:::

::: context time-horizon Every plan needs an end date
A **time horizon** is how far ahead a plan is meant to run. "Work very hard" has no horizon. "Work very hard for two years, then reassess" does. The horizon is what lets you check the plan later: at two years you can ask whether it paid what you expected. Without one, the plan can never be finished and never be judged — it just keeps going, which is how a two-year bargain turns into ten years nobody chose.
:::

::: context pass-criterion Deciding what "good" means before you look
In GNC work, a **Monte Carlo** campaign runs a simulation thousands of times with slightly different conditions each time. Before the runs start, engineers write down the **pass criterion** — for example, "at least 99 percent of landings end within a set distance of the target." Writing it first matters. If you wait until you have seen the results, it is tempting to pick a line that the results happen to pass. Your decision record is the same move: fix the line, then look at the evidence.

::: context regression When something that worked breaks
In software, a **regression** is a bug where something that used to work stops working after a change — the program has gone backward. A "regression in the guidance build" means a new version of the guidance software fails a test that an older version passed. Teams run large sets of automatic tests on every change to catch these, and chasing one down before a hardware test is a classic reason for a few late nights.
:::

::: context caring-responsibilities People who look after someone
**Caring responsibilities** means regularly looking after another person — a young child, a sick partner, an aging parent. Such a person cannot always stay late at short notice. If a team includes people like that and they are doing well, the team has found real ways to make its schedule flexible. If nobody on the team has such responsibilities, or everyone who did has left, that tells you something too. That is why the question works as a test of whether stated flexibility is real.
:::
