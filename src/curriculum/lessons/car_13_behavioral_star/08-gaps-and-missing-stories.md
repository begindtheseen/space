The previous lesson's list of red flags contains four behaviours and no biographical facts. Nothing on it is *had a gap*, *changed field*, *learned this outside a degree programme*, or *has not experienced one of the eight themes*. That distinction is the subject of this lesson, because those four things produce more anxiety than the actual red flags do, and the anxiety is what turns them into problems.

All four are handled by the same principle, which is easier to state than to do: say the true thing, early, in the smallest number of words, without apology and without overclaiming. What follows is what that looks like in each case, and why the alternatives fail.

## Part one: the theme you have no story for

You will be asked for a situation you have not been in. With eight themes and a history that is shorter than eight themes deep, this is close to inevitable, and it is not a failure state.

::: key
If you have no story for a theme, say so and offer the closest real experience rather than inventing one. Fabricated stories collapse under the standard three follow-up questions about specifics, and the collapse is far more damaging than the gap.
:::

### The three-part move

**Name the gap precisely.** Precision is the whole game here, because the narrower the true statement, the more of your real experience stays available. "I have not been in that situation" throws away everything. "I have never had to tell someone their work was not good enough — the closest I have come is telling a teammate that a result we were about to present was not one I could stand behind" keeps the part of your history that is relevant and is still completely true.

**Offer the nearest real experience, and say how it differs.** The difference is not a disclaimer; it is the interesting part, because naming what would be harder about the real case is itself evidence that you understand the real case. "The difference is that he was a peer and it was a student project, so I had no authority and no consequence to impose — what I had was an argument."

**Then reason about the real case.** Briefly, concretely, in terms of what you would do first. This half is what keeps the round productive rather than leaving the interviewer holding a dead question.

### Why invention fails

It is worth being specific about the mechanism rather than treating this as a rule about honesty, because the mechanism explains why invention fails so reliably even when the invented story is plausible.

A remembered story has detail that is *odd*: the calibration numbers you had been handed, the forty-minute verification case, the plot of residual against elevation angle. Nobody would invent those, and that is why they are convincing. An invented story has to generate detail live, and detail generated live is generated to be plausible, which makes it generic — round numbers, typical roles, the obvious complication. Then the follow-ups arrive: what exactly did you do, why that rather than the alternative, how do you know it worked. Three questions is this track's standard interrogation depth for a résumé line, and an invented story has to produce consistent new detail at each level while you also keep track of what you already said.

The asymmetry is what makes this an easy decision. The cost of the gap is that one theme goes unanswered by a story. The cost of being caught inventing is that everything else you said becomes unreliable, because the interviewer cannot tell which of your stories were real.

### This is not the same as "I do not know"

This track has two lessons on not knowing — recovering from a blank without bluffing in the screens module, and saying *I do not know, here is how I would find out* in the past-project module. The shape is similar and the content is different, and it is worth keeping them apart.

Not knowing is about a fact or a technique: the gap is in your knowledge, and the second half of the answer is how you would find the answer. Not having a story is about experience: the gap is in your history, which no amount of looking things up will close, and the second half of the answer is how you would approach the situation, not how you would research it. Offering to look something up in response to a behavioural question reads as a category error.

## Part two: gaps, career changes and a self-taught background

The same principle, applied to three facts about your history that are all completely legitimate and all commonly mishandled.

### A gap

State it in one factual sentence, say what you were doing if you are willing to, and move to what you did next. "I was out for nine months in 2023 for family reasons. I came back in January, and the first thing I did was rebuild my simulation work from scratch because I wanted to see whether I had lost anything — which is where the C++ rewrite came from."

Two things not to do. Do not over-explain: a long account invites the listener to weigh reasons rather than accept a fact. And do not apologise, because an apology asserts there was something wrong, which the interviewer had no basis for believing until you said it. You are also entitled to a boundary — "for personal reasons, and it is resolved" is a complete answer, and a normal one.

### A career change

The through-line is covered in this track's ninety-second narrative lesson, and the behavioural version adds one thing: how you talk about what you left.

The rule is that you can criticise work without criticising people. "The problems I was handed were all open-loop and the closed-loop ones were where I kept ending up" is a statement about work. "The company was badly run and nobody there wanted to do anything interesting" is a statement about people, and it produces a question the listener cannot answer: whether this is an account of the employer or of the candidate. That question is expensive out of all proportion to the satisfaction of having said it.

The strongest version names something you are moving *towards*, with evidence, rather than something you are escaping. A change described entirely as an escape leaves the listener with no way to tell whether the next job would become the next escape.

### A self-taught background

::: key
Present a self-taught background as evidence of drive with concrete artefacts, not as an apology or a claim of equivalence. *I taught myself estimation and built a validated MEKF; I am also completing an accredited degree because the basic qualification requires it* is a confident, accurate answer.
:::

Three positions are available and two of them fail.

**Apologising** invites doubt that was not there. It also makes the rest of the conversation harder, because every subsequent claim is now being weighed against a deficiency you introduced.

**Claiming equivalence** — that self-study is as good as a degree — fails for a concrete reason rather than a philosophical one: the basic qualification line is published, and the earlier modules in this track establish that it is a hard filter applied before an interview rather than a ranking signal applied during one. Arguing with it in the room is arguing with a document the interviewer can read, and it is not their filter to waive.

**Confidence plus accuracy** is the only position that survives. It has two halves: what you built, stated as evidence, and what you are doing about the credential, stated as a fact. The artefacts do the first half — a validated estimator with consistency tests, a 6-DOF simulation with a dispersion campaign, an orbit-determination fit to real data — which is precisely why the portfolio module insists that projects be defensible and verified rather than numerous. The second half is one sentence, and the earlier modules in this track lay out the actual routes.

The tone that carries this is neither defensive nor triumphant. You are describing a route, not litigating it.

::: warning Do not raise it three times
A candidate who is uncomfortable about a gap, a change of field or a missing credential will often mention it more than once — in the narrative, again unprompted mid-answer, and again at the end. Each repetition tells the listener that the candidate regards it as the most important fact about her, which is a much stronger claim than anything the fact itself supports. Say it once, clearly, at the point where it belongs, and then let the rest of the conversation be about the work.
:::

::: example A theme with no story, answered three ways
The question: *Tell me about a time you had to manage a conflict between two people on your team.*

The candidate has never led a team and has never mediated between two other people.

**Invented — and its collapse:**

"Sure. On one of my group projects there were two people who did not get on, and it was affecting the work, so I sat them both down separately to understand where each of them was coming from, and then brought them together to agree on a way forward. After that things were much better and we delivered on time."

*Interviewer:* "What was the actual disagreement about?"

*Candidate:* "I think it was partly a personality thing, and partly about how the work was divided."

*Interviewer:* "What did you propose to change about how it was divided?"

*Candidate:* "We ended up splitting the modules more clearly so they were not stepping on each other."

*Interviewer:* "Which modules?"

Nothing in the first answer is checkable and every follow-up has to be generated, so each one is more generic than the last. The candidate is now three levels into a story she does not have, and the only exits are worse than the entrance.

**Deflected:**

"I have not really had that come up, but I am sure I would handle it fine — I get on well with most people."

This answers nothing, and the reassurance at the end is a claim with no evidence in a round whose entire purpose is evidence. It also leaves the interviewer to spend another question rescuing the exchange.

**Honest and useful:**

"I have not mediated between two other people — I have never been in a position where that was mine to do. The closest I have is a disagreement I was one side of, on the club satellite project, where the two of us wanted different things three days before a demo. What I did there was ask him what he thought the demo was for, which turned out to be the actual disagreement, and then narrow the test until it was affordable for both of us.

If it were two other people, I think the part that transfers is finding what the disagreement is actually about, because in my case it was not what either of us had been arguing. The part that does not transfer is that I would not be inside it — I would have to get both accounts separately before I had any view, and I would be conscious that the first account I heard would frame the second. I have not had to do that, so I would want to ask someone who has."

**Why the third version works:** the gap is named narrowly and immediately, so nothing later has to be hedged. The real story is genuinely adjacent and is told with its own specifics. The candidate distinguishes what transfers from what does not, which is a harder and more useful piece of thinking than a mediation story would have been. And the last clause — that she would ask someone who has done it — is an accurate statement about how she would close the gap, which is the behavioural analogue of saying how you would find something out.
:::

::: example The self-taught background, three ways
The question: *I see you are doing a degree part-time. Tell me about how you got into GNC.*

**Apologetic:**

"Yeah, so I do not have the traditional background, unfortunately. I came at it the long way round and I am still finishing the degree, so I know I am behind where I should be. I have tried to make up for it with self-study — I have worked through a lot of the standard material, and I have done some projects, although obviously they are not the same as real industry experience."

Four separate concessions — *unfortunately*, *behind where I should be*, *make up for it*, *obviously not the same* — none of which was requested, and one outright falsehood in the last clause: personal projects are not the same as industry experience, but *obviously not the same* concedes far more than that. Every artefact she then mentions arrives pre-discounted.

**Claiming equivalence:**

"Honestly, I do not think the degree matters much. I have built more real GNC than most graduates ever will — I have a 6-DOF simulation with a full dispersion campaign, a multiplicative EKF with consistency testing, an orbit determination fit to real tracking data. The degree is a formality that filters out people who can actually do the work."

The evidence is strong and the frame ruins it. The basic qualification is published, it is a filter applied before anyone interviews her, and the person in the room did not write it and cannot waive it. Arguing about it here spends credibility on a decision nobody present is making, and the sentence about what most graduates will ever do is a claim about other people that carries no information about her.

**Confident and accurate:**

"I came in through estimation. I was doing motion control work and kept running into problems that were really state-estimation problems, so I taught myself the subject properly — Markley and Crassidis, then building rather than reading. The thing I would point at is a multiplicative EKF fusing gyro and star-tracker data, with a NEES and NIS consistency campaign rather than an error plot, because I wanted to know whether the filter was honest about its own covariance and not only whether it was accurate. It was not, the first time.

I am also completing an accredited degree, because the basic qualification requires it and that is a filter before anyone reads my projects. It finishes next spring. I would rather have arrived by the usual route, but the work I did getting here is the reason I am confident about the estimation side specifically, and it is all checkable — the repositories run from one command with a fixed seed."

**Why this version is the one that survives:** it states the route as a fact with no apology and no argument, it puts the artefact in the middle where the evidence belongs, it includes a detail only someone who did the work would offer — that the filter failed its first consistency test — and it names the credential position in one sentence, accurately, without disputing it. The last clause is the strongest part, because reproducibility turns every claim in the answer into something the listener could check rather than believe.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
Why does this lesson insist that the gap be named *narrowly* rather than admitted in general terms?
:::

::: answer
Because a broad admission throws away real experience that is still relevant. "I have not been in that situation" closes the subject; "I have never mediated between two other people — the closest is a disagreement I was one side of" closes only the part that is actually untrue of her, and keeps a real, specific story in play. Narrow statements are also more credible, because someone who has thought precisely about what she has and has not done sounds different from someone issuing a blanket denial. The precision costs one clause and preserves most of the answer.
:::

::: check
An interviewer asks for a time you worked under a regulatory or compliance constraint, and you never have. Sketch the answer in three moves.
:::

::: answer
Name the gap precisely: you have not worked under a formal compliance regime — no export-control reviews, no flight-safety sign-off. Offer the nearest real thing and say how it differs: perhaps a project where you had rules you did not set and could not change, such as the export and prior-employer constraints this track covers for portfolio work, where the constraint was real and the cost of getting it wrong was yours; the difference is that nobody audited you, so the discipline was self-imposed. Then reason about the real case: what you would want first is the actual text of the requirement rather than a summary of it, and the name of the person who signs off, because the failure mode you would worry about is discovering the constraint after the design is settled. That is three moves and about forty seconds.
:::

::: check
Why does an invented story tend to collapse under the follow-up questions rather than at the moment it is told?
:::

::: answer
Because the first layer of an invented story is prepared, or at least plausible on general knowledge, and the layers underneath it are not. Each follow-up demands detail that has to be generated live and stay consistent with everything already said, and live-generated detail is generic by construction — it is produced to be plausible rather than recalled, so it lacks the odd specificity that real memories carry. Meanwhile the true stories in the same conversation have that texture, and the contrast is the thing that becomes visible. The asymmetry of cost is why this is an easy decision: an unanswered theme costs one question, while being caught puts every other story you told in doubt.
:::

::: check
A candidate explains a two-year gap by describing a difficult period in detail. What is the risk, and what would you advise instead?
:::

::: answer
The risk is that a long explanation invites evaluation of the reasons, turning a fact that needed acknowledging into a subject the interviewer now has to form a view on — and it spends the answer's time on the part of your history you least want to be the focus. One factual sentence, a boundary if you want one, and a move to what you did next is enough: "I was out for two years for family reasons, which is resolved; I came back last spring, and the first thing I did was rebuild my simulation work." You are entitled to that boundary, and using it reads as composure rather than evasion.
:::

::: check
Why is claiming that self-study is equivalent to a degree described here as a concrete error rather than an arguable opinion?
:::

::: answer
Because of where the requirement sits in the process. The basic qualification is published and, as this track's earlier modules establish, functions as a hard filter applied before an interview rather than a ranking signal applied during one. The interviewer did not write it and cannot set it aside, so an argument about its merits is being made to the wrong audience at a moment when your evidence is being assessed. The accurate position costs nothing and keeps the conversation on the work: say what you built, say what you are doing about the credential, and let the artefacts carry the claim.
:::

::: check
A candidate leaving a job she disliked is asked why she is moving. Give a version that is honest without disparaging anyone.
:::

::: answer
Talk about the work rather than the people, and name what you are moving towards. "The work I was given was mostly open-loop hardware bring-up, and the closed-loop problems were the ones I kept gravitating to — I ended up owning the control side of the pointing stage because nobody else wanted the stability analysis. What I want next is that as the job rather than as the thing I do on top of the job, which is why I am looking at vehicle GNC." Honest, specific, and it contains no claim about anyone's competence or intentions. A version framed entirely as escape leaves the listener unable to tell whether the next role becomes the next escape; a version framed around a direction does not.
:::

## Summary

| Situation | The move | What fails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No story for a theme | Name the gap narrowly, offer the nearest real experience, say how it differs, reason about the real case | Inventing; deflecting with reassurance |
| A gap in your history | One factual sentence, a boundary if you want it, then what you did next | Over-explaining; apologising |
| A career change | Criticise the work, never the people; name what you are moving towards | An account framed entirely as escape |
| Self-taught background | Evidence plus artefacts, and the credential position stated as fact | Apologising; claiming equivalence; arguing with the filter |

The unifying rule is that no answer in this lesson requires you to claim anything you would later have to walk back. The next lesson takes a question that is not a story at all — the extended-hours expectation that postings state in plain language — and what an honest answer to it looks like.
