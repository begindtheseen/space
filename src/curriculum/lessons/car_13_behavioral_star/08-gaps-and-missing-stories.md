A new friend asks, "What's the scariest roller coaster you've been on?" You have never been on one. You could make one up — then they ask which park, how tall the drop was, and every answer gets harder. You could dodge: "Oh, I'm sure I'd love them." Or you could say, "Never been on a big one. The closest was the log flume at the county fair, and the drop scared me more than I expected." Only the last keeps the conversation going and stays true however many questions follow.

This lesson is that choice in an interview. The last lesson's red flags are four *behaviors*, not facts about your life. None of them is *had a gap*, *changed field*, *learned outside a degree program*, or *has not lived through one of the eight themes*. Those four cause more worry than the real flags — and the worry is what makes them problems.

All four follow one principle, easy to state and harder to do: **say the true thing, early, in as few words as possible, without apology and without overclaiming.**

## Part one: the theme you have no story for

With eight themes and a history that may not be eight themes deep, you will almost certainly be asked about a situation you have never been in. That is not a failure.

::: key
If you have no story for a theme, say so and offer the closest real experience rather than inventing one. Fabricated stories collapse under the standard three follow-up questions about specifics, and the collapse is far more damaging than the gap.
:::

### The three-part move

**1. Name the gap precisely.** The narrower your true statement, the more of your real experience stays in play. "I have not been in that situation" throws everything away. Compare: "I have never had to tell someone their work was not good enough — the closest I have come is telling a teammate that a result we were about to present was not one I could stand behind." Still true, and it keeps the relevant history.

**2. Offer the nearest real experience, and say how it differs.** The difference is not a disclaimer. Naming what would be harder about the real case shows you understand it. "The difference is that he was a peer and it was a student project, so I had no authority and no consequence to impose — what I had was an argument."

**3. Reason about the real case.** Briefly: what you would do first. This keeps the round useful instead of leaving a dead question.

### Why invention fails

"Be honest" is the rule, but the *mechanism* explains why invention fails so reliably, even when the story sounds plausible. A remembered story has detail that is *odd*: the calibration numbers you were handed, the forty-minute verification case, the plot of leftover error against how high the satellite sat above the horizon. Nobody would invent those, which is why they convince.

An invented story makes up its detail live, to sound likely, so it comes out **generic** — round numbers, typical roles, the obvious complication. Then the follow-ups arrive: what exactly did you do, why not the alternative, how do you know it worked. **[[Three questions deep|three-deep]]** is this track's standard depth for probing a résumé line. An invented story must produce new, consistent detail at each level, while you also remember everything you already said.

The costs are lopsided. The gap costs one theme. Being caught inventing makes every story you told unreliable.

### This is not the same as "I do not know"

This track teaches *I do not know, here is how I would find out* in its screens and past-project modules. The shape is similar; the content differs. **Not knowing** is about a fact or a technique. The gap is in your knowledge, and the second half of the answer is how you would *find* the answer. **Not having a story** is about experience. The gap is in your history, which looking things up cannot close, so the second half is how you would *approach* the situation. Offering to look something up answers the wrong kind of question.

## Part two: gaps, career changes and a self-taught background

The same principle applies to three facts about your history — all legitimate, all often handled badly.

### A gap

A **gap** is a stretch of time with no job or school on your record. State it in one factual sentence, say what you were doing if you are willing to, and move to what you did next. "I was out for nine months in 2023 for family reasons. I came back in January, and the first thing I did was rebuild my simulation work from scratch because I wanted to see whether I had lost anything — which is where the C++ rewrite came from."

**Do not over-explain:** a long account invites the listener to weigh your reasons instead of accepting a fact. **Do not apologize:** an apology announces that something was wrong, which nobody believed until you said it. You are also entitled to a boundary: "for personal reasons, and it is resolved" is a complete answer, and a normal one.

### A career change

The through-line — the thread from your old work to the new — is covered in this track's ninety-second narrative lesson. Here, the added question is how you talk about what you left.

The rule: **criticize work, never people.** "The problems I was handed were all [[open-loop|open-closed-loop]], and the closed-loop ones were where I kept ending up" is a statement about work. "The company was badly run and nobody there wanted to do anything interesting" is a statement about people. It leaves the listener wondering whether it describes the employer or the candidate — a doubt that costs far more than the satisfaction of saying it.

The strongest version names something you are moving **toward**, with evidence, rather than something you are escaping. A change told only as an escape gives the listener no way to know whether the next job would become the next escape.

### A self-taught background

**Self-taught** means you learned on your own — books, courses, projects — not through a degree program.

::: key
Present a self-taught background as evidence of drive with concrete artifacts, not as an apology or a claim of equivalence. *I taught myself estimation and built a validated MEKF; I am also completing an accredited degree because the basic qualification requires it* is a confident, accurate answer.
:::

An **artifact** here is a thing you made that someone else can inspect: code, a report, a plot. The **MEKF** (said "em-ee-kay-eff") is a *multiplicative extended Kalman filter* — a standard way to estimate which way a spacecraft is pointing; plain EKF (said "ee-kay-eff") is the extended Kalman filter it builds on. Three positions are available, and two of them fail.

**Apologizing** creates doubt that was not there, and every later claim gets weighed against the weakness you introduced.

**Claiming equivalence** — that self-study is as good as a degree — fails for a concrete reason. The **[[basic qualification|basic-qualification]]** line is published in the job posting. Earlier modules in this track show it is a hard filter applied before an interview, not a ranking signal applied during one. Arguing with it in the room is arguing with a document — one the interviewer cannot waive.

**Confidence plus accuracy** is the only position that survives. It has two halves: what you built, stated as evidence, and what you are doing about the credential, stated as fact. The artifacts carry the first half — a validated estimator with consistency tests, a **[[6-DOF|six-dof]]** simulation with a dispersion campaign, an orbit determination fitted to real data. That is why the portfolio module insists projects be verified, not numerous. The second half is one sentence; the earlier modules lay out the real routes to an **[[accredited|accredited]]** degree.

The tone is neither defensive nor triumphant: you are describing a route, not arguing a case.

::: warning Do not raise it three times
A candidate uneasy about a gap, a field change or a missing credential often mentions it in her opening, again mid-answer, and again at the end. Each repeat says she thinks it is the most important fact about her — a far bigger claim than the fact supports. Say it once, where it belongs, and let the rest be about the work.
:::

::: example A theme with no story, answered three ways
The question: *Tell me about a time you had to manage a conflict between two people on your team.*

The candidate has never led a team and has never mediated between two other people.

**Invented — and its collapse:**

"Sure. On one of my group projects there were two people who did not get on, and it was affecting the work, so I sat them both down separately to understand where each of them was coming from, and then brought them together to agree on a way forward. After that things were much better and we delivered on time."

*Interviewer:* "What was the actual disagreement about?"

*Candidate:* "I think it was partly a personality thing, and partly about how the work was divided."

*Interviewer:* "What did you propose to change about how it was divided?"

*Candidate:* "We ended up splitting the modules more clearly so they were not stepping on each other."

*Interviewer:* "Which modules?"

Nothing in the first answer can be checked, and each follow-up is made up on the spot, more generic than the last. She is three levels into a story she does not have, and every exit is worse than the entrance.

**Deflected:**

"I have not really had that come up, but I am sure I would handle it fine — I get on well with most people."

This answers nothing. The reassurance is a claim with no evidence in a round whose purpose is evidence, and the interviewer must spend another question rescuing the exchange.

**Honest and useful:**

"I have not mediated between two other people — I have never been in a position where that was mine to do. The closest I have is a disagreement I was one side of, on the club satellite project, where the two of us wanted different things three days before a demo. What I did there was ask him what he thought the demo was for, which turned out to be the actual disagreement, and then narrow the test until it was affordable for both of us.

If it were two other people, I think the part that transfers is finding what the disagreement is actually about, because in my case it was not what either of us had been arguing. The part that does not transfer is that I would not be inside it — I would have to get both accounts separately before I had any view, and I would be conscious that the first account I heard would frame the second. I have not had to do that, so I would want to ask someone who has."

**Why the third version works.** The gap is named narrowly and at once, so nothing later needs hedging. The nearby story comes with its own specifics. She separates what transfers from what does not — more useful thinking than a mediation story would show. And the last clause, asking someone who has done it, is how she would really close the gap: the behavioral version of saying how you would find something out.
:::

::: example The self-taught background, three ways
The question: *I see you are doing a degree part-time. Tell me about how you got into GNC.*

**Apologetic:**

"Yeah, so I do not have the traditional background, unfortunately. I came at it the long way round and I am still finishing the degree, so I know I am behind where I should be. I have tried to make up for it with self-study — I have worked through a lot of the standard material, and I have done some projects, although obviously they are not the same as real industry experience."

Four concessions nobody asked for: *unfortunately*, *behind where I should be*, *make up for it*, *obviously not the same*. The last overshoots: personal projects really are not industry experience, but *obviously* concedes far more, as if they were worth nothing. Every artifact she mentions now arrives discounted.

**Claiming equivalence:**

"Honestly, I do not think the degree matters much. I have built more real GNC than most graduates ever will — I have a 6-DOF simulation with a full dispersion campaign, a multiplicative EKF with consistency testing, an orbit determination fit to real tracking data. The degree is a formality that filters out people who can actually do the work."

The evidence is strong; the frame ruins it. The basic qualification is published, applied before anyone interviews her, and the person in the room cannot waive it. Arguing here spends credibility on a decision nobody present is making. And the line about most graduates is a claim about other people, which says nothing about her.

**Confident and accurate:**

"I came in through estimation. I was doing motion control work and kept running into problems that were really state-estimation problems, so I taught myself the subject properly — [[Markley and Crassidis|markley-crassidis]], then building rather than reading. The thing I would point at is a multiplicative EKF fusing gyro and [[star-tracker|star-tracker]] data, with a [[NEES and NIS|nees-nis]] consistency campaign rather than an error plot, because I wanted to know whether the filter was honest about its own covariance and not only whether it was accurate. It was not, the first time.

I am also completing an accredited degree, because the basic qualification requires it and that is a filter before anyone reads my projects. It finishes next spring. I would rather have arrived by the usual route, but the work I did getting here is the reason I am confident about the estimation side specifically, and it is all checkable — the repositories run from one command with a [[fixed seed|fixed-seed]]."

**Why this version survives.** The route is a fact, with no apology and no argument. The artifact sits in the middle, where evidence belongs. One detail only the builder would offer: the filter failed its first consistency test. The credential gets one accurate sentence, undisputed. And the last clause is strongest: one command with a fixed seed turns every claim into something the listener could check rather than believe.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
Why does this lesson insist the gap be named *narrowly* rather than admitted in general terms?
:::

::: answer
A broad admission throws away real experience. "I have not been in that situation" closes the subject. "I have never mediated between two other people — the closest is a disagreement I was one side of" closes only the part that is actually untrue, and keeps a real, specific story in play. The precision costs one clause, saves most of the answer, and sounds more believable than a blanket denial.
:::

::: check
An interviewer asks for a time you worked under a regulatory or compliance constraint, and you never have. Sketch the answer in three moves.
:::

::: answer
First, name the gap precisely: you have not worked under a formal compliance system — no export-control reviews, no flight-safety sign-off. Second, offer the nearest real thing and say how it differs. Perhaps a project with rules you did not set and could not change, such as the export and prior-employer limits this track covers for portfolio work; the difference is that nobody audited you, so the discipline was self-imposed. Third, reason about the real case: you would want the actual text of the requirement, not a summary, and the name of whoever signs off, because the failure to fear is discovering the constraint after the design is settled. Three moves, about forty seconds.
:::

::: check
Why does an invented story tend to collapse under the follow-up questions, rather than at the moment it is told?
:::

::: answer
Because the first layer is prepared, or at least plausible from general knowledge, and the layers underneath are not. Each follow-up demands new detail, made up live and consistent with everything said so far. Such detail is generic, lacking the odd specifics of real memory, and your true stories make the contrast visible. The costs are lopsided: an unanswered theme costs one question, while being caught puts every other story in doubt.
:::

::: check
A candidate explains a two-year gap by describing a difficult period in detail. What is the risk, and what would you advise instead?
:::

::: answer
A long explanation invites the interviewer to judge the reasons, turning a fact that only needed acknowledging into a subject to form a view on — and it spends the answer on the part of your history you least want in focus. Advise one factual sentence, a boundary if wanted, and a move to what came next: "I was out for two years for family reasons, which is resolved; I came back last spring, and the first thing I did was rebuild my simulation work." Using that boundary reads as composure, not evasion.
:::

::: check
Why is claiming that self-study equals a degree called a concrete error here, not an arguable opinion?
:::

::: answer
Because of where the requirement sits: it is published and applied as a hard filter before the interview. The interviewer cannot set it aside, so arguing its merits goes to the wrong audience, just when your evidence is being assessed. The accurate position keeps the talk on the work: what you built, what you are doing about the credential, and artifacts that carry the claim.
:::

::: check
A candidate leaving a job she disliked is asked why she is moving. Give a version that is honest without putting anyone down.
:::

::: answer
Talk about work, not people, and name a direction. "The work I was given was mostly open-loop hardware bring-up, and the closed-loop problems were the ones I kept gravitating to — I ended up owning the control side of the pointing stage because nobody else wanted the stability analysis. What I want next is that as the job rather than as the thing I do on top of the job, which is why I am looking at vehicle GNC." Honest, specific, and silent about anyone's skill or intentions. Told only as escape, it would leave the listener wondering whether the next role becomes the next escape; a direction removes that doubt.
:::

## Summary

| Situation | The move | What fails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No story for a theme | Name the gap narrowly, offer the nearest real experience, say how it differs, reason about the real case | Inventing; deflecting with reassurance |
| A gap in your history | One factual sentence, a boundary if you want it, then what you did next | Over-explaining; apologizing |
| A career change | Criticize the work, never the people; name what you are moving toward | An account told only as escape |
| Self-taught background | Evidence plus artifacts, and the credential position stated as fact | Apologizing; claiming equivalence; arguing with the filter |
| Any of these | Say it once, where it belongs | Raising it three times |

The thread through all of it: no answer here asks you to claim anything you would later take back. The next lesson takes a question that is not a story at all — the extended-hours expectation that job postings state in plain words — and what an honest answer to it looks like.

::: context open-closed-loop Steering with your eyes shut
An **open-loop** system follows a plan without checking the result — like a toaster that runs for two minutes whether the bread is pale or burning. A **closed-loop** system measures what is happening and corrects — like a thermostat that switches the heater off once the room is warm. The "loop" is the path the measurement takes back to the controller.

::: context basic-qualification The line the posting draws in advance
Many engineering job postings split requirements into two lists. **Basic qualifications** are the minimum: without them, an application usually does not go forward, however good the rest looks. **Preferred** skills are the extras that make a candidate stronger once past that line. For many engineering roles the basic list includes a degree in an engineering or science field. That is why an interviewer cannot trade your projects for the degree: the check happened earlier, by a rule written before anyone met you. Read the basic list of any posting you apply to word for word.

::: context six-dof Six ways to move, again
"6-DOF" is said "six dee-oh-eff": six degrees of freedom. A vehicle can move three ways (up-down, left-right, forward-back) and turn three ways (pitch, yaw and roll). A 6-DOF simulation tracks all six, so it can show whether the control system keeps the vehicle pointed correctly, not only where it goes. A **dispersion campaign** runs that simulation hundreds or thousands of times with the uncertain inputs nudged randomly, to count how many runs pass. Both came up in lesson 1 of this module.
:::

::: context accredited Who checks the degree
An **accredited** degree is one from a program that an outside body has inspected and approved. In the United States, engineering programs are usually accredited by ABET (said "AY-bet"), which reviews what is taught, how students are assessed, and whether graduates meet set outcomes. Employers use accreditation as a shortcut: rather than judging each school themselves, they trust the inspection. It is one reason a self-taught engineer, however capable, still meets a credential requirement she has to address directly.
:::

::: context markley-crassidis The book on the shelf
*Fundamentals of Spacecraft Attitude Determination and Control*, by F. Landis Markley and John L. Crassidis, is a standard graduate textbook on how a spacecraft works out which way it is pointing and how it turns to where it should. Naming the actual book, rather than "the standard material", is a small example of this module's rule: a specific is checkable. An interviewer who knows the book can ask about a chapter, and the candidate who really read it can answer.
:::

::: context star-tracker A camera that reads the sky
A star tracker is a small camera on a spacecraft that photographs the stars, matches the pattern against a catalog stored onboard, and works out which way the spacecraft is facing — very precisely. A **gyro** (short for gyroscope) measures how fast the spacecraft is turning. The two make a good pair. The gyro reports smoothly and often but slowly drifts. The star tracker is accurate but updates less often and can be blinded by the Sun. A Kalman filter blends them, keeping the gyro's smoothness and the star tracker's accuracy.
:::

::: context nees-nis Tests that ask the filter whether it is honest
A Kalman filter reports two things: its best estimate, and how unsure it is (its **covariance**). An error plot only shows accuracy. The consistency tests check the second claim. **NEES** (said "knees"), the normalized estimation error squared, compares the true error with the filter's stated uncertainty — possible in simulation, where the truth is known. **NIS** (said "niss"), the normalized innovation squared, does the same using only the measurements, so it works on real data. If either comes out much too large, the filter is overconfident. Lesson 1 of this module shows the NEES band in a picture.
:::

::: context fixed-seed Making random runs repeatable
A computer's "random" numbers come from a formula started by a number called the **seed**. Give it the same seed, and it produces exactly the same sequence of "random" numbers every time. So a dispersion campaign run with a fixed seed can be repeated exactly — by you next year, or by an interviewer tonight — and every plot comes out identical. Without a fixed seed, a claimed result can never be exactly reproduced, only roughly re-approximated. That is why "runs from one command with a fixed seed" is such a strong closing line.
:::
