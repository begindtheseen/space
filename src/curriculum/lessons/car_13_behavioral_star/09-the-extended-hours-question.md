Picture a summer job at the town pool. The flyer on the door says, in plain words: *must be able to work weekends in July.* At the interview, the manager asks, "Can you do weekends?" You could say, "Sure, whatever you need!" — even though your family's trip is the second weekend in July. Or you could say, "Yes, except July 12 and 13, which I've had planned since spring. I worked every Saturday at the car wash last summer, so I know what that's like." The first gets you the job and a problem. The second gets you a job that works.

This lesson is the grown-up version of that flyer. Every other behavioral question asks about your past. This one asks about your future, and the employer has already published their side. Job postings state the expectation **[[verbatim|verbatim]]** — word for word: the candidate must be willing to work extended hours and weekends when needed to meet critical deadlines.

So the question in the room is neither a test of enthusiasm nor a trick. It is a disclosed **[[condition of employment|condition-of-employment]]** — a term of the job, stated up front. The question is whether you accept it — asked of someone who has read it, so an evasive answer dodges something both sides can see.

::: key
Postings state the extended-hours expectation verbatim, so it is a real question about a real condition. Answer with a true example of when you did it and what it cost you. A performative yes you do not mean becomes a resignation in eight months.
:::

Below: what an honest answer contains, why the fake yes is a bad trade for *you*, how to state a real limit without negotiating, and what this module does not know about how often the condition bites.

## Why a published condition changes the question

Two things follow from the line being published, not sprung on you.

**First, enthusiasm is not the currency.** When a condition is stated openly, the useful information is whether you can live with it. Reassurance tells the interviewer nothing about that, because everyone can say yes. What separates answers is evidence: have you done this, under what circumstances, and what happened to you and to your work?

**Second, the balance of power in the exchange is less lopsided than it feels.** You have been told. That makes it fair — expected, even — to have thought about it, to hold a position, and to ask your own questions about how it works.

## What an honest answer contains

Four parts, under a minute in total.

1. **Your position, stated plainly.** One sentence. Not a philosophy of work, not a negotiation — where you actually stand.
2. **A specific instance.** When you did it, what it was for, and what it produced. A concrete week beats a promise, for the same reason a number beats an adjective in a Result: it can be checked, and only someone who has been there can say it.
3. **What it cost.** This is the part candidates cut — and cutting it is what makes the rest sound fake. Long hours have costs: tiredness, mistakes, the thing you dropped, the people you canceled on. Someone who names none has either not done it or is not saying. Naming the cost shows you are agreeing to something you understand, not to a phrase.
4. **Your real boundary, if you have one.** Stated once, as a fact, without drama or apology. If you do not have one, do not invent one to sound balanced.

## Why the fake yes is a bad trade

A **[[performative|performative]]** yes is one said for effect — to look good — rather than because it is true. This module is direct about it: a yes you do not mean becomes a resignation in eight months, which is worse for both sides than an honest answer.

- **You hold information the other side does not:** what you are really willing to do, what your obligations are, and what happened the last time you worked a month of long weeks.
- **If you misrepresent it, you win nothing that lasts.** You buy your way into an arrangement you already know you will want out of — at the price of moving again, a short stint on your record, and a lost reference.
- **The benefit you were protecting — not being ruled out on one answer — is only a guess.** This module does not say how any answer is weighed, and nobody you meet before the offer will either.

There is a second, more practical reason. A yes with no example is weak evidence *even when it is sincere*. If you have worked long hours and can say when, and what it cost, that answer is both more honest and stronger. If you have not, saying so, with a plan, is more believable than a confident claim about coping with something you have never done.

## A boundary: constraint or preference?

If something in your life is fixed, say it; both sides are better off knowing. The distinction to watch is between a **[[constraint and a preference|constraint-vs-preference]]**.

- A **constraint** is an obligation you cannot move: caring for a family member, a standing medical commitment, a visa or residency requirement, a custody arrangement.
- A **preference** is something you would *rather* have: not working Sundays as a rule, protecting your evenings, keeping a hobby.

**State constraints plainly, once, and pair each one with the flexibility you do have.** That pairing is what makes it information rather than an opening bid. "Tuesday and Thursday evenings are fixed for me because of a caring responsibility; outside those, my schedule is genuinely open, including weekends." The second half is as useful as the first.

**Preferences need a harder test: would you actually enforce it?** A preference you would drop in the first busy month is not worth raising. A preference you would *not* drop is really a constraint, and you should treat it as one — including in your own decision about whether to take the job, which is the next lesson's subject.

::: warning Three shapes that fail, for three different reasons
**The unconditional yes with no example.** "Absolutely, whatever it takes, I have no commitments" cannot be tested, matches everyone else's answer, and — if it is not true — is a promise your own conscience will hold you to long after the interview.

**The refusal to engage.** Declining to discuss hours treats a published condition as an improper question. It is not improper. It is what the posting says.

**The principled lecture.** Answering with a general view on work-life balance — that a well-run project should never need a weekend — answers a question about you with an opinion about organizations. Whatever its merits, it does not respond, and it sounds like an argument with the posting instead of a decision about it.
:::

## What this module does not tell you

How often the condition actually bites. Whether it comes in phases or is constant. Which teams, which programs, which parts of the year. How a given answer is weighed, by whom, or whether it is weighed at all.

This lesson will not make that up. It varies. The people who can tell you are the recruiter — whose screen this track covers as the place to ask process questions — and the engineers you meet later, who can tell you what their own last three months looked like. A **[[review site|review-sites]]** can hint at patterns, but it is not a measurement. The next lesson is about turning those answers into your own decision, and lesson 12 is about asking well.

What you *can* do in advance is decide what you are actually willing to do, so the answer you give is the one you would give either way.

::: example The same question, four answers
The question: *This role involves working extended hours and weekends when needed to meet critical deadlines. How do you feel about that?*

**The performative yes:**

"Absolutely, no problem at all. I am completely flexible, I have no real commitments outside work, and honestly I would rather be busy. Whatever hours the job needs, I am there."

It answers, and says nothing: no instance, no cost, no boundary. *Whatever hours the job needs* is a limitless promise nobody asked for. If it is not true, it is a mismatch already scheduled.

**The lecture:**

"I think if a team is planning properly it should not need to routinely work weekends, and in my experience crunch is usually a symptom of poor estimation rather than of genuinely hard problems. I am happy to work hard, but I would want to understand why the deadline slipped in the first place."

There is a reasonable argument in here, made to the wrong person at the wrong moment. The question was whether she is willing. The answer is an opinion about whether the condition should exist. (**[[Crunch|crunch]]** is industry slang for a stretch of very long hours before a deadline.)

**The hedge:**

"Yeah, I mean, I can be flexible when it is needed. Obviously it depends a bit on the situation, but broadly I am fine with it."

Nothing has been said. *Depends a bit on the situation* is what an unwilling person says without admitting it, and that is how it will be heard — with no example to change the reading.

**The honest answer:**

"I have read that line and I have thought about it. My position is that I am willing, and I would rather tell you what that has looked like for me than assert it.

The clearest instance was the two weeks before our launch-vehicle [[design review|design-review]]. I was running the dispersion campaign, the configuration froze four days later than planned, and I worked about eleven days straight, including both weekends, to get the full five hundred cases run and the results written up. It was worth doing — that campaign is where the [[wind-shear case that broke my gain schedule|wind-shear]] turned up, and we would not have found it in the reduced run.

What it cost: I made a mistake at the end of it. On day nine I re-ran a batch with the old wind model because I did not check which configuration file I had loaded, and I lost most of a day. That is the thing I actually take from it — I am willing to work those hours, and I know that past about day seven I need to put more checks in front of myself, so I now write down what I am about to run before I run it.

I do not have a hard constraint on evenings or weekends. What I would want to understand from you is what the normal week looks like when there is not a deadline, and how often this kind of period happens, because I would rather know that now than find out in month four."

**What the fourth version does, step by step.** It states a position in one sentence. It gives a specific instance with dates, hours and an outcome. It names a real cost that is unflattering and specific — a [[tired-hours mistake|fatigue-errors]] and the habit it produced. And it closes with a question of her own, which is fair precisely because the condition was published. Nothing needs taking back, and she would say it the same way to a friend.
:::

::: example A real constraint, stated well
The candidate has a caring responsibility two fixed evenings a week. It cannot be moved. Otherwise she is entirely flexible.

**The version that over-negotiates:**

"I should say up front that I do have some constraints. Tuesdays and Thursdays are completely out for me, and I would also want to protect my weekends where possible — I do a lot of climbing, and I have found I really need that time to stay sharp. I am flexible otherwise, but I did want to be transparent about all of that early on."

Two problems. A real constraint is bundled with a preference, so it sounds like a list of demands, not a fact about availability. And *protect my weekends where possible* is the softest version of exactly what the posting named, arriving beside a hard constraint and blurring both.

**The version that works:**

"One fixed thing: Tuesday and Thursday evenings are committed — I have a caring responsibility and that is not movable. Outside those two evenings my schedule is genuinely open, including weekends, and in a crunch period I would expect to be working like everyone else.

For what it is worth, I have worked that way before. When my club satellite team was integrating for a demo, I did three consecutive weekends and shifted my two evenings to early mornings for that period, which worked because I planned it in advance rather than discovering it on the Friday. What I cannot do is find out at four on a Tuesday that tonight is a late one — that is the part that needs notice rather than willingness."

**Why this works.** The constraint comes first, in one sentence, with just enough reason to be believable and no more. It is paired at once with how flexible she is, so the listener gets the complete picture, not an opening bid. The instance shows she has already made a version of this work. And the last sentence turns the constraint into an operating requirement — *notice*, not an exemption. That is far easier for a team to plan around.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
Why does this lesson treat an unconditional yes as a weak answer, even when the candidate truly means it?
:::

::: answer
Because sincerity is not evidence, and reassurance does not set you apart. Everyone in the process can say yes, so an answer made only of willingness says nothing about whether *this* person can live with the condition. An instance does: when you did it, what for, what it produced, and what it cost you. The unconditional version has a second problem: *whatever hours the job needs* is a limitless promise nobody asked for, which you cannot actually keep and which will sit badly with you later. The stronger answer is narrower: I am willing, here is what that has looked like, here is what it costs me.
:::

::: check
This module says a performative yes becomes a resignation in eight months. Rebuild the argument for why that is worse for the candidate than an honest answer the interviewer might not have hoped for.
:::

::: answer
Because the benefit is a guess and the cost is real. The benefit is not being ruled out on one answer — a guess, since this module does not say how any answer is weighed and nobody you meet before the offer will tell you. The cost, if the mismatch is real, is paid in full: the effort of moving again, a short stint on your record that needs explaining, the reference you did not get, and months doing a job on terms you had already decided you would not accept. And you were the only person who could have prevented it, because only you held the information about what you are really willing to do.
:::

::: check
Tell a constraint from a preference, and say why the difference changes how you handle each.
:::

::: answer
A constraint is an obligation you cannot move — a caring responsibility, a fixed medical commitment, a custody arrangement. A preference is something you would rather have. State constraints plainly and once, paired with the flexibility you do have, because they are facts the job has to fit around and both sides are better off knowing. Put preferences through a harder test: would you actually enforce it? One you would drop in the first busy month is not worth raising, and it clutters a real constraint if bundled with it. One you would never drop is a constraint in practice — and the place to take it seriously is your own decision about whether the job suits you.
:::

::: check
A candidate has never worked a long stretch of extended hours. What is the honest version of her answer?
:::

::: answer
Say so, narrowly, then say what you do know about yourself, with whatever evidence you have. "I have not had a month like that in a job. The closest is exam-period weeks and the two weeks before our competition, where I was doing long days for about ten days — what I learned is that my error rate climbs noticeably after about a week, so I put checks in front of myself rather than relying on being careful." That is true, specific and useful. What does not work is a confident claim about handling something you have never done: it cannot be tested now, and it will be tested at once later.
:::

::: check
Why does naming a cost — a mistake you made, something you dropped — make the answer stronger rather than weaker?
:::

::: answer
Because it shows you are agreeing to something you understand. Long hours have costs; someone who names none has either not worked them or is not saying, and both readings are worse than the cost itself. The cost also turns a claim into self-knowledge with a practical result. Knowing that your error rate climbs after a week, so you put a written check in front of yourself, is a statement about how you work under pressure — exactly what the round is for. It is the same principle as the failure story: the honest, unflattering specific is what makes the rest believable.
:::

::: check
You want to know how often extended hours actually happen on the team you would join, and you realize neither you nor this module can say. What can you legitimately do with that?
:::

::: answer
Turn it into a question you ask, not an assumption you make. The frequency, the phases it clusters around, and what a normal week looks like are not stated anywhere in this course. Inventing a figure — or treating one from a review site as fact — would be worse than asking. The people who can answer are the recruiter, for process questions, and the engineers you meet, for what their own last three months looked like. Asking is fair here in a way it would not be for a hidden condition: the expectation was published, so a follow-up is a reasonable request for detail about a term you have already been told applies.
:::

## Summary

| Part of the answer | What it contains | Why it is there |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Position | One sentence, plainly | The question is whether you accept a published condition |
| Instance | When, what for, what it produced | Evidence beats reassurance |
| Cost | Something specific and unflattering | Shows you understand what you are agreeing to |
| Boundary | A real constraint, once, paired with your flexibility | A fact the job must fit around |
| Your question | Normal week, how often, how much notice | Fair because the condition was published |

What varies, and is not stated anywhere in this module: how often the condition bites, on which teams, and how an answer is weighed. Ask the recruiter, and ask the engineers. The next lesson takes what they tell you and turns it into the decision you are making alongside theirs.

::: context verbatim Word for word
*Verbatim* is Latin for "word for word." When this module says postings state the expectation verbatim, it means the sentence is printed in the job posting itself, in those exact words, not paraphrased by a recruiter or guessed from reviews. That matters for how you answer. A paraphrase can be softened or misremembered; printed words cannot. So there is no point hoping the interviewer does not mean it — they are reading from the same page you are. The honest move is to treat it the way you would treat any other written requirement: read it closely, and decide.
:::

::: context condition-of-employment Terms written into the job
A **condition of employment** is something you agree to as part of taking the job: where you work, whether you need a security check, whether you must be able to travel, and here, whether you will work long hours when deadlines demand it. Some conditions are legal requirements, and some are the employer's choice. What they share is that they are stated before you accept, so accepting the job means accepting them. That is why this question is not a trap. It is the employer making sure you have noticed one of the terms.
:::

::: context performative Said for the audience
In ordinary speech, a **performative** statement is one made to create an impression — like a player who dives dramatically for the referee. A performative yes to the hours question is aimed at the interviewer's approval, not at the truth. The interviewer cannot see the difference in the moment. The difference shows up months later, when the words meet an actual Saturday.

::: context review-sites Reading reviews without being fooled
Sites like Glassdoor let employees post anonymous reviews of their employers. They can be useful, but they are self-selected: the people who bother to write tend to be the very happy and the very unhappy, and the middle mostly stays quiet. So a review site is not a survey. Read it for *recurring specifics* — the same team, the same phase of a program, the same kind of week described by several people — rather than for an overall verdict or a star rating.
:::

::: context crunch Where the word comes from
**Crunch** is slang for a period of very long hours before a deadline. The word is best known from the video game industry, where "crunch time" before a release became notorious, but it is used across engineering. Aerospace has its own versions: the weeks before a major design review, a launch campaign, or an anomaly investigation after something goes wrong. Knowing the word helps you ask sharper questions — "how often does crunch happen, and what triggers it?" is a normal thing to ask an engineer.
:::

::: context design-review Checkpoints on the way to flight
A **design review** is a formal meeting where a design is presented to experienced engineers, who look for problems before the project moves on. Big aerospace programs have a chain of them. NASA's, for example, include the Preliminary Design Review (PDR, said "P-D-R") and the Critical Design Review (CDR, "C-D-R").

::: context wind-shear When the wind changes with height
**Wind shear** is a sudden change in wind speed or direction as altitude changes — the air at 10 km blowing very differently from the air at 9 km. A rising rocket crosses those layers in seconds, so the push on it swings quickly. A **gain schedule** is a control system's plan for changing its own settings (its gains) as the flight condition changes, since the right response at liftoff is not right at high speed. A shear case that "breaks" a gain schedule is one where the planned settings cannot keep the vehicle steady. Finding it in simulation, not in flight, is the whole point of a dispersion campaign.
:::

::: context fatigue-errors Why tired engineers add checks
People working long stretches make more mistakes, and — the dangerous part — they are often poor judges of how tired they are. That is well established in general, though how fast it happens varies from person to person, which is why the candidates in this lesson describe *their own* pattern ("past about day seven", "after about a week") rather than quoting a rule. Engineering teams guard against tired mistakes the same way pilots do: checklists, a second person reviewing a command before it is sent, and writing down what you are about to do before you do it. The candidate's new habit is a one-person checklist.
:::
