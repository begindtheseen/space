*Tell me about a time you disagreed with a colleague* is the theme where the obstacle in the story is a person, and that changes what a good answer looks like. In every other theme the difficulty is a system, a deadline or your own mistake, and you can describe it flatly. Here the difficulty has a point of view, and the way you represent that point of view is most of what the answer conveys.

The characteristic failure is to make the other person the villain — not usually by insulting them, which almost nobody does, but structurally, by writing them into the story as an obstacle to your being right. The tell is that their position is stated only well enough to be dismissed. "He wanted to skip the verification because he thought it was a waste of time" is a villain sentence: it gives a position with no reason behind it, which no competent person would hold, so either the colleague was not competent or the account is not complete.

This matters for a mechanical reason, not a moral one. The listener has exactly one source for the other person's view, and it is you. An account that presents their position at its strongest is the only kind that demonstrates you understood it — and understanding the counter-position is the thing being probed, because it is what makes someone possible to work with when the disagreement is real.

## What this theme can establish

Not whether you win arguments. A story where you were right and prevailed is the weakest common version, because it is indistinguishable from a story told by someone who cannot hear a counter-argument.

What the answer can show is narrower and more useful: that you can hold a technical position and change it on evidence rather than on pressure; that you can tell what kind of disagreement you are in; and that you can get to a decision without damaging the working relationship you will need next week. Those are three different skills, and a good story shows at least two of them.

## Telling what kind of disagreement you are in

Naming the axis is half the resolution, and it is the move that most often gets skipped.

**A disagreement about facts.** You believe the filter is inconsistent; your colleague believes the plots look fine. This kind is settleable, and the resolution is a measurement both of you agree in advance to accept. If you can propose that test — and say what result would change your own mind — the disagreement stops being about who is more confident.

**A disagreement about risk tolerance.** You both agree the campaign has a four percent infeasible fraction; you think that blocks the demo, they think it does not. No measurement settles this, because the disagreement is about an acceptance criterion that was never written down. The move is to surface the criterion: what would have to be true for this to be acceptable, and who gets to decide that?

**A disagreement about priorities.** Your colleague wants the demo ready Friday; you want the verification done. Both are legitimate, they are competing for the same time, and the tradeoff usually belongs to someone else. Recognising that early saves an argument that neither of you has the standing to settle, and *I realised this was not ours to decide, so I took it to the lead with both options costed* is a strong sentence in a story.

Mistaking one kind for another is what turns a twenty-minute conversation into a fortnight of friction. A risk-tolerance disagreement argued as if it were about facts produces two people presenting more and more evidence at each other, neither of whom is actually disputing the evidence.

::: key
A disagreement story is stronger when the other person's position is stated at its strongest, with the reason they held it. Name which kind of disagreement it was — facts, risk tolerance or priorities — because the resolution differs: a test settles facts, an explicit criterion settles risk, and priorities usually belong to whoever owns the tradeoff.
:::

## The shape of the story

Five parts, in this order, inside the usual ninety seconds.

1. **The two positions, both reasonable.** Theirs first, with its reason. Yours second.
2. **What was actually at stake.** Schedule, risk, scope, correctness — and which axis the disagreement sat on.
3. **What you did.** The moves are the Action section: restating their position back to them, going to find a piece of missing information, proposing a test, costing both options, escalating, conceding.
4. **How it resolved.** Who turned out to be right, what settled it, or how you proceeded without agreement.
5. **What happened afterwards.** One sentence. The relationship, and whether the resolution held.

Part five is short and does real work. A disagreement that ended in a decision but left a colleague unwilling to bring you the next problem was not resolved, it was won.

::: warning The story where the other person never speaks
If your account contains no sentence in which the other person makes a point you found reasonable, the story has a hole in it, and the hole is visible from outside. Two ways to fix it honestly. Either recover what their actual reason was — it existed, and if you cannot remember it you probably never asked, which is itself worth saying. Or, if their position genuinely was not reasonable, tell a different story: a disagreement with an unreasonable person demonstrates nothing except that you met one.
:::

## Disagreeing with a manager

The same structure, with one asymmetry: you can argue, and then they decide. A story that pretends otherwise reads as someone who has not worked in an organisation; a story where you never argued at all reads as someone who will not tell them when something is wrong.

The shape that works is advocacy, then commitment, and the separation between them said out loud.

**Advocate once, clearly, with evidence.** State the concern, its size, and what it would cost to address. Put a number on it if you can — the previous lessons' standard applies here too, and a concern with a magnitude attached is a different conversation from a concern expressed as discomfort.

**Ask what the decision rests on.** Not rhetorically. Managers usually have constraints you cannot see, and "what would have to be true for the campaign to be worth the extra two days?" often produces the actual reason, which may be one you agree with.

**Then commit or escalate, and do not do a third thing.** If you are overruled on a judgement call, commit properly: the decision was theirs to make, and continuing to relitigate it while half-executing is the failure mode worth naming. If the matter is one where you believe the risk is genuinely unacceptable — a safety issue, something you think is being hidden — that is what escalation exists for, and the right course depends on the organisation's own process rather than on anything this module can tell you.

What almost never plays well is the passive version: agreeing in the room, disagreeing afterwards to colleagues, and doing the work slowly. That is a story you would not tell in an interview, which is a reasonable test of whether it is a way to behave.

## The version where you were wrong

A conflict story in which you turn out to have been mistaken is often the strongest one available, for the same reason the failure story is strong: it is evidence you can be moved by an argument. It has to be a real technical position, sincerely held, changed by something specific — not a token concession.

The closing sentence matters. "She was right, and the thing that convinced me was the plot of the residual against elevation angle, which I had not looked at" is a much stronger ending than "we compromised", because it names the evidence and credits the person who produced it.

::: example One disagreement, two accounts
The event: the candidate and a teammate on a club satellite project disagreed about whether to run estimator consistency tests before a departmental demo three days away.

**The villain version:**

"On the club project I had a teammate who wanted to cut corners. We were coming up to the demo and he decided the consistency tests were not worth doing — he just wanted the thing to look good on the day. I pushed back because I did not think we should be showing results we had not checked, and eventually I stayed up and ran them myself. Two of the runs were outside the band, which proved my point. He was not thrilled about it but the numbers were the numbers."

Everything here may be true, and the account still fails. *Wanted to cut corners*, *decided they were not worth doing*, *just wanted it to look good* — three characterisations of the teammate's motives and no statement of his reason. *Which proved my point* frames the outcome as a score. And the last sentence leaves a colleague who is now less likely to raise a concern with this candidate again, which the story treats as a minor detail rather than as the cost it is.

**The same events, told straight:**

"Three days before a departmental demo, a teammate and I disagreed about whether to run the estimator consistency tests. His argument was that the demo was a communication exercise, not a review — the audience was going to look at an animation, the tests would take most of a day of compute and rework we did not have, and there was a real chance of finding something we then could not fix in time, which would leave us with nothing to show. That is a coherent position and it was not lazy.

Mine was that the demo was the first time anyone outside the team would see attitude numbers from our filter, and I did not want to put a covariance on a slide I had not tested. We were not actually disagreeing about the facts — we were disagreeing about what the demo was for, and neither of us had said that out loud until I asked him what he thought the audience would do with the numbers.

What broke it open was narrowing the test. Instead of the full campaign I proposed a hundred-run version of the consistency check overnight, which was affordable, and we agreed in advance that if it came back inside the band we would show the numbers, and if it did not we would show the animation and say the filter was still in validation. It came back with the average above the upper limit of the band, so that is what we did — we presented it as work in progress and said what we were checking next.

He was the one who suggested we put the consistency plot in the appendix and talk about it if anyone asked, which turned out to be the part two people in the audience were most interested in."

**What is different:** his position is stated with its reason and is genuinely defensible. The axis is named — this was not a factual disagreement but one about what the event was for. The Action contains a move that made the disagreement resolvable rather than a contest of conviction: narrow the test, agree the decision rule in advance. And the last line gives the colleague a contribution, which costs the candidate nothing and is the clearest possible evidence that the resolution left the relationship intact.
:::

::: example Overruled by a lead, told without bitterness and without spinelessness
The question: *Tell me about a time you disagreed with a decision your manager made.*

**The answer:**

"On a student launch project I owned the dispersion campaign, and our lead wanted to cut it from five hundred cases to a hundred to make a review date. I disagreed, and I said so once, with the reason: at a hundred cases the tail is thin enough that a failure mode showing up in about one percent of runs might appear zero times, and the whole point of the campaign was the tail. I costed it as well — the full run was about nine hours on our hardware, so the real question was whether we could start it a day earlier rather than whether to do it at all.

He said no, because the review slot was fixed, the vehicle configuration was still moving, and he expected to re-run everything after the design froze anyway — so spending our last compute day on a configuration that was about to change was the thing he was avoiding. That was a better reason than I had assumed, and it was based on schedule information I did not have.

So we ran the hundred cases, and I did two things rather than sulking. I wrote one line into the results summary saying the campaign was a hundred cases and that the tail was not resolved at that size, so nobody downstream would read it as a full campaign. And I set the full five-hundred-case run going the night the configuration froze, which is where the wind-shear case that broke my gain schedule actually turned up.

What I would do differently is ask about the constraint first. I spent the first part of that conversation arguing for the campaign before I had asked why the date was immovable, and the answer to that question was most of what I needed."

**Why this works:** the disagreement is real and is stated once, with a magnitude attached. The lead's reasoning is given fairly and turns out to be sound, which costs the candidate nothing. The commitment is genuine — she ran the smaller campaign — and is accompanied by two things she did anyway: labelling the limitation so nobody would over-read the result, and scheduling the full run at the first moment it was worth doing. And the closing line is a change in her own practice rather than a complaint about the decision.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
A candidate describes a disagreement in which her colleague "did not really have a technical reason, he just preferred his own approach." What is wrong with this, and what are the two honest repairs?
:::

::: answer
The colleague's position has been given with no reason behind it, which leaves the listener with a story in which one participant is not a serious engineer — and since the only source for that characterisation is the candidate, it reflects on the account rather than on the colleague. The first repair is to recover the reason: it existed, and if the candidate cannot state it, the useful admission is that she never asked, which is a real finding about her own conduct in the disagreement. The second is to tell a different story, if the position genuinely was not reasonable, because a disagreement with someone unreasonable demonstrates nothing that a disagreement with a competent colleague does not demonstrate better.
:::

::: check
You and a colleague agree on every number and still disagree about whether to proceed. What kind of disagreement is this, and what is the move?
:::

::: answer
It is a disagreement about risk tolerance, not about facts, and producing more evidence will not resolve it — both of you already accept the evidence. The move is to surface the acceptance criterion that was never written down: what would have to be true for this to be acceptable, who is entitled to decide that, and what the consequence is if the judgement is wrong. Often that conversation reveals that the criterion belongs to someone else entirely, at which point the disagreement is not yours to settle and the honest step is to take both positions, costed, to the person who owns it.
:::

::: check
Why does this lesson claim that a story where you were right and prevailed is the weakest common version, when being right is presumably good?
:::

::: answer
Because as an answer it carries almost no information. A story that ends in vindication is told identically by an engineer who genuinely weighed the counter-argument and by one who could not hear it, so the listener learns nothing about which they are dealing with. The themes that carry information are the ones where something was at risk: a story where you changed your position on specific evidence, or where you were overruled and committed properly, or where you and a competent colleague had to find a way to decide without agreeing. Being right is not disqualifying — but if the only conflict story in your bank ends in your vindication, the bank is missing the version that actually demonstrates something.
:::

::: check
Your manager overrules you on a judgement call you still think is wrong. What are the two legitimate courses, and what is the third thing people do instead?
:::

::: answer
Commit properly, or escalate through the organisation's own process if you believe the risk is genuinely unacceptable. The third thing — agreeing in the room, continuing to argue it elsewhere, and executing at half speed — is the one worth naming because it feels like a middle course and is worse than either. It leaves the decision unexamined by anyone with the standing to change it and adds an unreliable execution, and it is a story you would never tell in an interview, which is a reasonable test of whether it is a way to behave. Committing properly is compatible with recording the limitation: running the smaller campaign and writing one line saying the tail is unresolved is commitment, not sabotage.
:::

::: check
Why does the shape of a conflict story include a sentence about what happened afterwards?
:::

::: answer
Because a disagreement is not resolved by reaching a decision; it is resolved by reaching a decision the working relationship survives. A story that ends at the moment of resolution leaves the listener unable to tell whether the colleague was persuaded, steamrollered or quietly alienated — and if the last of those, the candidate has described a cost as if it were a win. One factual sentence is enough: what the two of you did next, whether the arrangement held, whether they brought you the next problem. It is also the part that is hardest to fake, which is why it is worth having.
:::

::: check
A candidate's conflict story ends: "In the end we compromised and did a bit of both." What is weak about this, and what would strengthen it without changing the events?
:::

::: answer
*Compromised* names an outcome without naming a mechanism, so the listener cannot tell whether the two of you found a way to test the question, split the difference arbitrarily, or ran out of energy. Splitting a technical disagreement down the middle is often the worst available option, since neither position's reasoning survives it. Strengthen it by stating what the resolution actually was and why it was defensible: a narrowed test both parties agreed to accept in advance, an explicit decision rule for each outcome, or a division that gave each concern the thing it needed. "We ran the hundred-case version overnight and agreed beforehand what we would show in each case" is the same compromise, with the reasoning visible.
:::

## Summary

| Element | What it must contain | How it fails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Their position | Stated at its strongest, with the reason | A position with no reason behind it |
| The axis | Facts, risk tolerance, or priorities | Arguing a risk question as if it were factual |
| Your moves | Restating, testing, costing, escalating, conceding | Accumulating evidence at each other |
| Resolution | What settled it, or how you proceeded without agreement | "We compromised" |
| Afterwards | One sentence on whether the relationship held | Ends at the moment of victory |

A disagreement with a manager has one asymmetry: advocate once with a magnitude attached, ask what the decision rests on, then commit or escalate — and label any limitation the decision leaves behind. The next lesson takes the module's four reported red flags together, including the two this lesson and the last one have been circling: blaming others, and being unable to name a real failure.
