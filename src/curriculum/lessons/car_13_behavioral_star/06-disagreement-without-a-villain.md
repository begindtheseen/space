Think of a movie where the hero argues with someone. If the other character has no reason for what they want — they are stubborn, or lazy, or mean — you know they are the **villain**, and the argument is not interesting. The arguments that are interesting are the ones where both sides have a point.

*Tell me about a time you disagreed with a colleague* is the theme where the obstacle in your story is a person. In every other theme the difficulty is a system, a deadline or your own mistake. Here the difficulty has a point of view, and how you describe it is most of what your answer says about you.

The typical failure is to make the other person the villain — not by insulting them, but by writing them in as an obstacle to your being right. The giveaway is that the colleague's position is stated only well enough to be dismissed. "He wanted to skip the verification because he thought it was a waste of time" is a villain sentence: a position with no reason behind it, which no competent person would hold. So either the colleague was not competent, or the account is incomplete.

This matters for a practical reason, not a moral one. The listener has exactly one source for the other person's view: you. Only an account that gives their position [[at its strongest|steelman]] shows that you understood it — and understanding the other side is what this theme probes, because it is what makes you possible to work with when a disagreement is real.

## What this theme can show

Not whether you win arguments. A story where you were right and won is the weakest common version. It sounds exactly like a story told by someone who cannot hear a counter-argument.

What the answer can show is narrower and more useful:

- that you can hold a technical position and change it because of evidence, not pressure;
- that you can tell what kind of disagreement you are in;
- that you can reach a decision without damaging the working relationship you will need next week.

A good story shows at least two of these three skills.

## Telling what kind of disagreement you are in

Naming the [[axis|three-kinds]] — the thing you actually disagree about — is half the resolution, and it is the step most often skipped. There are three kinds.

**A disagreement about facts.** You believe the filter is inconsistent; your colleague believes the plots look fine. This kind can be settled. The resolution is a measurement you both agree *in advance* to accept. If you propose that test — and say what result would change your own mind — the argument stops being about who sounds more confident.

**A disagreement about risk tolerance.** **Risk tolerance** is how much chance of something going wrong you are willing to accept. You both agree the campaign has a four percent [[infeasible fraction|infeasible-fraction]]. You think that blocks the demo; your colleague thinks it does not. No measurement settles this, because you are arguing about an **acceptance criterion** — the pass/fail rule — that was never written down. The move is to bring the criterion into the open: what would have to be true for this to be acceptable, and who gets to decide that?

**A disagreement about priorities.** Your colleague wants the demo ready Friday; you want the verification done. Both goals are legitimate, they compete for the same hours, and the tradeoff usually belongs to someone else. Spotting that early saves an argument neither of you has the authority to settle. *I realized this was not ours to decide, so I took it to the lead with both options costed* — meaning with the time and effort of each option spelled out — is a strong sentence in a story.

Mistaking one kind for another turns a twenty-minute conversation into two weeks of friction. Argue a risk-tolerance question as if it were about facts, and you get two people throwing more and more evidence at each other when neither is disputing the evidence.

::: key
A disagreement story is stronger when the other person's position is stated at its strongest, with the reason they held it. Name which kind of disagreement it was — facts, risk tolerance or priorities — because the resolution differs: a test settles facts, an explicit criterion settles risk, and priorities usually belong to whoever owns the tradeoff.
:::

## The shape of the story

Five parts, in this order, inside the usual ninety seconds:

1. **The two positions, both reasonable.** Theirs first, with its reason. Yours second.
2. **What was at stake.** Schedule, risk, scope or correctness — and which kind of disagreement it was.
3. **What you did.** These moves are the Action section: repeating their position back to them, finding a missing piece of information, proposing a test, costing both options, escalating, conceding.
4. **How it resolved.** Who turned out to be right, what settled it, or how you went ahead without agreeing.
5. **What happened afterwards.** One sentence on the relationship, and whether the resolution held.

Part five does real work. A disagreement that ended in a decision but left a colleague unwilling to bring you the next problem was not resolved. It was won.

::: warning The story where the other person never speaks
If your account has no sentence where the other person makes a point you found reasonable, the story has a hole, and the interviewer can see it. There are two honest fixes. Recover their actual reason — it existed, and if you cannot remember it, you probably never asked, which is itself worth saying. Or, if their position truly was unreasonable, tell a different story: a disagreement with an unreasonable person shows nothing except that you met one.
:::

## Disagreeing with a manager

The same structure, with one difference in power: you can argue, and then *they* decide. A story that pretends otherwise sounds like someone who has never worked in an organization. A story where you never argued at all sounds like someone who will not tell them when something is wrong.

The shape that works is **advocate, then commit** — and say out loud where one ends and the other begins.

**Advocate once, plainly, with evidence.** State the concern, how big it is, and what fixing it would cost. Put a number on it if you can. The earlier lessons' standard applies: a concern with a size attached is a different conversation from a vague feeling of discomfort.

**Ask what the decision rests on.** Really ask. Managers usually have constraints you cannot see. "What would have to be true for the campaign to be worth the extra two days?" often brings out the actual reason — and you may agree with it.

**Then commit or [[escalate|escalate]] — nothing else.** If you are overruled on a judgment call, commit properly: it was their decision to make. Continuing to argue while doing the work half-heartedly is the failure mode worth naming. If you believe the risk is truly unacceptable — a safety issue, or something you think is being hidden — that is what escalation exists for, and the right path depends on the organization's own process rather than anything this module can tell you.

What almost never goes well is the [[quiet version|passive-resistance]]: agreeing in the room, complaining to colleagues afterward, and doing the work slowly. You would never tell that story in an interview — a reasonable sign it is not a way to behave.

## The version where you were wrong

A conflict story where you turn out to be mistaken is often the strongest you have, for the same reason the failure story is strong: it proves an argument can move you. It must be a real technical position, sincerely held, changed by something specific — not a token concession.

The last sentence matters. "She was right, and what convinced me was the [[plot of the residual against elevation angle|residual-plot]], which I had not looked at" is much stronger than "we compromised". It names the evidence and credits the person who found it.

::: example One disagreement, two accounts
The event: the candidate and a teammate on a club satellite project disagreed about whether to run the estimator consistency tests before a departmental demo three days away.

**The villain version:**

"On the club project I had a teammate who wanted to cut corners. We were coming up to the demo and he decided the consistency tests were not worth doing — he just wanted the thing to look good on the day. I pushed back because I did not think we should be showing results we had not checked, and eventually I stayed up and ran them myself. Two of the runs were outside the band, which proved my point. He was not thrilled about it but the numbers were the numbers."

All of this may be true, and it still fails. *Wanted to cut corners*, *decided they were not worth doing*, *just wanted it to look good* — three guesses about the teammate's motives and no statement of his reason. *Which proved my point* treats the outcome as a score. And the last sentence leaves a colleague who is now less likely to bring this candidate a concern again; the story treats that as a minor detail instead of the cost it is.

**The same events, told straight:**

"Three days before a departmental demo, a teammate and I disagreed about whether to run the estimator consistency tests. His argument was that the demo was a communication exercise, not a review. The audience would look at an animation, the tests would take most of a day of compute and rework we did not have, and there was a real chance of finding something we could not fix in time — leaving us nothing to show. That is a coherent position, and it was not lazy.

Mine was that the demo was the first time anyone outside the team would see attitude numbers from our filter, and I did not want to put a covariance on a slide I had not tested. We were not really disagreeing about facts. We were disagreeing about what the demo was for, and neither of us had said so until I asked him what he thought the audience would do with the numbers.

What broke it open was narrowing the test. Instead of the full campaign, I proposed a hundred-run version of the consistency check overnight, which we could afford. We agreed in advance: inside the band, we show the numbers; outside, we show the animation and say the filter is still in validation. It came back with the average above the upper limit of the band, so that is what we did — we presented it as work in progress and said what we were checking next.

He was the one who suggested putting the consistency plot in the appendix, to talk about if anyone asked. That turned out to be the part two people in the audience were most interested in."

**What changed:** his position comes with its reason and is genuinely defensible. The kind of disagreement is named — not facts, but what the event was for. The Action contains a move that made the disagreement settleable instead of a contest of conviction: shrink the test and agree the decision rule in advance. And the last line gives the colleague a contribution, which costs the candidate nothing and is the clearest evidence that the relationship survived.
:::

::: example Overruled by a lead, told without bitterness and without spinelessness
The question: *Tell me about a time you disagreed with a decision your manager made.*

**The answer:**

"On a student launch project I owned the [[dispersion campaign|dispersion-tail]], and our lead wanted to cut it from five hundred cases to a hundred to make a review date. I disagreed, and I said so once, with the reason: at a hundred cases the tail is thin enough that a failure mode showing up in about one percent of runs might appear zero times, and the whole point of the campaign was the tail. I costed it too — the full run was about nine hours on our hardware, so the real question was whether we could start it a day earlier, not whether to do it at all.

He said no. The review slot was fixed, the vehicle configuration was still changing, and he expected to re-run everything after the design froze anyway — so spending our last compute day on a configuration about to change was exactly what he was avoiding. That was a better reason than I had assumed, based on schedule information I did not have.

So we ran the hundred cases, and I did two things instead of sulking. I wrote one line in the results summary saying the campaign was a hundred cases and the tail was not resolved at that size, so nobody downstream would read it as a full campaign. And I started the full five-hundred-case run the night the configuration froze — which is where the wind-shear case that broke my [[gain schedule|gain-schedule]] actually turned up.

What I would do differently is ask about the constraint first. I spent the start of that conversation arguing for the campaign before asking why the date could not move, and the answer to that question was most of what I needed."

**Why this works:** the disagreement is real and stated once, with a size attached. The lead's reasoning is given fairly and turns out to be sound, which costs the candidate nothing. The commitment is genuine — she ran the smaller campaign — and she did two more things: labeled the limitation so nobody would over-read the result, and scheduled the full run at the first moment it was worth doing. The closing line is a change in her own practice, not a complaint about the decision.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
A candidate describes a disagreement in which her colleague "did not really have a technical reason, he just preferred his own approach." What is wrong with this, and what are the two honest repairs?
:::

::: answer
The colleague's position has no reason behind it, so the listener hears a story in which one person is not a serious engineer. Since she is the only source, it reflects on her account, not on him. **Repair one:** recover the reason. It existed; if she cannot state it, the useful admission is that she never asked — a real finding about her own part in the disagreement. **Repair two:** if his position truly was unreasonable, tell a different story. A disagreement with someone unreasonable shows nothing that a disagreement with a competent colleague does not show better.
:::

::: check
You and a colleague agree on every number and still disagree about whether to go ahead. What kind of disagreement is this, and what is the move?
:::

::: answer
It is about risk tolerance, not facts, so more evidence will not settle it — you both already accept the evidence. The move is to bring the unwritten acceptance criterion into the open: what would have to be true for this to be acceptable, who is entitled to decide, and what happens if the judgment is wrong. Often that reveals the criterion belongs to someone else entirely. Then the disagreement is not yours to settle, and the honest step is to take both positions, costed, to the person who owns the decision.
:::

::: check
Why is a story where you were right and won the weakest common version, when being right is presumably good?
:::

::: answer
Because it carries almost no information. A story ending in vindication sounds the same whether it comes from an engineer who truly weighed the counter-argument or one who could not hear it, so the listener cannot tell which you are. The stories that carry information are the ones where something was at risk: you changed position on specific evidence, or were overruled and committed properly, or you and a competent colleague had to decide without agreeing. Being right is not disqualifying — but if your only conflict story ends in your vindication, the bank is missing the version that actually shows something.
:::

::: check
Your manager overrules you on a judgment call you still think is wrong. What are the two legitimate courses, and what is the third thing people do instead?
:::

::: answer
Commit properly, or escalate through the organization's own process if you believe the risk is truly unacceptable. The third thing — agreeing in the room, arguing it elsewhere, and working at half speed — feels like a middle path but is worse than either. Nobody with the authority to change the decision ever examines it, and the work gets done unreliably. You would never tell that story in an interview — a good test of whether it is a way to behave. Committing properly is compatible with recording a limitation: running the smaller campaign and writing one line saying the tail is unresolved is commitment, not sabotage.
:::

::: check
Why does the shape of a conflict story include a sentence about what happened afterward?
:::

::: answer
Because a disagreement is not resolved by reaching a decision. It is resolved by reaching a decision the working relationship survives. A story that stops at the moment of decision leaves the listener unable to tell whether the colleague was persuaded, steamrolled or quietly alienated — and if the last, the candidate has described a cost as if it were a win. One factual sentence is enough: what you did together next, whether the arrangement held, whether they brought you the next problem. It is also the hardest part to fake.
:::

::: check
A candidate's conflict story ends: "In the end we compromised and did a bit of both." What is weak about this, and how could it be strengthened without changing the events?
:::

::: answer
*Compromised* names an outcome without a mechanism. The listener cannot tell whether you found a way to test the question, split the difference arbitrarily, or ran out of energy. Splitting a technical disagreement down the middle is often the worst option, since neither side's reasoning survives. Strengthen it by saying what the resolution actually was and why it was sound: a smaller test both sides agreed in advance to accept, a decision rule for each outcome, or a split that gave each concern what it needed. "We ran the hundred-case version overnight and agreed beforehand what we would show in each case" is the same compromise with the reasoning visible.
:::

## Summary

| Element | What it must contain | How it fails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Their position | Stated at its strongest, with the reason | A position with no reason behind it |
| The axis | Facts, risk tolerance, or priorities | Arguing a risk question as if it were factual |
| Your moves | Restating, testing, costing, escalating, conceding | Piling up evidence at each other |
| Resolution | What settled it, or how you proceeded without agreement | "We compromised" |
| Afterwards | One sentence on whether the relationship held | Ends at the moment of victory |

A disagreement with a manager has one difference in power: advocate once with a size attached, ask what the decision rests on, then commit or escalate — and label any limitation the decision leaves behind. The next lesson takes the module's four reported red flags together, including the two this lesson and the last have been circling: blaming others, and being unable to name a real failure.

::: context steelman The opposite of a straw man
A **straw man** is a weak, silly version of someone's argument, built so it is easy to knock down — like punching a scarecrow instead of a real opponent. People have started calling the opposite a **steel man**: the strongest, fairest version of the other side, the one its best defender would recognize.

In a disagreement story, steelmanning does two jobs at once. It proves you actually listened, and it makes your own choice look better, because you are shown choosing well against a strong alternative instead of beating a scarecrow.
:::

::: context infeasible-fraction What "four percent infeasible" means
In a **dispersion campaign** (a Monte Carlo campaign), you run the same flight hundreds of times, each with slightly different winds, masses and sensor errors drawn at random. ::: context escalate When escalating is the right call
To **escalate** is to take a problem up the chain, to someone with more authority than the person who decided. It is not a tantrum; most engineering organizations have a formal path for it, especially for safety.

The reason it exists is history. The night before the Space Shuttle Challenger launched in January 1986, engineers at the contractor Morton Thiokol warned that the cold could stop the rubber O-ring seals in the solid rocket boosters from sealing properly, and recommended not launching. They were overruled. The shuttle broke apart 73 seconds after liftoff, and all seven crew died. Every spaceflight organization now teaches that case.

The everyday version is gentler: Amazon lists "disagree and commit" among its leadership principles — argue hard, then back the decision fully once it is made.
:::

::: context passive-resistance The name for the quiet version
Agreeing to someone's face and then resisting by complaining, dragging your feet or doing the work badly is often called being **passive-aggressive**. It feels safer than open disagreement because nobody can point to a single moment where you said no.

For a team, it is the worst of both options. The decision never gets reconsidered by anyone with the power to change it, and the work gets done unreliably. Open disagreement followed by honest commitment is both kinder and more useful.
:::

::: context residual-plot What that plot would show
A **residual** is a measurement minus what the model predicted — what is left over. If the model is right, residuals look like random noise around zero.

**Elevation angle** is how high above the horizon the satellite is when it is measured. Signals from a satellite low on the horizon pass through much more air, which bends and slows them. So if residuals grow at low elevation angles, the problem is probably the atmosphere model, not the orbit. One plot like that can settle an argument about where an error comes from, which is why the example credits it.
:::

::: context dispersion-tail Why a hundred runs can miss the tail
The **tail** is the rare, bad end of the spread of outcomes. Suppose a failure mode shows up in 1 run out of 100 on average. The chance that a 100-run campaign shows it *zero* times is $0.99^{100} \approx 0.37$. With 500 runs it is $0.99^{500} \approx 0.0066$.

::: context gain-schedule Gain schedules and wind shear
A controller's **gains** are the numbers that set how hard it reacts to an error. A rocket's behavior changes a lot as it speeds up and the air thins, so one set of gains cannot suit the whole flight. A **gain schedule** is a table that switches gains as flight conditions change.

**Wind shear** is a sudden change in wind speed or direction with height. A strong shear at the wrong moment can push a vehicle harder than the schedule was tuned for — exactly the kind of rare case that only a large campaign tends to find. The control modules later in the course build gain schedules from scratch.
:::
