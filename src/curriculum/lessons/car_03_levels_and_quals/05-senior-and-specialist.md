Think about two ways into a concert. One venue has three doors: a ticket gets you in, a backstage pass gets you in, and so does a wristband from last night's show. Any one of them works. Another venue has a single door marked "members only," and no other way in is posted anywhere.

So far, every posting in this module has had one shape of basic qualification: a degree **and** a number of years, both required together. This lesson meets two postings that break that shape, in opposite directions.

The **Sr. GNC Engineer** posting is the venue with three doors. Its requirement splits into three routes joined by "or," and one of them is the first of exactly two places in this role family where professional experience formally *replaces* a degree, rather than adding to one. The **specialist** postings — Navigation and State Estimation, and Operations Automation — are the members-only door. They raise the floor to a graduate degree and state no bachelor's-plus-experience alternative at all.

Neither one is merely "the next rung" above Level I to II. They are two different moves, and seeing them side by side is the clearest way to understand how differently a basic qualification can be built once a role stops being an entry-to-mid-level generalist job.

## Sr. GNC Engineer: three ways to qualify

Here is the basic qualification, word for word:

> Bachelor's in computer science, aerospace, physics or engineering plus 5+ years of professional experience in GNC or software engineering, OR 7+ years of professional experience in lieu of a degree, OR a postgraduate degree in a related field.

This sentence has a new **[[shape|three-branches]]**. It has three branches joined by "or," and any one branch, met in full, is enough on its own. Compare Level I to II: there you had to meet a degree clause *and* a years clause together. Here you need to meet only one branch — but all of that branch.

### Branch one: bachelor's plus 5+ years

A bachelor's degree in computer science, aerospace, physics or engineering, plus 5+ years of professional experience in GNC or software engineering.

Notice that this list names computer science outright, next to the broad "engineering" category. That closely matches the New Graduate posting's four named fields from lesson two. Lesson three raised a real question about whether a CS degree fits Level I to II's narrower "physics or an engineering discipline." That question does not carry over here. Computer science is named.

### Branch two: 7+ years in lieu of a degree

Seven or more years of professional experience **[[in lieu of|in-lieu]]** a degree — "in place of" a degree. No degree of any kind is needed, as long as the years threshold is met.

This is one of exactly two stated degree-optional routes in all the postings this module covers. At New Graduate and at Level I to II, a missing degree is a hard filter with no stated alternative. So this branch is a real route, if a long one. You should be able to state it accurately without hesitating.

### Branch three: a postgraduate degree in a related field

A **postgraduate degree** — a **[[master's or a doctorate|grad-degrees]]** — "in a related field."

Read that phrase slowly. It differs from every earlier degree clause in this module. New Graduate named four fields. Level I to II named two. Branch one of this very posting names four. Branch three names none. It uses exactly the kind of open "related field" wording that lesson one warned you to read carefully — and this time there is no list beside it to settle the question.

So here is the honest answer. The source material behind this module does not list what counts as "related." Inventing a confident boundary — declaring that some unusual field definitely does or does not qualify — would be manufacturing certainty the text does not support.

- A postgraduate degree with an obvious, direct link to GNC work is a safe reading of "related."
- A postgraduate degree with a real but less direct link is exactly the case to **[[ask a recruiter about|ask-recruiter]]** directly, rather than guess.

::: key
Sr. GNC Engineer — basic qualifications: bachelor's in computer science, aerospace, physics or engineering plus 5+ years of professional experience in GNC or software engineering, OR 7+ years of professional experience in lieu of a degree, OR a postgraduate degree in a related field.
:::

::: warning "A related field" in branch three is not a blank check
Because no list narrows branch three, it is tempting to read it as "any graduate degree at all." But the word "related" is still doing real work. It is a qualifier, not a formality. A graduate degree with no plausible technical link to GNC work is not plainly covered merely because the clause does not spell out what is excluded. Where the link is not evident, treat the question as open — not as settled in your favor.
:::

## What "senior" adds beyond a bigger number

The three branches only say who may be *considered*. They say nothing about how the work changes. That is stated separately, and it is a list of four things:

- **[[architecture and code reviews|code-review]]** — checking other people's design decisions and code, not only producing your own;
- **writing [[production-quality code|production-code]]** — code meant to run and last in real use, not code that shows a result once;
- **leading mission-level design** — owning the design for a whole mission or vehicle program, not only one subsystem;
- **mentoring** — helping other engineers grow, directly.

Notice what kind of list this is. It is not a longer list of the same technical knowledge Level I to II already asked for. It describes **ownership**.

Here is a way to picture it. A Level I to II engineer is like a skilled player on a team. A senior engineer is also a skilled player, but the coach now asks them to call some plays, check teammates' work, and teach the rookies. They are not expected to out-derive a Level I to II engineer on paper. They are trusted with decisions that reach further, and that someone else is no longer double-checking as closely.

So the step up to senior is about **ownership and judgment**, not about knowing more equations.

That has a practical consequence for interviews. Ownership and judgment show up in specific decisions on specific past work, not in a credential. So the **past-project deep-dive round** — where interviewers dig into one thing you built — and the behavioral evidence that lesson seven examines carry even more weight for a senior candidate than at Level I to II.

## Specialist roles: when the floor is a graduate degree

Two named postings move in the opposite direction from branch three. Branch three offers a graduate degree as *one* route among three. These postings make it *the* floor.

They are both from the earlier role-families module:

- **Navigation and State Estimation** — star-tracker and signals-based attitude determination, inertial propagation, **[[GNSS|gnss]]** work and **[[orbit determination|orbit-determination]]**.
- **Operations Automation** (full title: GNC Software Engineer, Operations Automation) — building the analysis tools, pipelines and automation that support GNC work broadly.

Their basic qualification reads:

> A Master's or PhD in an engineering discipline, computer science or physics, plus experience developing, debugging and deploying software used in real-world applications.

Read this exactly, and no further than it goes. The clause states a graduate degree plus real software experience. The text available to this module does not *explicitly forbid* some other route. What you can say accurately is narrower, and still useful:

- No bachelor's-plus-experience alternative is named anywhere in this clause.
- That contrasts sharply with Sr. GNC Engineer, whose second and third branches offer exactly those kinds of alternatives in writing.

For a candidate deciding whether to apply, the practical reading is the same either way. The stated floor is the graduate degree. A bachelor's-only application would be relying on an alternative the posting never offers.

### Why a graduate degree here?

The source material gives no reason for this particular employer's choice, and none should be invented. What can be said honestly is a general pattern seen across the industry. Research-adjacent specialist roles often set a graduate degree as the entry bar, because the subject matter overlaps heavily with what most engineers only reach through graduate courses or research. The theory behind state estimation is one example. The breadth of tooling across a whole simulation and flight-software stack is another.

That is a common pattern, not a universal law. Treat it as a plausible general explanation, not as a stated fact about why any one posting is written the way it is.

::: key
Navigation and State Estimation, and Operations Automation — basic qualifications: a Master's or PhD in an engineering discipline, computer science or physics, plus experience developing, debugging and deploying software used in real-world applications.
:::

## Comparing the two moves

Put side by side, the two postings do opposite things to the same idea.

**Sr. GNC Engineer adds doors.** A candidate without a matching bachelor's still has two other stated routes: one built on extra years, and one built on a different kind of degree.

**The specialist postings close a door.** No bachelor's-plus-experience alternative is stated. The graduate degree is not one option among several; it is the floor itself.

Neither is plainly "harder." They are **[[testing for different things|two-axes]]**:

- Seniority is a claim about accumulated ownership and judgment. Years of experience — even a long record with no degree — can plausibly show it.
- Specialist depth is a claim about a deep theoretical or technical background in one sub-field. These postings treat formal graduate training as the most reliable proof of it.

::: warning A specialist posting is not "one level above" Sr. GNC Engineer
Specialist roles ask for more formal education than even senior's most degree-heavy branch, so it is easy to assume they stack on top of it. They do not. Specialist postings sit on a different axis — depth of formal training in one sub-discipline — not a higher rung on the seniority ladder this module has been climbing. A specialist candidate and a senior candidate are shaped by different postings, not by the same posting at two heights.
:::

::: example A near-miss on branch two
A candidate has six and a half years of professional software engineering experience, much of it close to GNC work, and no bachelor's degree. Check each branch in turn.

**Branch one** — bachelor's plus 5+ years. There is no degree to pair with the years. Fails.

**Branch two** — 7+ years in lieu of a degree. They have 6.5 years. The gap is $7 - 6.5 = 0.5$ years, which is 6 months. Close, but not yet met.

**Branch three** — postgraduate degree. None. Fails.

**Result today:** on paper, this candidate does not qualify for Sr. GNC Engineer.

**What kind of gap is it?** A **[[time gap|months-to-go]]**: about 6 more months on the path they are already on. It is not a structural mismatch.

**Contrast with Level I to II.** Picture the same person — no degree — applying to Level I to II. That posting states no in-lieu-of-degree route at all. There, no amount of extra time closes the gap, because there is no branch to grow into.

**Sanity check:** the same missing-degree fact produces two entirely different situations, depending on which structure a posting states. That is why reading the exact sentence matters more than knowing your own record.
:::

::: example A postgraduate degree the text does not settle
A candidate holds a master's degree in applied mathematics, focused on optimization and statistical estimation. They have two years of professional software experience since graduating.

**Branch one** needs a bachelor's in computer science, aerospace, physics or engineering *plus* 5+ years. Whatever their bachelor's was in, two years is short of five. Fails on years alone.

**Branch three** needs a postgraduate degree "in a related field." Optimization and estimation link directly to GNC work, so "related" is a plausible reading. But mathematics is not named in this or any other degree clause this module has examined, so the posting does not confirm it.

**Specialist postings** list "an engineering discipline, computer science or physics." Mathematics is not on that list either — a real gap in the text, not an oversight this lesson can resolve for the candidate.

**Honest position:** treat both routes as plausible, not confirmed, and raise the question directly rather than assume the convenient answer.

**Sanity check:** guessing wrong costs time either way. Over-confidently applying where the degree clause was never going to count wastes an application. Wrongly ruling out a posting the degree would likely have satisfied wastes a chance.
:::

::: context in-lieu Reading "in lieu of"
**In lieu of** means "in place of" or "instead of." It is said "in loo of." The phrase came into English from French, where *lieu* means "place" — the same root as **lieutenant**, the officer who "holds the place" of a higher one.

So "7+ years of professional experience in lieu of a degree" means the years stand *in the place of* the degree. They do not add to one; they replace it.
:::

::: context grad-degrees Master's and PhD in a sentence each
In the US, a **master's degree** usually takes one to two years after a bachelor's, mostly advanced courses and sometimes a thesis. A **PhD** — "doctor of philosophy," said "P-H-D" — usually takes several more years and ends with original research written up as a dissertation. Both are **postgraduate** or **graduate** degrees: they come after the first, undergraduate degree. Exact lengths vary widely by school and field.
:::

::: context ask-recruiter How to ask about an unclear line
Asking a recruiter is normal and costs little. Keep it short and factual: name the posting, quote the exact line, and state your case. For example: "The Sr. GNC Engineer posting lists a postgraduate degree in a related field. My master's is in applied mathematics, focused on optimization and estimation. Would that be read as related?"

A clear question like that gets a clear answer faster than a long, hopeful paragraph. Whatever the answer is, save it with the date — postings and readings change.
:::

::: context code-review What a code review is
Before new code joins a shared project, another engineer reads it line by line and comments: is it correct, is it clear, is it tested, does it fit the design? That is a **code review**. An **architecture review** is the same idea one level up: checking how the big pieces of a system fit together before much code is written.

Reviewing is where a senior engineer's judgment spreads to the whole team. One good catch in review can prevent a bug that would otherwise reach a vehicle.
:::

::: context production-code Prototype code versus production code
A **prototype** is code written to answer a question once — "does this filter converge?" — and then set aside. **Production code** is code that other people and other programs depend on, sometimes for years, and that may fly.

Production-quality code is tested, reviewed, documented and written so a stranger can safely change it later. For flight software it also has to meet strict rules about timing and memory. Writing code of that standard is a separate skill from getting the right answer once.
:::

::: context gnss GPS is one of several
**GNSS**, said letter by letter "G-N-S-S," stands for **global navigation satellite system**. GPS, run by the United States, is the best-known one. Others include Europe's Galileo, Russia's GLONASS and China's BeiDou. A receiver that listens to several systems sees more satellites at once, which makes its position fix more robust. That is why navigation postings often say GNSS rather than GPS.
:::

::: context orbit-determination Working out an orbit
**Orbit determination** is the job of working out exactly which orbit a spacecraft is on, from measurements such as GNSS fixes or radio ranging from the ground. Once you know the orbit, physics predicts where the spacecraft will be later.

For a fleet of thousands of satellites, this has to be done over and over, automatically and accurately — which is why it is specialist work, and why the course's orbital mechanics and estimation modules both feed into it.
:::
