Think of a sports league with a division only for first-year players. Nobody asks how many seasons you have played, because the whole point is that you have not played many. They ask whether you are really a first-year player and whether you can do the basics.

The New Graduate Engineer, GNC posting is that division. It is written for someone who does not yet have a work record to point to, so it is where most readers of this module will actually start. Two things about it are easy to misread without the basic-versus-preferred framework from the previous lesson:

- Its basic qualifications say **nothing about years of experience**. That silence is itself information, once you know to look for it.
- Its preferred qualifications name a **graduate degree** and a list of subjects that can look, at first, like a bar far higher than "new graduate" should need.

This lesson reads the posting clause by clause. Along the way it introduces a distinction that matters for every later level: what a posting **lists by name** versus what it leaves to a vague "related field."

## What the tag means

Postings like this one carry a tag in the title saying which hiring cycle and place the job belongs to. Here it is "(26/27, Starlink, Redmond WA)." Read it as carefully as the qualifications, because it tells you something they do not: who this particular **[[requisition|requisition]]** — the company's formal, approved request to fill a job — is trying to reach, and where.

- **"26/27"** marks a **[[graduating-class window|cohort-window]]**: people finishing their degree in the 2026 or 2027 academic cycle. Companies that hire many graduates at once often tie a new-grad requisition to a class window like this. Campus recruiting, start dates and training for a group of new hires all run on a yearly or twice-yearly rhythm.
- **"Starlink"** names the program the job sits inside — SpaceX's satellite internet network.
- **"Redmond WA"** names the site: Redmond, Washington, where SpaceX does **[[Starlink engineering|starlink-redmond]]**. Other GNC role families are based at other SpaceX sites.

None of this changes what the qualifications require. It changes who should expect this particular requisition to fit them. Someone graduating outside the window, or aiming at a different program or site, is not shut out by the qualifications text. But they are probably looking at the wrong posting for their situation, and a different requisition open at the same time is likely a better match.

## The basic qualifications, clause by clause

The stated line is:

> Bachelor's degree in computer science, aerospace, physics, or an engineering discipline; software development experience in either C++ or Python.

That is two clauses joined by a **[[semicolon|semicolon-and]]**. Each does its own job, and you must meet both.

### The degree clause

It names four fields: computer science, aerospace, physics, and "an engineering discipline." The last one covers mechanical, electrical, systems and the other usual engineering majors.

Notice a word that is *missing*: "related." The clause does not ask you to guess whether your major is close enough to some unnamed category. It lists the category directly.

That matters. The vague phrase many candidates brace for — "or related field" — pushes a guessing problem onto the reader. A named list takes the guessing away. Where a posting lists the fields it accepts, "does my degree count?" can almost always be answered by reading the list.

### The software clause

"Software development experience in either **[[C++ or Python|cpp-python]]**" has no number of years. It also has no word "professional" in front of "experience." Both absences matter.

Compare the line in the next lesson, which asks for "2+ years of professional experience." That wording plainly wants a paid, employer-based record. This one asks only for software development experience in one of two named languages, and says nothing about where it came from. A substantial coursework project, a personal project carried through to something working, or an internship are all reasonable ways to meet a clause worded this openly.

Still, as the previous lesson showed, an unstated question like this is worth confirming with a recruiter, rather than assuming confidently either way.

::: key
New Graduate Engineer, GNC — required: Bachelor's degree in computer science, aerospace, physics, or an engineering discipline; software development experience in either C++ or Python. Targets recent and upcoming graduates for the 2026 to 2027 window.
:::

## The preferred qualifications

The stated preferred line is:

> Master's or PhD in an engineering discipline or physics; background in orbital mechanics, control theory, state estimation, spacecraft systems design, optimization, and software development.

Apply the previous lesson's framework. This is a signal, not a gate. A candidate with only a bachelor's degree is not disqualified by it. The basic qualifications already set the real floor, and a **[[graduate degree|graduate-degrees]]** appears nowhere in them.

What the preferred line does tell you is what a stronger-than-baseline candidate for this job tends to look like:

- some graduate coursework or research, and
- **background** across the named subjects.

Look closely at the word "background." It is looser than "experience," and much looser than "professional experience." Exposure through courses and projects counts as background. Each named subject — orbital mechanics, control theory, **[[state estimation|background-bridge]]**, spacecraft systems design, optimization, software development — is also a subject this course teaches.

::: key
New Graduate Engineer, GNC — preferred: Master's or PhD in an engineering discipline or physics; background in orbital mechanics, control theory, state estimation, spacecraft systems design, optimization, and software development.
:::

### When a soft signal sorts hard

There is a real tension here, worth saying out loud. A new-grad requisition draws from a whole graduating class, so its applicant pool tends to be large. In a **[[large pool|large-pool]]**, a preferred qualification that is optional on paper can still do a lot of practical sorting. Enough applicants show some of it that a candidate who shows none is competing from behind — even though nothing has disqualified them.

This is not special to this posting. It is how any ranking signal behaves once the pool is big. It is also why the approach the next few lessons build — treating a preferred list as something to *study toward*, not a fixed bar — matters even for qualifications nobody is required to have.

::: warning A graduate degree in the preferred list is not a hidden requirement
Seeing "Master's or PhD" under preferred for a job titled "New Graduate Engineer" makes some candidates think a bachelor's-only application is a waste of time. It is not. The basic qualifications are where a hard floor would be stated if one existed, and they say nothing about a graduate degree. A bachelor's-only candidate who meets the basic line and shows some of the preferred background is a normal, viable applicant, not a long shot.
:::

::: example A graduating senior, checked clause by clause
A student is finishing a mechanical engineering degree. They took one controls course. Over two semesters they built a flight-computer simulation in Python for a university **[[rocketry team|rocketry-team]]**. They have had no paid engineering internship.

**Basic qualifications:**

- Degree clause: mechanical engineering is squarely "an engineering discipline." Met.
- Software clause: the rocketry simulation is real software development in Python. The line never asked for it to be paid or supervised by an employer. Met.

**Preferred qualifications:**

- Graduate degree: none. Unmet.
- Background list, one subject at a time:
  - control theory — partly covered by the one controls course;
  - software development — really covered by the simulation project;
  - orbital mechanics, state estimation, spacecraft systems design, optimization — thin or absent.

So on the background list: 1 subject solid, 1 partial, 4 thin, out of 6.

Sanity check: the student clears the real floor comfortably and shows a genuine but partial match on the signal after it. That is a normal, competitive starting position for this posting, not a weak one.
:::

::: example A year past graduation, wondering which posting fits
A candidate finished a physics degree last year. Since then they have finished a master's degree. Their only work has been the research assistantship that came with the graduate program. They are unsure whether "New Graduate Engineer" still describes them, or whether they should read the Level I to II posting from the next lesson instead.

- Step 1 — basic qualifications. Physics is named in the degree clause. Graduate research with code almost certainly gives software experience in C++ or Python. So the basic text is very likely met either way. It does not settle the question.
- Step 2 — the tag. "(26/27)" suggests the requisition is aimed at people finishing a degree in that window. Their master's may or may not fall inside it, depending on exactly when it was completed.
- Step 3 — decide what to do. This is an honestly unclear fit. Re-reading the posting a tenth time will not settle it. The useful move is to look at both live postings, apply to whichever is the closer current match, and let a recruiter conversation settle facts the text alone cannot.

Sanity check: the candidate did not "fail" anything. The uncertainty is about which door is the right one, not whether any door is open.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
State the New Graduate Engineer, GNC basic qualification exactly, and name the two independent clauses in it.
:::

::: answer
The basic qualification is: "Bachelor's degree in computer science, aerospace, physics, or an engineering discipline; software development experience in either C++ or Python." The two clauses, joined by a semicolon, are the degree requirement (one of four named fields) and the software development experience requirement (in one of two named languages). Each must be met on its own.
:::

::: check
Why does the software clause in this posting carry no number of years and no word "professional," and what does that suggest about what can satisfy it?
:::

::: answer
The clause asks only for "software development experience in either C++ or Python," with no year count and nothing limiting it to paid or employer-based work. That suggests a broader range of evidence can reasonably satisfy it — substantial coursework, a personal project carried to a working result, or an internship — because the posting never narrowed where the experience must come from, the way a "professional experience" line does.
:::

::: check
Explain why this posting's degree clause, which names four fields, works differently from a posting that says "BS in Aerospace or related."
:::

::: answer
A named list removes the guessing that a vaguer clause creates. "Or related" asks the reader to judge, with no guidance, whether their field is close enough to aerospace — a call with no stated boundary. Naming computer science, aerospace, physics and "an engineering discipline" answers the question directly for most candidates. Real uncertainty is left only at the true edges of "an engineering discipline," not across a whole unnamed category.
:::

::: check
A candidate has a mathematics degree, which is not one of the four named fields, but strong software and controls-related project experience. Judge their fit against the basic qualification as written, without assuming a generous reading the text does not support.
:::

::: answer
Mathematics is not computer science, aerospace, or physics, and on an ordinary reading it is not "an engineering discipline" — mathematics departments are usually organized and accredited separately from engineering ones. Taken at face value, the degree clause does not include this degree, however strong the projects are. The degree clause and the software clause are independent, and a strong showing on one does not make up for the other. This is not an unclear case like a borderline years line. Asking a recruiter is still worthwhile, but expect a real no, not a formality.
:::

::: check
Why might the preferred qualifications matter more in practice for a new-graduate requisition than their optional status suggests?
:::

::: answer
A new-graduate requisition draws from a whole graduating class, so the applicant pool is comparatively large. In a large pool, a qualification that is formally a ranking signal can still do a lot of practical sorting: enough applicants show some of it that a candidate showing none starts from behind on ranking, even though nothing has disqualified them. The formal role of a qualification and its practical weight in a big pool are not the same thing.
:::

## Summary

| Clause | Section | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bachelor's in CS, aerospace, physics, or an engineering discipline | Basic | Hard filter; met by finding your field in the list, not by arguing it is "related" |
| Software development experience in C++ or Python | Basic | Hard filter; no stated years and no "professional" |
| Master's or PhD in an engineering discipline or physics | Preferred | Ranking signal; not required for a bachelor's-only applicant |
| Background in orbital mechanics, control theory, state estimation, spacecraft systems design, optimization, software development | Preferred | Ranking signal; partial coverage is normal |
| "(26/27, Starlink, Redmond WA)" | Neither — a targeting tag | Names the class window, program and site the requisition aims at |

::: context requisition What a requisition is
Inside a company, a job does not exist until someone approves paying for it. A **requisition** — recruiters say "req," rhyming with "deck" — is that approved request: this team may hire this many people, at this level, for this program. Each posting on a careers page is the public face of one requisition.

That is why two postings with almost the same title can differ in small ways. They are separate requisitions, written for separate teams' needs at separate times.
:::

::: context starlink-redmond Starlink and Redmond
**Starlink** is SpaceX's network of thousands of satellites in low Earth orbit that provide internet service to users on the ground. Every one of those satellites has to point itself, raise and lower its orbit, and avoid collisions — so there is a great deal of GNC work in it.

Redmond is a city near Seattle, Washington, where SpaceX has a Starlink engineering site. The company also has large sites in Hawthorne, California and at Starbase in south Texas, among others. Which site a posting names tells you where the team sits, and so where you would most likely work.
:::

::: context cpp-python C++ and Python
**C++** is read aloud "see plus plus." It is a fast, strict language used for software that has to run in real time on the vehicle's own computers, where every millisecond and every byte of memory counts. **Python** is slower but quick to write, and is used everywhere for analysis, test scripts, simulation tools and plotting results.

Many GNC teams use both: design and check an idea in Python, then build the version that flies in C++. That is why the language pair keeps turning up in these postings.
:::

::: context graduate-degrees Master's and PhD
After a four-year **bachelor's** degree comes graduate school. A **master's** degree in engineering in the US often takes one to two more years of advanced courses, sometimes with a research thesis. A **PhD** (Doctor of Philosophy, said "P-H-D") usually takes several more years, and its center is original research — something nobody knew before — written up as a dissertation.

For GNC, a master's often means deeper coursework in estimation and control. A PhD means the candidate has already owned a hard, open-ended technical problem for years.
:::

::: context background-bridge Where these subjects live in the course
Each "background" item is a subject this course teaches. **Orbital mechanics** is how things move under gravity in space. **Control theory** is how to make a system do what you want using feedback. **State estimation** is working out where a vehicle is and how it is moving from noisy sensor readings — a Kalman filter is the famous tool. **Optimization** is finding the best choice under limits, like the least-fuel path.

Working through those parts of the course is one honest way to build the "background" the preferred line asks for.
:::

::: context large-pool Why big pools sort on soft signals
Imagine 300 applicants who all clear the basic line, for a handful of openings. Someone still has to pick whom to interview first. The preferred list gives them a fair, job-related way to do it.

If half the pool shows some orbital mechanics background, a candidate who shows none is not rejected — but 150 people are ahead of them on that axis. The fix is not to give up; it is to build and show some of the background before or while applying. (The numbers here are made up to show the idea.)
:::

::: context rocketry-team University rocketry teams
Many universities have student teams that design, build and fly their own rockets, often for intercollegiate competitions. Students on them write flight software, build simulations, choose sensors and test hardware, usually with very little outside help.

That is why a rocketry-team project can honestly count as "software development experience": the code had to work, on real hardware, by a deadline. It is also a natural source of stories for the interview questions later lessons discuss.
:::
