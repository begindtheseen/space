The New Graduate Engineer, GNC posting is where most readers of this module will actually start, because it is the one written for someone who does not yet have a professional record to point to. Its basic qualifications say nothing about years of experience at all — an absence that is itself informative once you know to look for it — and its preferred qualifications name a graduate degree and a list of subject areas that can look, on first read, like a bar far higher than "new graduate" should require. Both of those facts are easy to misread if you have not already worked through the basic-versus-preferred framework from the previous lesson. This lesson applies that framework to one real posting, clause by clause, and uses it to introduce a distinction — what a posting enumerates versus what it leaves as an unstated "related field" — that matters for every level this module covers after this one.

Postings like this one usually carry a tag identifying the hiring cycle and location they are attached to — here, "(26/27, Starlink, Redmond WA)." That tag is worth reading as carefully as the qualifications themselves, because it tells you something the qualifications section does not: which candidates this specific requisition is actually trying to reach, and where.

## What the tag means

"26/27" marks a graduating-class window: candidates who will complete their degree in the 2026 or 2027 academic cycle. Companies that hire in volume from universities commonly run this kind of new-grad requisition against a specific cohort window rather than leaving it open-ended, because the hiring cycle itself — campus recruiting, cohort start dates, onboarding built around a class of incoming graduates — runs on an annual or twice-yearly rhythm. "Starlink" names the program the role sits inside, and "Redmond, WA" names the site — SpaceX's Starlink engineering location, distinct from the company's Hawthorne, California headquarters, where a different set of GNC role families are based. None of this changes what the qualifications section requires. It changes who should expect to find this specific requisition relevant: someone graduating outside that window, or aiming at a different program or site entirely, is not excluded by the qualifications text, but is very likely looking at the wrong posting for their situation and should expect a different, contemporaneous requisition to be the better match.

## The basic qualifications, clause by clause

The stated line is:

> Bachelor's degree in computer science, aerospace, physics, or an engineering discipline; software development experience in either C++ or Python.

Two clauses, joined by a semicolon, each doing independent work.

The degree clause names four fields explicitly: computer science, aerospace, physics, and, more broadly, "an engineering discipline" — which covers mechanical, electrical, systems, and the rest of the conventional engineering majors alongside aerospace specifically. Notice what this clause does not contain: the word "related." It does not ask you to judge whether your major is close enough to some unstated category. It enumerates the category directly. This is worth dwelling on, because the generic posting language many candidates brace themselves against — "or related field" — pushes an interpretation problem onto the reader that a well-written enumerated list removes entirely. Where a posting lists the fields it accepts, the honest answer to "does my degree count" is almost always answerable by reading the list, not by guessing at what "related" was meant to include.

The second clause — "software development experience in either C++ or Python" — carries no stated number of years and no word like "professional" in front of "experience." That absence is doing real work. Compare it to a line this module covers in the next lesson, which explicitly requires "2+ years of professional experience": that line is unambiguous about wanting a paid, employer-based track record. This line asks only for software development experience in one of two named languages, with nothing said about where that experience needs to come from. A substantial coursework project, a personal project carried through to something working, or an internship are all plausible ways to satisfy a clause phrased this openly — though, as the previous lesson established, an unstated ambiguity like this is exactly the kind of thing worth confirming directly with a recruiter rather than assuming confidently in either direction.

::: key
New Graduate Engineer, GNC — basic qualifications: Bachelor's degree in computer science, aerospace, physics, or an engineering discipline; software development experience in either C++ or Python.
:::

## The preferred qualifications, and what the absent word tells you

The stated preferred line is:

> Master's or PhD in an engineering discipline or physics; background in orbital mechanics, control theory, state estimation, spacecraft systems design, optimization, and software development.

Applying the previous lesson's framework directly: this is a signal, not a gate. A candidate with only a bachelor's degree is not disqualified by this line — the basic qualifications above already established the actual floor, and a graduate degree sits nowhere in them. What the preferred line tells you instead is what a stronger-than-baseline candidate for this specific requisition tends to look like: some formal graduate coursework or research, and exposure — not necessarily professional experience, "background" is a looser word than either "experience" or "professional experience" — across orbital mechanics, control theory, state estimation, spacecraft systems design, optimization, and software development.

There is a real tension worth naming honestly rather than glossing over. A "new graduate" requisition, by its nature, tends to draw a large applicant pool, and in a large pool a preferred qualification that is genuinely optional in its formal function can still end up doing a great deal of practical sorting, because enough applicants show it that a candidate who shows none of it is competing from behind on ranking even though nothing has disqualified them. This is not a special property of this posting — it is a general feature of how ranking signals behave once a pool is large — and it is exactly why the technique the next few lessons build, treating a preferred list as a study target rather than a fixed bar, matters even for qualifications nobody is formally required to meet.

::: key
New Graduate Engineer, GNC — preferred qualifications: Master's or PhD in an engineering discipline or physics; background in orbital mechanics, control theory, state estimation, spacecraft systems design, optimization, and software development.
:::

::: warning A graduate degree in the preferred list is not a hidden requirement
Seeing "Master's or PhD" under preferred qualifications for a role titled "New Graduate Engineer" reads, to some candidates, as evidence that a bachelor's-only application is a waste of time. It is not. The basic qualifications section is where a hard floor would be stated if one existed, and it says nothing about a graduate degree. A bachelor's-only candidate who meets the basic line and shows some of the preferred background is a normal, viable applicant, not a long shot.
:::

::: example A graduating senior checked clause by clause
A student finishing a mechanical engineering degree has taken one controls course, built a flight-computer simulation in Python for a university rocketry team over two semesters, and has no paid engineering internship. Checked against the basic qualifications: the degree clause is satisfied — mechanical engineering is squarely "an engineering discipline." The software clause is satisfied too, since the rocketry-team simulation work is genuine software development experience in Python, and the basic line never asked for it to be paid or supervised by an employer.

Checked against the preferred qualifications: no graduate degree, so that half of the first clause is unmet. Against the background list, the one controls course gives partial coverage of control theory, and the simulation project gives real coverage of software development, but orbital mechanics, state estimation, spacecraft systems design, and optimization are all thin or absent. The honest read is a candidate who clears the actual floor comfortably and shows a genuine, if partial, match on the signal that follows it — which is a normal, competitive starting position for this posting, not a deficient one.
:::

::: example A year past graduation, wondering which posting actually fits
A candidate finished a physics degree the previous year, has since completed a master's degree, and has no paid engineering role yet — only the research assistantship that came with the graduate program. They are unsure whether "New Graduate Engineer" still describes them, since they are not graduating this year, or whether they should instead be reading the Level I to II posting this module covers next.

Nothing in the basic qualifications text above settles this by itself — the posting's degree and software clauses would very likely be satisfied by this candidate either way, and the "(26/27)" tag suggests the new-grad requisition is aimed at people finishing their degree in that specific window rather than someone a year removed from theirs. The honest answer is that this is a genuinely ambiguous fit, of the same shape this curriculum's earlier module described for an unrelated ambiguity: the useful move is not to resolve it alone by re-reading the posting for the tenth time, but to look at both live postings, apply to whichever is the closer current match, and let a recruiter conversation settle a fact pattern that the text alone was never going to settle definitively.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
State the New Graduate Engineer, GNC basic qualification exactly, and identify the two independent clauses within it.
:::

::: answer
The basic qualification is: "Bachelor's degree in computer science, aerospace, physics, or an engineering discipline; software development experience in either C++ or Python." The two clauses, joined by a semicolon, are the degree requirement (one of four named fields) and the software-development-experience requirement (in one of two named languages), and each must be satisfied independently.
:::

::: check
Why doesn't the software-development clause in this posting carry a stated number of years or the word "professional," and what does that imply about what can satisfy it?
:::

::: answer
The clause asks only for "software development experience in either C++ or Python," with no year count and no qualifier restricting it to paid or employer-based work. That absence implies a genuinely broader range of evidence can plausibly satisfy it — substantial coursework, a personal project carried to a working result, or an internship — since the posting never narrowed the source of the experience the way a "professional experience" line would.
:::

::: check
Explain why this posting's degree clause, which names four specific fields, functions differently than a posting that instead states "BS in Aerospace or related."
:::

::: answer
An enumerated list removes the interpretation problem a vaguer clause creates. "Or related" asks the reader to judge, without guidance, whether their field counts as close enough to aerospace — a judgment call with no stated boundary. Naming computer science, aerospace, physics, and "an engineering discipline" directly answers the question for the large majority of candidates by listing what counts, leaving genuine ambiguity only at the true edges of "an engineering discipline" rather than across an entire unstated category.
:::

::: check
A candidate holds a mathematics degree, which is not among the four fields named in the basic qualification, but has strong software and controls-adjacent project experience. Evaluate their fit against the basic qualification as written, without assuming a generous reading that the text does not support.
:::

::: answer
A mathematics degree is not computer science, aerospace, physics, or, on an ordinary reading, "an engineering discipline" — mathematics departments are conventionally housed and credentialed separately from engineering ones. Taken at face value, the stated basic qualification does not include this degree, regardless of how strong the candidate's project experience is, since the degree clause and the software clause are independent and a strong showing on one does not substitute for the other. This is a case where the posting's text is not ambiguous the way a borderline years-of-experience line would be; a direct question to a recruiter is still worthwhile, but it should be asked expecting a genuine no, not a formality.
:::

::: check
Why might the preferred qualifications end up mattering more in practice for a new-graduate requisition than their formally optional status suggests?
:::

::: answer
A "new graduate" requisition typically draws from an entire graduating cohort, producing a comparatively large applicant pool. In a large pool, a qualification that is formally a ranking signal rather than a gate can still end up doing substantial practical sorting, because enough applicants will show some of it that a candidate showing none of it is starting from behind on relative standing, even though nothing about their application has been disqualified. The qualification's formal function and its practical weight in a specific, high-volume pool are not the same thing.
:::
