Picture an after-school robotics club open to "grades 7 to 8." There is one sign-up sheet and one set of rules to join. But once you are in, the coach puts you on the beginner team or the advanced team, depending on what you show in the first few weeks. One door, two possible places inside.

The GNC Engineer, Level I to II posting works like that. It is what most people picture as "an ordinary GNC engineering job." It is not the new-graduate program from the previous lesson, and not the senior role two lessons ahead. It is the main requisition for someone who has already spent real time doing this kind of work. For most readers of this module, this is where a sentence like "Engineer II — 2+ years' experience, BS in Aerospace or related" stops being a made-up example and becomes the real line on a real careers page.

This lesson takes that line apart, the same way the previous lesson took apart the new-grad posting. Two things are new here:

- a **years-of-experience threshold** — the number people argue with themselves about most in this whole module;
- a title, "Level I to II," that names **one posted floor that can lead to either of two internal levels**.

## The exact line, and what "Level I to II" means

The stated basic qualification is:

> Bachelor's degree in physics or an engineering discipline, plus 2+ years of professional experience in control systems, orbital mechanics, classical dynamics, aerodynamics, sensors and actuators, modeling and simulation, and the software languages C++ and Python.

"Level I to II" is read aloud "level one to two." It names a single requisition that can end in a hire at either of two **[[internal levels|levels-ladder]]**, depending on how strong a case the candidate shows.

That is different from the new-grad posting, which hires into one level. It is different again from the senior posting, which is a separate requisition of its own.

The basic qualification above is the floor for the whole posting — the least a candidate must show to be considered for *either* level. It is not a lower bar for "I" and a secret higher bar for "II." Where you land inside the band is decided by the strength of the case you show through the interview **loop** (the full set of interviews), not by a second, unstated list you are supposed to guess.

## The degree clause: narrower than it first looks

Read the degree clause closely: "physics or an engineering discipline." Now compare the new-grad posting, which named four things: computer science, aerospace, physics, and "an engineering discipline." This line names only two: physics, and the general category of engineering disciplines.

**Aerospace** is not named, but it does not need to be. Aerospace engineering is plainly an engineering discipline, so an aerospace degree is fully covered.

**Computer science** is a different case. Depending on the university, a CS program might sit inside a college of engineering, a college of science, or a school of its own. "An engineering discipline" does not have one single answer for every CS program everywhere — and program **[[accreditation|accreditation]]** does not settle it either.

So be honest about it. A CS graduate applying to this posting is on less certain ground for the degree clause than the same person applying to the new-grad posting, where computer science is named outright. Nothing in the posting text resolves that. Inventing a confident answer would be worse than admitting the honest one: this is a borderline reading worth raising directly with a recruiter, not assuming either way.

::: warning "Or related" is doing no work here, because the sentence does not use it
This degree clause is not written "physics, engineering, or related field." It names two categories. Treat it that way. A physics or engineering degree checks the box cleanly. A degree outside both is not rescued by an "or related" reading the sentence never offers, however similar the field may feel.
:::

### What is missing: no in-lieu route

Notice one more thing the line does *not* contain. There is no clause like "or X years of experience in lieu of a degree" — *in lieu of* meaning "in place of." At this level, the bachelor's degree is part of the floor, with no stated substitute. Two other postings in this module do offer such a route; lessons five and six cover them. Here, no amount of experience or preferred-list strength stands in for the degree.

## The years clause: what "professional" is doing

"2+ years of professional experience" does two jobs in five words.

- The **number** sets a floor: at least two years.
- The word **professional** limits where those years can come from.

Compare the new-grad posting's looser "software development experience," which had no such word. The contrast is deliberate. This line wants a paid, employer-based record — not a personal project or coursework, however strong.

Two real questions live inside the word "professional." The posting text does not settle either one, and the answer can vary by employer and by whoever reads your résumé.

1. **Does an [[internship|internship]] count?** Across the industry, internship time is commonly read as professional experience when the work was professional in character — real assigned work on real systems, under real supervision. It is not automatically in or out because of the title "intern."
2. **Does part-time count the same as full-time?** Usually it is **[[prorated|prorating]]** in some way — counted at a fraction — rather than counted in full or thrown out. Exactly how is a question for the recruiter screen, not something to assume from outside.

What is *not* unclear is the floor itself. Two years is the stated number. Read it as a real number, not a hope that a strong enough story can talk its way around.

## The named areas: one cluster, not seven clocks

After "professional experience," the clause lists seven items:

1. control systems
2. orbital mechanics
3. classical dynamics
4. aerodynamics
5. sensors and actuators
6. modeling and simulation
7. the software languages C++ and Python

Read naturally, this is **one** two-year requirement spread across a **[[cluster|one-cluster]]** of related areas that GNC work touches. It is not seven separate two-year requirements. Read that way, it would ask for $7 \times 2 = 14$ years from someone applying to an early-career posting — which makes no sense.

Nobody arrives with two full years in each area. Picture a candidate whose two years went mostly to sensor and actuator integration, with lighter but real exposure to control systems and simulation, and daily use of both languages. That candidate is reading the clause correctly, not falling short of it.

This matters because reading the list as seven separate demands is a common way for a qualified candidate to talk themselves out of applying. The named areas describe the *shape* of the work at this level — the shape the previous module on role families laid out program by program — not a checklist graded line by line.

The two languages, though, are named on purpose. The basic line asks for **C++ and Python** by name. Other tools, such as **[[MATLAB|matlab]]**, may show up elsewhere in some postings, but they are not what this line names.

## The Dragon variant, and what a variant tells you

The module adds a second number next to the general one. The same posting, run for the **[[Dragon|dragon]]** program, has been seen stating **1+ years** instead of 2+.

Take this seriously as a fact about how postings work in general, not only as a number. A basic-qualification threshold is set **per posting** — for one program's hiring need at one moment. It is not one fixed number stamped across the whole company. A number that held for one program's requisition six months ago does not guarantee what another program's requisition, or the same program's requisition today, says.

So the only reliable source for the exact number right now is the **[[live posting|live-posting]]** itself. What this lesson can responsibly tell you is that the number moves, and roughly how far it has been seen to move — not that it is fixed.

::: key
GNC Engineer Level I to II — basic qualifications: Bachelor's degree in physics or an engineering discipline, plus 2+ years of professional experience in control systems, orbital mechanics, classical dynamics, aerodynamics, sensors and actuators, modeling and simulation, and the software languages C++ and Python. The Dragon variant has been seen at 1+ years. There is no in-lieu-of-degree clause at this level.
:::

::: key
"Level I to II" names a single posted floor that can result in a hire at either of two internal levels. It is not two separate qualification lists; which level a candidate lands at is decided by the strength of the case shown through the loop.
:::

::: example An electrical engineer, checked clause by clause
A candidate has an electrical engineering degree. They have spent two and a half years at a small aerospace supplier, building and testing electronics for a satellite's attitude-control system — the part that points the satellite. Their work: wiring in sensors, the electronics that drive actuators, and test benches written mostly in Python. In the last several months they have started writing C++ as well.

- **Degree clause:** electrical engineering is plainly an engineering discipline. Met, no doubt.
- **Years clause:** 2.5 years of professional experience against a floor of 2. Since $2.5 - 2 = 0.5$, they clear it by six months. Met.
- **The cluster, area by area:**
  - sensors and actuators — strong, direct match;
  - control systems, modeling and simulation — present but lighter;
  - orbital mechanics, classical dynamics — mostly absent, because the work was at the subsystem level, not the trajectory level;
  - aerodynamics — absent, as you would expect for spacecraft electronics;
  - languages — Python strong, C++ real but newer.

Read as one cluster, this is a normal, viable candidate: strong in some areas, thinner in others. That is the ordinary shape of a real career, not a disqualifying gap.

Sanity check: which line is the honest weak point? Not orbital mechanics — the cluster never demanded every area. It is the newer C++, because C++ is one of the two languages the line names outright.
:::

::: example A computer-science graduate, testing the unclear clause
A candidate has a computer science degree from a university where CS sits inside the college of engineering. They have three years of professional experience writing state-estimation and control software for a company that builds ground robots. They use C++ and Python daily. They have no aerospace experience.

- **Years clause:** 3 years against a floor of 2. Met, with a year to spare.
- **The cluster:** control systems and modeling and simulation are squarely covered. Met.
- **Languages:** both, daily. Met.
- **Degree clause:** this is the open question. "An engineering discipline" plausibly covers a CS program housed in an engineering college. But the posting does not say so. The same person, with a CS degree from a science school, would be on shakier ground on paper — with identical skills.

Conclusion: not a confident yes or no. Apply, and expect the degree clause to be exactly where a recruiter conversation is needed.

Sanity check: is robotics instead of spaceflight a problem? Nothing in the cluster limits *where* the experience was earned. Control software is control software; the clause names areas, not industries.
:::

## Summary

::: context accreditation What accreditation means
**Accreditation** is an outside check that a degree program meets agreed standards. In the US, the main accreditor for engineering programs is ABET (said "A-bet"). ABET also accredits computing programs, including many in computer science — but through a separate commission from the one for engineering.

So "Is my CS degree accredited?" and "Is CS an engineering discipline?" are different questions. The posting asks the second, and does not say how it decides. That is why this lesson sends the question to a recruiter rather than answering it.
:::

::: context internship What an internship is
An **internship** is a short, usually paid job for a student — often a summer, sometimes a semester — doing real work on a real team. At engineering companies interns are often given a genuine project with a deadline and a mentor.

Whether internship months count toward "professional experience" depends on who reads your résumé. List them honestly with their dates and what you actually built, so the reader can judge. Do not blend them into a full-time job's dates.
:::

::: context prorating How prorating works
To **prorate** is to count something in proportion. One common way to prorate part-time work is by hours. A job at 20 hours a week, against a 40-hour full-time week, counts at

$$
\frac{20}{40} = 0.5
$$

of full time. So twelve months of it would count as about $12 \times 0.5 = 6$ months of full-time experience.

That is one reasonable method, not a rule any posting states. Some readers may weigh part-time work differently. Write down your real hours and dates, and let the recruiter apply their own method.
:::

::: context matlab Where MATLAB fits
**MATLAB** is a commercial tool for numerical computing, long used in university control and signal courses; **Simulink**, its companion, builds simulations out of connected blocks. Some postings mention MATLAB for high-fidelity simulation work, but it is secondary. The basic line names C++ and Python — which is why this course leans on those two rather than starting from MATLAB.
:::

::: context dragon What Dragon is
**Dragon** is SpaceX's capsule. Cargo Dragon carries supplies to the International Space Station, and Crew Dragon has carried astronauts there since 2020. GNC work on Dragon includes steering the capsule to meet the station and dock with it — called rendezvous and proximity operations — and bringing it home through the atmosphere to a splashdown under parachutes.

A lower years number on a Dragon posting says something about that team's need at that moment. It is not a permanent rule for the program.
:::

::: context live-posting Save the posting you applied to
Postings change: numbers move, lines are added, a requisition closes and a new one opens. A practical habit is to save a copy of the full text of every posting you apply to — the date, the title, and both qualification sections — in your own notes.

This is also the first exercise of this module: copy the basic qualifications of several live postings word for word. Doing it trains you to read the exact wording instead of your memory of it.
:::
