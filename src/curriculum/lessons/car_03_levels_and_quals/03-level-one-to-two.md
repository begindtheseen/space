This is the posting most people mean when they picture "an ordinary GNC engineering job" — not the new-graduate program from the previous lesson, and not the senior role two lessons ahead, but the mainline requisition for someone who has already spent some real time doing this kind of work and is ready to do more of it as a full engineer. It is also, for most readers of this module, the posting where "Engineer II — 2+ years' experience, BS in Aerospace or related" stops being a hypothetical example and becomes the actual sentence they are reading on a real careers page, with a real number in it and a real degree clause to check themselves against.

This lesson takes that sentence apart the way the previous two lessons took apart the New Graduate posting, with two things added that make this one worth its own lesson: a years-of-experience threshold, which is the single most negotiated-over number in this whole module, and a title — "Level I to II" — that names something this module has not yet explained: a posted floor that can land a candidate at either of two internal levels, not just one.

## The exact line, and what "Level I to II" means

The stated basic qualification is:

> Bachelor's degree in physics or an engineering discipline, plus 2+ years of professional experience in control systems, orbital mechanics, classical dynamics, aerodynamics, sensors and actuators, modeling and simulation, and the software languages C++ and Python.

A posting titled "Level I to II" is naming a single requisition that can result in a hire at either of two internal levels, depending on how strong the case a candidate presents turns out to be. This is different from the New Graduate posting, which effectively hires into one level, and different again from the senior posting two lessons ahead, which is its own separate requisition entirely. The basic qualification above is the floor for the posting as a whole — the minimum a candidate must show to be considered for either internal level — not a separate, lower bar for "I" and a separate, higher bar for "II." Where a specific candidate lands inside that band is decided by the strength of the case shown through the loop, not by a second, unstated qualifications list you are supposed to guess at.

## The degree clause: narrower than it first looks

Read the degree clause closely: "physics or an engineering discipline." Compare it against the New Graduate posting's degree clause from the previous lesson, which named four things explicitly — computer science, aerospace, physics, and "an engineering discipline." This line names only two: physics, and the general category of engineering disciplines.

Aerospace engineering is unambiguously an engineering discipline, so a candidate with an aerospace degree is squarely covered even though the word "aerospace" does not appear here by name. Computer science is a genuinely different case. Depending on the university, a computer science program sits inside a college of engineering, a college of science, or its own separate school, and "an engineering discipline" is not a phrase with one universal, institution-independent answer for every CS program everywhere. This is worth stating plainly rather than smoothing over: a computer-science-degree candidate targeting this specific posting is in a less textually certain position for the degree clause than the same candidate would be applying to the New Graduate posting, where computer science is named outright. Nothing in the text this module has access to resolves that uncertainty definitively one way or the other — and inventing a confident answer here would be worse than admitting the honest one, which is that this is exactly the kind of borderline reading worth raising directly with a recruiter rather than assuming either way.

::: warning "Or related" is doing no work here, because the sentence doesn't use it
This posting's degree clause is not phrased as "physics, engineering, or related field." It is phrased as two named categories. Treat it accordingly: a physics or engineering degree checks the box cleanly; a degree outside both categories is not covered by an implied "or related" reading the sentence never actually offers, no matter how analytically similar the field might feel.
:::

## The years clause: what "professional" is doing

"2+ years of professional experience" is doing two jobs in five words. The number sets a floor; the word "professional" restricts where that floor can be filled from. Read against the New Graduate posting's looser "software development experience," with no such qualifier, the contrast is deliberate: this line wants a paid, employer-based track record, not a personal project or coursework, however strong.

Two genuinely open questions live inside that word, and this module will not invent confident answers to either, because the text alone does not settle them and the specifics plausibly vary by employer and by the person reading a given résumé. Does an internship count as "professional" experience? Commonly, across the industry, internship time is read as professional experience when the work itself was genuinely professional in character — real assigned work on real systems, under real supervision — rather than automatically excluded or automatically included by title alone. Does a part-time role count the same as full-time? Typically it is prorated in some fashion rather than counted or discarded outright, though exactly how is, again, a recruiter-screen question rather than something to assume from outside. What is not ambiguous is the floor itself: two years is the stated number, and a candidate should read it as a real number, not as an aspirational one a strong enough case can talk its way around.

## The named technical areas: one cluster, not seven separate clocks

The clause after "professional experience" lists seven things: control systems, orbital mechanics, classical dynamics, aerodynamics, sensors and actuators, modeling and simulation, and the two languages C++ and Python. Read naturally, this is one two-year professional-experience requirement spanning a cluster of related technical areas that a GNC role touches — not seven independent two-year requirements that would, read literally, demand fourteen years of experience from someone applying to an entry-to-mid-level posting. Nobody arrives with two full years in every one of these areas individually; a candidate whose two years were spent mostly on sensor and actuator integration work, with lighter but real exposure to control systems and simulation, and daily use of both languages, is reading the clause correctly, not falling short of it.

This matters because misreading the clause as seven separate demands is a genuinely common way for a qualified candidate to talk themselves out of applying. The named areas describe the shape of the work a GNC engineer at this level actually does — a shape this curriculum's earlier module on role families already laid out program by program — not a checklist graded item by item.

## The Dragon variant, and what a variant tells you

The module places a second number alongside the general one: the same posting, run against the Dragon program specifically, has been seen stating 1+ years rather than 2+. This is worth taking seriously as a fact about how these postings work generally, beyond the specific number: a basic-qualification threshold is set per posting, for a specific program's specific hiring need at a specific point in time, not as one fixed constant stamped across the whole company. A number that held for one program's requisition six months ago is not a guarantee about what a different program's requisition, or the same program's requisition today, currently states. The only reliable source for the exact number on offer right now is the live posting itself — everything this lesson can responsibly tell you is that the number moves, and roughly by how much it has been seen to move, not that it is fixed.

::: key
GNC Engineer, Level I to II — basic qualifications: Bachelor's degree in physics or an engineering discipline, plus 2+ years of professional experience in control systems, orbital mechanics, classical dynamics, aerodynamics, sensors and actuators, modeling and simulation, and the software languages C++ and Python. A Dragon-program variant has been seen stating 1+ years instead of 2+.
:::

::: key
"Level I to II" names a single posted floor that can result in a hire at either of two internal levels; it is not two separate qualification lists, and which level a candidate lands at is decided by the strength of the case shown through the loop.
:::

::: example An electrical engineer, checked clause by clause
A candidate holds an electrical engineering degree and has spent two and a half years at a small aerospace supplier, building and testing avionics interfaces for a satellite attitude-control subsystem — sensor integration, actuator drive electronics, and test benches written mostly in Python, with growing but newer C++ work in the last several months. Checked against the degree clause: electrical engineering is squarely an engineering discipline, no ambiguity. Checked against the years clause: two and a half years of professional experience clears the stated 2+ floor. Checked against the technical-area cluster: sensors and actuators is a strong, direct match; control systems and modeling and simulation are present but lighter; orbital mechanics and classical dynamics are largely absent, since the work was subsystem-level rather than trajectory-level; Python is strong, C++ is real but newer.

Read as one cluster rather than seven separate boxes, this is a normal, viable candidate for the posting — strong in some named areas, thinner in others, which is the ordinary shape of a real career rather than a disqualifying gap. The honest weak point worth naming plainly, if this candidate were doing their own self-assessment, is the newer C++ experience, not the absent orbital-mechanics background.
:::

::: example A computer-science graduate, testing the genuinely ambiguous clause
A candidate holds a computer science degree from a university where the CS program sits inside the college of engineering, and has three years of professional experience writing state-estimation and control software at a ground-robotics company — squarely inside "control systems" and "modeling and simulation," with strong C++ and Python from daily use, though no aerospace-specific experience at all. The years clause and the technical-area cluster are comfortably met. The degree clause is the genuinely open question this lesson raised above: "an engineering discipline" plausibly covers a CS program housed within an engineering college, but the posting's text does not say so explicitly, and a CS program at a different university, housed in a science school, would leave the same candidate in a more uncertain position on paper despite an identical technical background.

The honest conclusion is not a confident yes or no — it is that this candidate should apply, expecting the degree clause to be exactly where a recruiter conversation is needed, and should not assume the robotics-industry origin of their control-systems experience is itself a problem: nothing in the technical-area clause restricts the domain the experience was earned in to spaceflight specifically.
:::

::: check

:::

::: answer
It means one posted basic-qualification floor covers a requisition that can result in a hire at either of two internal levels, with the specific level decided by the strength of the case a candidate presents through the loop, not by a separate, unstated qualifications list for each level. A posting that hires into a single fixed level, like the New Graduate posting, has no such internal split — everyone who clears its floor is being considered for the same level.
:::

::: check
Explain why reading the seven named technical areas and languages as seven independent two-year requirements is a misreading of the clause.
:::

::: answer
The clause states one "2+ years of professional experience" floor that spans a cluster of related technical areas describing the shape of GNC work generally, not seven separate two-year requirements that would literally demand fourteen years from an entry-to-mid-level candidate. A candidate with two years of experience distributed unevenly across the named areas — strong in some, lighter in others — is reading and meeting the clause correctly; treating it as seven independent clocks is a common way a qualified candidate wrongly rules themselves out.
:::

::: check
A candidate has a computer science degree and is evaluating whether it satisfies this posting's degree clause. Why can't this module give a definitive yes or no, and what is the honest way to handle that uncertainty?
:::

::: answer
The clause names "physics or an engineering discipline" without listing computer science explicitly, and whether a given CS program counts as an engineering discipline depends on institutional facts — which college or school houses that specific program — that vary by university and are not stated anywhere in the posting's text. Because the text itself does not resolve this, the honest approach is to apply and ask the recruiter directly rather than either assuming automatic inclusion or ruling the case out entirely from outside.
:::

::: check
Why does the existence of the lower-threshold Dragon variant matter for how you should treat years-of-experience numbers across postings generally, beyond this specific posting?
:::

::: answer
It shows that a basic-qualification threshold is set per posting, for a specific program at a specific point in time, rather than fixed as one company-wide constant. A number seen on one program's requisition, or seen at an earlier point in time, is not a reliable stand-in for what a different program's current, live posting actually states — the only dependable source for the number that applies right now is that live posting itself.
:::

::: check
What is the practical difference, for this posting's years clause, between "professional experience" and a strong personal project carried out independently?
:::

::: answer
The clause explicitly requires "professional experience," which restricts the floor to paid, employer-based work rather than experience earned any way at all. A strong personal project may be genuinely persuasive evidence of skill and could matter elsewhere in an application — and, as the next lesson shows, some preferred-qualification language explicitly welcomes project experience — but it does not, by the wording of this specific basic-qualification clause, satisfy the stated "2+ years of professional experience" floor on its own.
:::

## Summary

| Clause | What it requires | Notable ambiguity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Degree | Physics or an engineering discipline | Whether a specific computer science program counts depends on the institution |
| Years | 2+ years of professional experience (1+ years seen for a Dragon variant) | The exact threshold is set per posting and per program; verify on the live requisition |
| Technical areas | Control systems, orbital mechanics, classical dynamics, aerodynamics, sensors and actuators, modeling and simulation | Read as one cluster the two years spans, not seven separate two-year requirements |
