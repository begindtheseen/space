Imagine signing up for swimming lessons. The pool puts you in a group — beginner, intermediate or advanced — after watching you swim one length. Get put in the beginner group when you can already swim well, and you spend the whole term bored, doing drills you mastered years ago. Moving up means waiting for the next test. Sign up for advanced when you cannot yet float, and you are turned away at the edge of the pool on day one. Both mistakes are real. Only one of them is loud.

Seven lessons ago this module opened with a sentence — "Engineer II — 2+ years' experience, BS in Aerospace or related" — and a promise that you would learn to read every clause in it precisely. Which parts are hard filters. Which are shorthand for a later judgment. Which leave a hiring manager real room for discretion. You now have all of that, posting by posting: New Graduate Engineer, Level I to II, Sr. GNC Engineer, the specialist roles, and Site Reliability Engineer, GNC.

Now for the question the module has been building toward. Given everything you now know, which **[[level|engineering-levels]]** — which rung on a company's ladder of job grades — should you actually apply to? And how do you find that out honestly instead of guessing?

This lesson first explains how a level gets decided, and why that happens *before* an offer. Then it gives a method for an honest self-assessment, so "which level should I target?" becomes an answer you can defend.

## How a level gets decided

An honest limit first. This module's source material does not describe any one employer's internal method for deciding a level, and this lesson will not invent one. What it can describe is how leveled hiring generally works at employers with numbered or named engineering levels, as this role family has.

Your level is not read off one number. It comes from the **whole case** you present:

- your degree;
- your years of experience;
- what that experience actually contained, measured against the posting's named technical areas;
- and, once the **[[interview loop|interview-loop]]** is underway, the depth and ownership evidence the previous lesson described.

A résumé gets a first level read at screening, against the basic-qualification lines. That read is not necessarily final. It is common for a candidate to be moved up or down a level as the loop goes on, depending on how the technical and behavioral rounds actually go.

What this module cannot tell you is the exact weighting any one employer's scoring uses, or who makes the final call. Those details vary by employer, are rarely published, and change over time. The safe general shape is this: **leveling is a judgment built from the evidence of the whole process, not a single gate at a single stage.**

## Why level is set before the offer

Think of a school's grade levels and its reading lists. Once you are placed in seventh grade, you can pick different books from the seventh-grade list. But which list you get was decided by the placement, not by the books you ask for.

Pay works the same way. Generally, pay for a role is structured around a range attached to a level, called a **[[band|pay-band]]** — the lowest to highest pay that level allows. It is not invented from nothing for each person. Once your level is set, negotiation happens *inside* that band. That is a real move, but a bounded one.

The level itself is generally not reopened after an offer. It was never reached by negotiation in the first place. It came out of the whole evaluation the loop already ran.

Fixing a level after you are hired is a different act. It runs through the employer's own **[[promotion process|promotion-process]]**, on its own schedule and its own standard of evidence, and it needs a fresh case built from inside the job. No timeline for that internal process appears anywhere in this module's source material, and none should be assumed. The structural fact holds without a number: the first leveling decision and a later promotion are not the same act repeated. The first is a single, outside, whole-case judgment. The second is a separate internal process with its own bar to clear.

::: key
Level is decided before the offer because the band is attached to the level, not to the candidate. Negotiating the number inside a band moves you a little; being placed at the right level moves you a lot, and that placement is driven by the evidence presented during the loop. Fixing it later runs through a separate internal promotion process, not a repeat of the hiring judgment.
:::

## Aiming too low can hurt as much as aiming too high

**Aiming too high** fails loudly. A basic-qualification line goes unmet, and in the ordinary case the application ends at the earliest, cheapest stage — the outcome the first lesson of this module described. That hurts, but you can see it — and if you read the posting carefully, you usually know which line caused it.

**Aiming too low** fails quietly, and quiet is what makes it dangerous. Nothing trips a filter. You clear the stated bar easily, the application moves along, and nothing visibly goes wrong. The cost shows up in three other places:

1. **Fixing it is slow.** Correcting an under-placement runs through the promotion path above, not the one whole-picture evaluation you could have had from outside.
2. **The work shrinks to fit.** The work you are given tends to be sized to the level you were hired at, not to your real ability. Showing "complex problems with little to no supervision" at a higher level needs work of that shape, which a lower-level role produces less readily.
3. **The mismatch shows.** A case far stronger than the level you applied for can itself read as a mismatch in the loop. Where the employer supports it, that may prompt a conversation about leveling you up. Where it does not, you were never judged at the level your case actually deserved.

None of this makes "aim high" the safer instinct — the loud failure is the more immediate cost of the two. The honest target is **the level your evidence actually supports**, read the way this module taught you to read it. Guessing low to feel safe is still guessing.

::: warning "Aim low to be safe" is not actually the safe choice
Because a too-high mismatch is visible and a too-low mismatch is not, it is tempting to treat the second as free. It is not. It is a cost that arrives later, more slowly, through a mechanism harder to reverse than the original decision would have been. Weigh both directions as real risks — not one obvious risk and one non-issue.
:::

## An honest self-assessment method

The method is easy to state and easy to do badly if you are not strict with yourself.

**Step 1 — the basic matrix.** Pick one specific target posting. Build a **[[matrix|matrix-grid]]**: a grid with one row per basic-qualification clause. Rate each row **met**, **partially met**, or **not met**, and write the specific evidence behind the rating.

**Step 2 — be strict about "partially met."** It requires an actual **[[artifact|artifact-word]]**: a specific project, a specific job responsibility, a graded course — something a stranger could ask you to describe in detail. An intention is not evidence. "I plan to learn C++ properly this year" may be sincere, but letting it count as "partially met" is the most common way this exercise quietly lies to the person doing it.

**Step 3 — the preferred list.** Do the same for the preferred qualifications, using the technique from lessons four and seven: for each item, the one question an interviewer could plausibly ask, and an honest self-rating against it.

**Step 4 — name the line.** Finish with the single most useful thing the exercise produces. Name the *one* basic-qualification line most likely to end this specific application today — not a vague feeling of being underprepared. A gap you can name precisely is a gap you can plan around. A general sense of "not ready yet" is not.

::: key
An honest self-assessment matrix rates each basic-qualification clause as met, partially met, or not met, with a specific artifact as evidence for anything short of fully met — and ends by naming the single line most likely to disqualify the application today, rather than a general impression of readiness.
:::

## What can stand in for stated experience, and what cannot

Across the six postings, three real substitutes for stated professional experience showed up.

**Demonstrated project work** counts explicitly where a posting says so. The GNC Engineer preferred list's closing clause, from lesson four, names "demonstrated project or professional experience" outright. It strengthens a *preferred* showing. Nowhere in this module did it replace a *basic* qualification's years-of-experience line.

**Adjacent-domain professional experience** means controls, estimation or software work earned outside spaceflight — in robotics, cars, defense work, or factory process control. It plausibly earns real credit against the technical-area cluster from lesson three, because that cluster reads naturally as one overall requirement, not seven separate locks tied to space. That is a common industry reading, not a confirmed fact about any one employer's recruiters.

**Internal mobility** — moving between teams inside the same company — came up honestly in the lesson on Site Reliability Engineer, GNC. Many employers support it in some form. This module has no company-specific detail about how it works here or on what timeline. And it is explicitly *not* a way, from outside, to swap one role's basic qualifications for another's.

**Where nothing substitutes** deserves saying as plainly. At postings with no in-lieu clause — New Graduate Engineer and Level I to II — the stated degree requirement is a floor with no written alternative. A strong preferred showing does not override an unmet basic one. The only two in-lieu-of-degree doors this module found are Sr. GNC Engineer's 7+ years and the SRE role's 4+ years. And beneath all of it sits the most absolute "nothing substitutes" in the whole hiring picture, from this curriculum's first career module: a **[[legal eligibility|legal-eligibility]]** requirement that no hiring manager has the standing to set aside for one candidate they like.

::: warning A strong preferred showing does not rescue a failed basic qualification
This is the misreading this whole module was built to prevent, so here it is one last time: preferred qualifications never override a basic one. If your honest self-assessment marks a basic-qualification line as unmet, no pile of preferred-list strength changes the outcome for that posting.
:::

::: example A full self-assessment matrix, worked end to end
A candidate has a physics degree. She has spent two years at a defense contractor, doing **[[sensor-fusion|sensor-fusion]]** work for a guided-systems program — professional, full-time and squarely technical. Her Python is strong from daily use. Her C++ is real but newer: mostly written in the last several months, under a senior engineer's close review.

Check her against the GNC Engineer, Level I to II basic qualifications from lesson three, one row per clause:

| Clause | Rating | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Degree: physics or an engineering discipline | Met | Physics degree |
| 2+ years professional experience | Met | Two years, full-time, at a defense contractor |
| Control systems / sensors and actuators | Met | Core of the sensor-fusion work |
| Orbital mechanics / classical dynamics | Not met | No stated exposure in this role |
| Modeling and simulation | Partially met | Used simulation tools; did not build them from scratch |
| Python | Met | Daily professional use |
| C++ | Partially met | Real but recent, closely supervised |

Count the ratings: 4 met, 2 partially met, 1 not met — 7 rows, one per clause.

Now read it the way lesson three taught, as one cluster rather than seven separate boxes.

- The two lines that act as true filters — degree and years — are met cleanly.
- The technical cluster is uneven but plausible. Orbital mechanics is the one area that is genuinely absent.
- The line likely to be probed hardest is not a missing basic qualification at all. It is the newer C++ experience, sitting in "partially met" rather than "not met."

Sanity check: every "partially met" row names a real artifact (tools used on the job, code written under review), not a plan. That is a specific, useful answer, not a vague worry.
:::

::: example Two candidates, two different ways of missing the level
**Candidate A** has three years of general software engineering experience and no bachelor's degree. He applies directly to Sr. GNC Engineer, reasoning that three years is meaningful experience worth a try. Check him against all three branches from lesson five:

1. Bachelor's plus 5+ years — fails; there is no degree.
2. 7+ years in lieu of a degree — fails; $3 < 7$, four years short.
3. A postgraduate degree — fails; there is none.

All three branches fail, so the application ends at the earliest stage. This is the loud failure — one the self-assessment matrix would have flagged at once.

**Candidate B** has a case that is genuinely competitive for Level I to II: a qualifying degree, two and a half years of directly relevant professional experience since graduating, and solid coverage across the technical cluster. But she applies only to **New Graduate** postings, reasoning that it feels like the safer target.

Nothing fails outright. The application moves along normally. But it is very likely read as a marginal fit for a **[[requisition|requisition]]** aimed at a specific graduating **[[cohort|cohort]]** — not as the strong Level I to II candidate her case would have made her.

No alarm goes off for Candidate B. That is exactly why her mistake is easy to make and easy to miss, until the chance to apply at the right level has passed.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
Describe, in general terms, how a recruiter and a hiring manager arrive at the level they judge a candidate against. State plainly what this module can and cannot say about the method at any one employer.
:::

::: answer
In general, level is set from the whole case a candidate presents — degree, years, experience measured against the posting's named technical areas, and, as the loop goes on, ownership and depth evidence from the interviews. It is not read off one number, and a first read at screening can be revised as the loop continues. This module cannot state the exact weighting or the decision-maker any one employer uses. Those details vary by employer and change over time, so none should be assumed or invented.
:::

::: check
Explain, in your own words, why being placed at the correct level in the first place matters more than negotiating within a pay band.
:::

::: answer
A band is attached to a level, so negotiating within it is a bounded move around a range the level has already fixed. The level itself is set once, from the whole case the hiring loop judged, before any offer exists. Correcting it afterward is not a repeat of that judgment. It is a separate, slower internal promotion process with its own bar of evidence. So being placed correctly the first time reaches further than any negotiation inside a band can.
:::

::: check
Give one concrete cost of aiming too high and one concrete cost of aiming too low when choosing which level to apply for. Explain why the second is easier to overlook.
:::

::: answer
Aiming too high risks an unmet basic-qualification line, which ends the application at the earliest stage — a visible, understandable outcome. Aiming too low risks being placed and given work below what your case could support, with correction running through a slower internal promotion process instead of the original hiring judgment. The second is easier to overlook because nothing trips a filter. The application proceeds, and the cost only shows up after the chance to apply at the right level has passed.
:::

::: check
Name the three things this module found that can substitute for stated professional experience, and the one place it found that nothing substitutes at all.
:::

::: answer
First, demonstrated project work counts explicitly where a posting says it does, as in the GNC Engineer preferred list's closing clause — but only under preferred qualifications, never for a basic qualification's years-of-experience line. Second, adjacent-domain professional experience plausibly earns credit against a technical-area cluster read as a whole, as the Level I to II lesson discussed. The only two stated degree-optional doors in this role family, Sr. GNC Engineer's 7+ years and Site Reliability Engineer, GNC's 4+ years, do not apply there.

::: check
A candidate meets every basic qualification for GNC Engineer, Level I to II, but is strong in only two of the ten items on its preferred list. Using everything from this module, what should they conclude about applying?
:::

::: answer
They should apply. Meeting every basic qualification means the application passes the actual filter — the only thing standing between a candidate and consideration. The preferred list is a ranking signal, not a second gate, and nobody is expected to be strong in all ten items. Two genuine strengths is a normal, honestly partial showing. Present it directly, and treat the other eight items as the study plan for the technical rounds — not as a reason to hold back an application that already clears the real bar.
:::

::: context engineering-levels What engineering levels are
Many engineering employers sort jobs into numbered or named **levels**: something like Engineer I, Engineer II, Senior, and above that titles such as Staff or Principal. The exact names and how many there are differ from company to company, so a "Senior" at one place may match a different title somewhere else. That is why sites such as levels.fyi exist — to line up one company's ladder against another's. Each level carries its own expectations for scope, independence and ownership, which is why the same phrase can mean a bigger story at a higher level.

::: context interview-loop What the interview loop is
The **loop** is the set of interviews a candidate goes through after the first screens — often several conversations with different engineers, sometimes on one long day. You can picture it as a circuit: the candidate goes round a set of interviewers, each testing a different area — technical depth, coding, a past project, how you work with people. At the end, the interviewers compare notes. The level you are placed at comes out of that comparison.
:::

::: context pay-band How pay bands work
A **band** is the range of pay a company allows for one level, from a lowest to a highest number. At many companies, bands for neighboring levels overlap a little.

::: context promotion-process How promotions usually work
At many larger companies, a promotion is not one manager's snap decision. Often your manager builds a written case showing you have been working at the next level for a while, and a group of senior people reviews it alongside other cases. That takes time and a track record inside the job. It is a very different act from an outside hiring loop, which judges your whole case once, before you join.
:::

::: context artifact-word What counts as an artifact
An **artifact** is something a person made that still exists and can be looked at. The word comes from Latin for "made by skill." In engineering it means a tangible output: a code repository, a test report, a simulation you built, a graded project, a design document. The test for this lesson is simple: could a stranger ask you to open it, or walk them through it in detail? If yes, it is evidence. A plan to learn something next year has nothing to open.
:::


::: context legal-eligibility The gate before every other gate
The first career module covered ITAR (said "eye-tar"), the US export-control rules for defense technology, including rockets. Under those rules, sharing controlled technical data with a foreign person needs government permission. So many space-company jobs are open only to **US persons** — US citizens, lawful permanent residents (green-card holders), and certain protected individuals such as people granted asylum or refugee status. That is a law, not a company preference, so no hiring manager can waive it. That is why it sits beneath every qualification line in this module.
:::


::: context sensor-fusion Blending sensors into one answer
**Sensor fusion** means combining readings from several sensors into one estimate that is better than any single sensor alone. A phone does it to show your position: GPS is accurate but slow and can drop out, while motion sensors are fast but drift. Blended, they give a smooth, steady answer. Guided vehicles do the same with GPS, inertial sensors and sometimes cameras — which is why this work maps so directly onto the "sensors and actuators" and filtering lines of a GNC posting.
:::

::: context requisition What a requisition is
A **requisition**, often shortened to "req" (said "reck"), is a company's approved opening for one job: the budget, the level, the team and the posting all tied together. Recruiters talk about "filling a req." A new-graduate requisition is built for people finishing a degree, so someone who graduated years earlier and has built real experience fits it awkwardly, even though they could technically apply.
:::

::: context cohort Where "cohort" comes from
In the Roman army, a **cohort** was a unit of a few hundred soldiers, one tenth of a legion, who trained and marched together. Today the word means any group that starts something at the same time — like everyone graduating in the same year. New-graduate programs hire a cohort each year, which is why the posting names a window like 2026 to 2027.
:::
