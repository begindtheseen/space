Seven lessons ago, this module opened with a sentence — "Engineer II — 2+ years' experience, BS in Aerospace or related" — and a promise that you would learn to read every clause in it precisely: which parts are hard filters, which are shorthand for a later judgment, which leave real room for a hiring manager's discretion, and what the vague ones have actually meant in the specific postings this module examined. You now have all of that, posting by posting, from New Graduate Engineer through Sr. GNC Engineer, the specialist roles, and Site Reliability Engineer, GNC. What remains is the question the whole module has been building toward without yet answering directly: given everything you now know about how these lines work, which level should you actually be applying to, and how do you find that out about yourself honestly rather than by guessing?

This lesson does two things. It explains, in general terms, how a recruiter and a hiring manager actually arrive at the level they evaluate you against, and why that placement is decided before an offer rather than negotiated afterward. Then it gives you a concrete method for running an honest self-assessment against the specific lines this module has covered, so that "which level should I target" stops being a feeling and becomes an answer you can defend.

## How a recruiter and a hiring manager arrive at a level

Nothing in this module's source material describes the specific internal mechanism any one employer uses to decide a candidate's level, and this lesson will not invent one. What can be said accurately is how leveled hiring generally works across employers that use a system of numbered or named engineering levels, which this whole role family clearly does: a candidate's level is not read off a single number in isolation. It comes from the whole case presented — the degree, the years, the specific content of the experience measured against a posting's named technical areas, and, once the loop is underway, the depth and ownership evidence the previous lesson worked through. A résumé gets an initial level read at screening, checked against the basic-qualification lines this module has now walked through for six postings. That read is not necessarily final — it is common for a candidate to be moved up or down a level as the loop proceeds, based on how the technical and behavioral rounds actually go, rather than locked to whatever the first read suggested.

This module cannot tell you the specific weighting any one employer's rubric uses, or who makes the final leveling call. Those specifics vary by employer, are rarely published, and change over time. What is safe to say is the general shape: leveling is a judgment formed from the accumulated evidence of an entire process, not a single gate at a single stage.

## Why level is set before the offer, not after

This module's earlier lessons already established one piece of this precisely: compensation for a role is generally structured around a range attached to a level — a band — rather than negotiated individually, person by person, from nothing. Once a level is set, negotiation happens inside that band's range, which is a real but bounded move. The level itself is generally not something reopened for negotiation after an offer is issued, because it was not arrived at through negotiation in the first place — it was derived from the whole evaluation the loop already ran.

This distinction matters more than it might first appear, because it explains why the two ways of being wrong about your own level — aiming too high or aiming too low — are not mirror images of each other in when they get caught. Being placed at the wrong level is, in the ordinary case, decided once, from outside, before an offer exists at all. Correcting a level after you are already hired is a different act entirely: it generally runs through the employer's own internal promotion process, on its own schedule and its own evidentiary standard, requiring you to build a fresh case for the higher level from within the job itself, rather than the one-time, whole-picture evaluation an external hiring loop performs before you have even started. Nothing in this module's source material states how long that internal process typically takes at any specific employer, and no timeline should be assumed. What is accurate and useful without a specific number is the structural fact itself: the initial leveling decision and a later promotion are not the same kind of act, repeated — the first is a single, external, whole-case judgment; the second is an internal process with its own separate bar to clear.

::: key
Compensation is generally attached to a level, not negotiated from nothing for an individual candidate. Negotiating within a band's range is a real but bounded move; being placed at the correct level in the first place moves you far more, because that placement is set once, from the whole case the loop evaluated, and revisiting it later runs through a separate, internal promotion process rather than a repeat of the original hiring judgment.
:::

## Why aiming too low can be as damaging as aiming too high

Aiming too high has a loud, visible failure mode: a basic-qualification line, of exactly the kind this module has spent six lessons reading precisely, goes unmet, and in the ordinary case the application ends at the earliest, cheapest stage — the outcome the first lesson of this module described in detail. That failure is unpleasant, but it is at least legible: you know it happened, and you generally know which line caused it, provided you read the posting as carefully as this module has taught you to.

Aiming too low has a quiet failure mode, and quiet is what makes it dangerous rather than harmless. Nothing about it trips a filter — you clear the stated bar comfortably, the application proceeds, and there is no moment at which anything visibly goes wrong. The cost shows up elsewhere, in three ways worth naming plainly. First, if you are placed at a level below what your actual case could have supported, correcting that later runs through the slower, harder promotion path described above rather than the single, whole-picture hiring evaluation you could have gone through once, from outside, with nothing yet to unwind. Second, the work you are actually given tends to be scoped to the level you were hired at, not to your underlying ability — and the previous lesson's account of what "complex problems with little to no supervision" actually looks like at a higher level depends on being given work with that shape in the first place. Work scoped to a lower level does not generate that evidence as readily, which can make the case for a later promotion harder to build than it would have been to make correctly the first time. Third, presenting a case far stronger than the level you actually applied for can itself read, in the loop, as a mismatch — prompting a leveling-up conversation where the employer supports one, or, where it does not, simply meaning you were never evaluated at the level your case actually warranted.

None of this means "aim high" is the safer instinct — the first failure mode in this section is exactly what aiming too high risks, and it is the more immediate, visible cost of the two. The honest target is neither reflexive caution nor reflexive ambition: it is the level your evidence, read the way this module has taught you to read it, actually supports. Guessing low to feel safe is still guessing.

::: warning "Aim low to be safe" is not actually the safe choice
Because a mismatch at too high a level is visible and a mismatch at too low a level is not, it is tempting to treat the second as free. It is not free — it is a cost that shows up later, more slowly, and through a harder mechanism to reverse than the original decision would have been. Treat both directions as real risks to weigh, not one obvious risk and one non-issue.
:::

## An honest self-assessment method

The method itself is simple to state and easy to do badly if you are not strict with yourself. For a specific target posting, build a matrix: one row per basic-qualification clause, and for each row, record whether it is met, partially met, or not met, together with the specific evidence that supports that rating. Be strict about "partially met" in particular — it requires an actual artifact behind it: a specific project, a specific job responsibility you actually held, a course with a grade attached, a publication, something a stranger could ask you to describe in detail. An intention — "I plan to learn C++ properly this year" — is not evidence, however sincere it is, and letting it count as "partially met" is the most common way this exercise quietly lies to the person doing it.

Once the basic-qualification matrix is built, do the same for the preferred list, using the technique lesson four and lesson seven both introduced: for each item, the one question an interviewer could plausibly ask, and an honest self-rating against it. Then do the single most useful thing this whole exercise produces: name the one basic-qualification line, not a vague feeling of underpreparedness, that is most likely to end this specific application today. A gap you can name precisely is a gap you can plan around. A general sense of not being ready yet is not.

::: key
An honest self-assessment matrix rates each basic-qualification clause as met, partially met, or not met, with a specific artifact as evidence for anything short of fully met — and ends by naming the single line most likely to disqualify the application today, rather than a general impression of readiness.
:::

## What actually substitutes for stated experience, and what does not

Across the six postings this module has covered, three real substitutes for stated professional experience showed up in the actual text, and it is worth gathering them in one place now that you have seen all of them in context.

**Demonstrated project work** substitutes explicitly where a posting says so — the GNC Engineer preferred list's closing clause, from lesson four, named "demonstrated project or professional experience" outright. That substitution is real, but bounded exactly where the text bounds it: it strengthens a preferred-qualification showing, and it does not, anywhere this module found, substitute for a basic qualification's stated "years of professional experience" line.

**Adjacent-domain professional experience** — controls, estimation, or software work earned outside spaceflight specifically, in robotics, automotive systems, defense work, or industrial process control — plausibly earns real credit against the technical-area clusters this module's lesson on Level I to II worked through, since those clusters read naturally as one holistic requirement rather than seven domain-locked ones. This is a plausible and, across the software and engineering industry generally, a common way such clusters get read — not a confirmed fact about how any one specific employer's recruiters actually evaluate it, which this module has no way to state with certainty.

**Internal mobility** was raised honestly in the lesson on Site Reliability Engineer, GNC as a real mechanism many employers support in some form, without any company-specific detail this module can respectably supply about how it works, how often it succeeds, or on what timeline — and explicitly not a way to treat one role's basic qualifications as a substitute for a different role's, from outside.

And then there is where nothing substitutes, which deserves to be stated as plainly as everything above: at the postings this module found no in-lieu clause stated at all — New Graduate Engineer and GNC Engineer, Level I to II — the bachelor's degree requirement is a floor with no written alternative route around it. A strong preferred-qualification showing does not override an unmet basic one; this module's own earlier lessons showed that plainly, and it bears repeating here as the closing point rather than a passing warning. The only two in-lieu-of-degree doors this module found anywhere in this role family are the ones now covered in full: Sr. GNC Engineer's 7+ years, and Site Reliability Engineer, GNC's 4+ years. And beneath all of this, this curriculum's very first module already established the most absolute version of "nothing substitutes" in the entire hiring picture: a legal eligibility requirement, which no hiring manager, however impressed, has the standing to set aside for one candidate they like.

::: warning A strong preferred showing does not rescue a failed basic qualification
This is, in a real sense, the single misreading this entire module has been built to prevent, and it is worth stating one last time, plainly, now that you have seen every posting it applies to: preferred qualifications never override a basic one. If the honest self-assessment above names a basic-qualification line as unmet, no accumulation of preferred-list strength changes that outcome for this specific posting.
:::

::: example A full self-assessment matrix, worked end to end
A candidate holds a physics degree and has spent two years at a defense contractor doing sensor-fusion work for a guided-systems program — professional, full-time, and squarely technical. Their Python is strong from daily use; their C++ is real but newer, mostly written in the last several months under a senior engineer's close review. Checked against GNC Engineer, Level I to II's basic qualifications from lesson three:

| Clause | Rating | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Degree: physics or an engineering discipline | Met | Physics degree |
| 2+ years professional experience | Met | Two years, full-time, at a defense contractor |
| Control systems / sensors and actuators | Met | Core of the sensor-fusion work |
| Orbital mechanics / classical dynamics | Not met | No stated exposure in this role |
| Modeling and simulation | Partially met | Simulation tooling used, not built from scratch |
| Python | Met | Daily professional use |
| C++ | Partially met | Real but recent, closely supervised |

Read as a cluster rather than seven separate boxes, as lesson three taught, this candidate clears the degree and years lines cleanly and shows a plausible, if uneven, match across the technical cluster — orbital mechanics is the one area genuinely absent rather than merely thin. The single line most likely to be probed hardest in an interview, on this honest accounting, is not a missing basic qualification at all; it is the newer C++ experience, which sits inside "partially met" rather than "not met." That is a real, specific, and useful answer — a candidate who did this exercise honestly now knows exactly what to be ready to discuss, rather than carrying a vague worry about the whole application.
:::

::: example Two candidates, two different ways of missing the level
Candidate A has three years of general software engineering experience and no bachelor's degree, and applies directly to Sr. GNC Engineer, reasoning that three years is meaningful experience and the role is worth a try. Checked against all three branches from lesson five: branch one fails outright for lack of a degree; branch two needs 7+ years and three falls far short; branch three needs a postgraduate degree, which does not exist here either. The application ends at the earliest stage — the loud, visible failure mode this lesson described, and one this module's earlier lessons would have flagged immediately had this candidate run the self-assessment matrix first.

Candidate B has a case that would be genuinely competitive for Level I to II — a qualifying degree, two and a half years of directly relevant professional experience, solid coverage across the technical cluster — but applies only to New Graduate postings a year after finishing their degree, reasoning that a new-grad program feels like the safer, lower-pressure target. Nothing about this application fails outright; it proceeds normally. But it is very likely evaluated as a marginal fit for a requisition tuned to a specific graduating cohort with little or no professional experience yet, rather than as the strong Level I to II candidate this person's actual case would have made them. Nothing here trips an alarm the way Candidate A's application does — which is exactly why Candidate B's mistake is easy to make and easy to miss, on both sides, until well after the opportunity to apply at the right level has passed.
:::
