Every estimate in this module so far has been handed to you: average the samples for the mean, divide the squared deviations by $N - 1$ for the variance, weight two sensors by their inverse variances, read the correlation time off the lag where the autocorrelation drops to $1/e$. Each of these is a good estimator, and none of them was derived from a principle. There is a principle, and it generates all of them, along with the weighted least-squares fit, the Kalman filter, and the parameter-fitting step of every sensor calibration: choose the parameter value under which the data you actually observed would have been most probable. That is **maximum likelihood estimation** (MLE), and Fisher's formulation of it is the foundation of statistical estimation.

This lesson defines the likelihood, maximises it for the Gaussian mean and variance to see where the familiar formulas come from and why the variance formula comes out with $N$ rather than $N - 1$, introduces the Fisher information and the Cramér–Rao bound that say how good any estimator can be, and then works through the two maximum-likelihood problems a GNC engineer meets most often: weighted least squares for a linear model, which is one step from the Kalman filter, and fitting the Gauss-Markov parameters $\sigma$ and $T$ to a recorded bias, which turns the previous lesson's read-it-off-the-plot into a number with an error bar.

## The likelihood function

Suppose a model says the data $x_1, \ldots, x_N$ were generated by a density $p(x \mid \theta)$ that depends on an unknown parameter $\theta$, and the samples are independent. The joint density of the observed data is

$$
L(\theta) = p(x_1, \ldots, x_N \mid \theta) = \prod_{i=1}^{N} p(x_i \mid \theta).
$$

Read as a function of the data with $\theta$ fixed, this is a probability density. Read as a function of $\theta$ with the data fixed at what was observed, it is the **likelihood function**. It is not a probability distribution over $\theta$: it does not integrate to one in $\theta$, and it says nothing about how probable any $\theta$ is. It says how probable the *data* would have been under each $\theta$. The Bayes lesson met this quantity as the forward sensor model evaluated at the observation, and Bayes' theorem multiplies it by a prior to get a posterior; maximum likelihood uses it alone.

> **Maximum likelihood estimate.** $\hat{\theta}_{\text{ML}} = \arg\max_\theta L(\theta)$: the parameter value that makes the observed data most probable.

Products are awkward to differentiate, so work with the **log-likelihood** $\ell(\theta) = \ln L(\theta) = \sum_i \ln p(x_i \mid \theta)$, which has the same maximiser because the logarithm is increasing. The maximiser satisfies the **score equation** $\partial\ell/\partial\theta = 0$, provided the maximum is interior and the density is smooth.

## The Gaussian mean

Let $x_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ independently, with $\sigma$ known and $\mu$ to be estimated. Each factor is $p(x_i \mid \mu) = (\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})^{-1}\exp\big(-(x_i - \mu)^2/2\sigma^2\big)$, so

$$
\ell(\mu) = -N\ln\big(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}\big) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_i - \mu)^2.
$$

The first term is constant in $\mu$. The second is a downward parabola, and maximising it means minimising the sum of squared deviations: maximum likelihood for Gaussian data *is* least squares. Differentiating,

$$
\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\mu} = \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_i - \mu) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat{\mu}_{\text{ML}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i = \bar{x}.
$$

The sample mean is the maximum likelihood estimate. Notice what did not matter: $\sigma$ dropped out of the score equation, so the estimate is the same whether the noise is large or small. What $\sigma$ controls is how sharply the likelihood is peaked around $\bar{x}$, and that, as the next section shows, is what determines the estimate's precision.

::: example How peaked is the likelihood of a gyro bias?
The expectation lesson's eight one-second gyro samples, in $^\circ/\mathrm{h}$, had $\bar{x} = 0.574$ and $s = 0.187$. Take $\sigma = 0.187$ as known. The log-likelihood at $\mu = \bar{x}$ is its maximum; at $\mu = \bar{x} + \delta$ it is lower by

$$
\ell(\bar{x}) - \ell(\bar{x} + \delta) = \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\Big[\sum_i(x_i - \bar{x} - \delta)^2 - \sum_i(x_i - \bar{x})^2\Big] = \frac{N\delta^2}{2\sigma^2},
$$

the cross term vanishing because the deviations from $\bar{x}$ sum to zero. For $\delta = 0.1\,^\circ/\mathrm{h}$ this is $8 \times 0.01/(2 \times 0.0350) = 1.14$: the data are $e^{1.14} = 3.1$ times more probable under $\mu = 0.574$ than under $\mu = 0.674$. A drop of $\tfrac{1}{2}$ in log-likelihood corresponds to one standard error, so setting $N\delta^2/2\sigma^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}$ gives $\delta = \sigma/\sqrt{N} = 0.066\,^\circ/\mathrm{h}$, exactly the standard error of the mean from the expectation lesson. The curvature of the log-likelihood and the precision of the estimate are the same thing.
:::

## The Gaussian variance, and why MLE is not always unbiased

Now let both $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ be unknown. With $v = \sigma^2$,

$$
\ell(\mu, v) = -\frac{N}{2}\ln(2\pi) - \frac{N}{2}\ln v - \frac{1}{2v}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_i - \mu)^2.
$$

The $\mu$ equation is unchanged and gives $\hat{\mu} = \bar{x}$. The $v$ equation is

$$
\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial v} = -\frac{N}{2v} + \frac{1}{2v^2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_i - \mu)^2 = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\hat{\sigma}^2_{\text{ML}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_i - \bar{x})^2.
$$

This divides by $N$, not $N - 1$. The expectation lesson showed that $\mathbb{E}[\sum(x_i - \bar{x})^2] = (N - 1)\sigma^2$, so $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\sigma}^2_{\text{ML}}] = \frac{N-1}{N}\sigma^2$: the maximum likelihood estimate of the variance is biased low, by a factor of $7/8$ for the eight gyro samples, giving $\hat{\sigma}_{\text{ML}} = 0.175$ against the unbiased $s = 0.187\,^\circ/\mathrm{h}$. The bias vanishes as $N$ grows, but it is there.

This is a general lesson about the method. Maximum likelihood produces the parameter under which the data are most probable, and that is not the same as producing an estimator whose average over many data sets equals the truth. The two criteria often agree, as they do for the mean; when they disagree, the ML estimate can be corrected if the bias is known, as Bessel's $N/(N-1)$ does here. What maximum likelihood *does* guarantee, under mild conditions, is stated in the next section.

## Fisher information and the Cramér–Rao bound

The gyro example suggested that the curvature of the log-likelihood measures how much the data pin down the parameter. The **Fisher information** makes this a definition:

$$
I(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\Big(\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\theta}\Big)^2\right] = -\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{\partial^2\ell}{\partial\theta^2}\right],
$$

the expected squared slope of the log-likelihood, or equivalently its expected negative curvature at the true parameter; the two forms agree by a short calculation that uses $\int p\,dx = 1$ differentiated twice. For $N$ independent samples, $\ell$ is a sum of $N$ terms, so $I$ is $N$ times the information in one sample: information adds.

For the Gaussian mean, $\partial^2\ell/\partial\mu^2 = -N/\sigma^2$, a constant, so $I(\mu) = N/\sigma^2$. Its reciprocal is $\sigma^2/N$, the variance of the sample mean. That is not a coincidence but an instance of the central result of estimation theory.

> **Cramér–Rao bound.** For any unbiased estimator $\hat{\theta}$ of $\theta$,
> $$
> \operatorname{Var}(\hat{\theta}) \geq \frac{1}{I(\theta)}.
> $$

No unbiased estimator, however clever, can have a variance smaller than the inverse Fisher information. An estimator that attains the bound is called **efficient**, and the sample mean is efficient for the Gaussian mean. When a colleague proposes a new way of estimating a bias from the same data, the Cramér–Rao bound is the first question to ask: how far is it from $1/I$, and how far is the current method?

Three further properties make maximum likelihood the default choice. Under regularity conditions and as $N \to \infty$, the ML estimate is **consistent** (converges to the true $\theta$), **asymptotically normal** with $\hat{\theta}_{\text{ML}} \sim \mathcal{N}\big(\theta, 1/I(\theta)\big)$, so that it attains the Cramér–Rao bound in the limit, and **invariant** under reparameterisation: if $\hat{\theta}$ is the MLE of $\theta$ then $g(\hat{\theta})$ is the MLE of $g(\theta)$. The last property is used constantly without comment. Estimate $\phi = e^{-\Delta t/T}$ by maximum likelihood and $\hat{T} = -\Delta t/\ln\hat{\phi}$ is automatically the MLE of $T$; estimate a variance and its square root is the MLE of the standard deviation.

::: key
The maximum likelihood estimate maximises $\ell(\theta) = \sum_i \ln p(x_i \mid \theta)$, the log-probability of the observed data. For Gaussian data it is least squares: $\hat{\mu} = \bar{x}$ and $\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{1}{N}\sum(x_i - \bar{x})^2$ (biased by $(N-1)/N$). The Fisher information $I(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}[\partial^2\ell/\partial\theta^2]$ bounds every unbiased estimator by $\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\theta}) \geq 1/I(\theta)$ (Cramér–Rao), and the MLE attains this bound asymptotically.
:::

## Weighted least squares: maximum likelihood for a linear model

The problem that leads to the Kalman filter is this. An unknown vector $\mathbf{x}$ of $n$ parameters is observed through $m \geq n$ linear measurements corrupted by Gaussian noise:

$$
\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{H}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{v}, \qquad \mathbf{v} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{R}),
$$

with $\mathbf{H}$ a known $m \times n$ matrix and $\mathbf{R}$ a known measurement covariance, not necessarily diagonal. The likelihood is the multivariate Gaussian density of $\mathbf{z}$ with mean $\mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}$, so

$$
\ell(\mathbf{x}) = -\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{z} - \mathbf{H}\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}^{-1}(\mathbf{z} - \mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}) + \text{const}.
$$

Maximising $\ell$ minimises the quadratic form $J(\mathbf{x}) = (\mathbf{z} - \mathbf{H}\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}^{-1}(\mathbf{z} - \mathbf{H}\mathbf{x})$, the squared Mahalanobis distance between the data and the model's prediction. Each residual is weighted by the inverse of its noise covariance: a precise measurement pulls hard, a noisy one pulls weakly, and correlated measurements are handled correctly through the off-diagonal terms of $\mathbf{R}^{-1}$. Setting the gradient $\partial J/\partial\mathbf{x} = -2\mathbf{H}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}^{-1}(\mathbf{z} - \mathbf{H}\mathbf{x})$ to zero gives the **normal equations** and their solution,

$$
\mathbf{H}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\,\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{H}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{z}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \big(\mathbf{H}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{H}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{z},
$$

the **weighted least squares** estimate. It is a linear map of $\mathbf{z}$, so its covariance follows from the linear-transformation lesson: with $\mathbf{A} = (\mathbf{H}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{H})^{-1}\mathbf{H}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}^{-1}$, the product $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf{T}}$ simplifies to

$$
\mathbf{P}_{\hat{x}} = \big(\mathbf{H}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\big)^{-1}.
$$

The matrix $\mathbf{H}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{H}$ is the Fisher information matrix for this problem, the multi-parameter form of $I(\theta)$: it is the negative Hessian of $\ell$, and the estimate's covariance is its inverse. The weighted least-squares estimate is unbiased and attains the Cramér–Rao bound exactly, not only asymptotically, because the model is linear and the noise Gaussian. Estimation theory calls $\mathbf{H}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{H}$ the **information matrix**, and "information adds" is now the statement that adding a measurement adds its $\mathbf{h}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{h}/r$ to the sum.

Check it against something known. Two sensors measure the same scalar $x$ with independent noise of variances $\sigma_1^2$ and $\sigma_2^2$: $\mathbf{H} = (1, 1)^{\mathsf{T}}$, $\mathbf{R} = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1^2, \sigma_2^2)$. Then $\mathbf{H}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{H} = 1/\sigma_1^2 + 1/\sigma_2^2$ and $\hat{x} = (z_1/\sigma_1^2 + z_2/\sigma_2^2)/(1/\sigma_1^2 + 1/\sigma_2^2)$, with variance $(1/\sigma_1^2 + 1/\sigma_2^2)^{-1}$. This is the inverse-variance combination of the linear-transformation lesson, derived there by minimising the variance over the weight and here by maximising the likelihood; the two principles agree, and for $\sigma_1 = 3\,\mathrm{m}$, $\sigma_2 = 4\,\mathrm{m}$, $z_1 = 10.2\,\mathrm{m}$, $z_2 = 9.5\,\mathrm{m}$ both give $\hat{x} = 9.95\,\mathrm{m}$ with $\sigma = 2.4\,\mathrm{m}$.

::: example Gyro scale factor and bias from a rate table
A gyro is mounted on a rate table and driven at five known rates, $-100$, $-50$, $0$, $50$ and $100\,^\circ/\mathrm{s}$. At each rate the output is averaged long enough that its noise has $\sigma = 0.05\,^\circ/\mathrm{s}$, and the averaged outputs are $-99.895$, $-49.737$, $0.253$, $50.450$ and $100.487\,^\circ/\mathrm{s}$. The model is $z_i = k\,\omega_i + b + v_i$, with scale factor $k$ and bias $b$ the unknowns, so $\mathbf{x} = (k, b)^{\mathsf{T}}$ and the rows of $\mathbf{H}$ are $(\omega_i, 1)$. With $\mathbf{R} = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}$ the weights cancel and the normal equations are the ordinary least-squares line fit:

$$
\mathbf{H}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{H} = \begin{bmatrix} \sum\omega_i^2 & \sum\omega_i \\ \sum\omega_i & 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 25\,000 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf{H}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{z} = \begin{bmatrix} \sum\omega_i z_i \\ \sum z_i \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 25\,047.6 \\ 1.558 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The symmetric choice of rates has made the information matrix diagonal, so the two parameters decouple: $\hat{k} = 25\,047.6/25\,000 = 1.00190$ and $\hat{b} = 1.558/5 = 0.312\,^\circ/\mathrm{s}$. The covariance is $\sigma^2(\mathbf{H}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{H})^{-1} = \operatorname{diag}(0.0025/25\,000,\ 0.0025/5)$, giving $\sigma_k = 3.16 \times 10^{-4}$ (that is, $316$ parts per million) and $\sigma_b = 0.0224\,^\circ/\mathrm{s}$. The scale-factor error of $1900\,\mathrm{ppm}$ is therefore detected at six standard errors and is real; the bias of $0.312\,^\circ/\mathrm{s}$ is detected at fourteen. The residuals $\mathbf{z} - \mathbf{H}\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ are $-0.016$, $0.047$, $-0.059$, $0.043$ and $-0.015\,^\circ/\mathrm{s}$, and their sum of squares divided by $\sigma^2$ is $3.18$; the chi-square lesson explains why that number should be close to $m - n = 3$, and what to conclude when it is not.
:::

::: warning
The quantity being minimised is $(\mathbf{z} - \mathbf{H}\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}^{-1}(\mathbf{z} - \mathbf{H}\mathbf{x})$, with $\mathbf{R}^{-1}$ in the middle. Fitting with unweighted least squares when the measurements have different variances is not maximum likelihood, is not efficient, and reports a covariance that is wrong. And the covariance $(\mathbf{H}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{H})^{-1}$ is only as honest as $\mathbf{R}$: a fit that assumes $\sigma = 0.05$ when the true noise is $0.15$ will report parameter uncertainties three times too small, and the residuals will be the only witness.
:::

## Other distributions, same method

The method does not care that the data are Gaussian. For any density with a parameter, write the log-likelihood and differentiate.

**Exponential.** Ten star-tracker outages, in minutes, last $0.4$, $1.7$, $0.9$, $2.6$, $0.3$, $1.1$, $0.8$, $3.2$, $0.6$ and $1.4$, modelled as exponential with rate $\lambda$, density $\lambda e^{-\lambda t}$. Then $\ell(\lambda) = N\ln\lambda - \lambda\sum_i t_i$, whose derivative $N/\lambda - \sum t_i$ vanishes at $\hat{\lambda} = N/\sum t_i = 1/\bar{t}$. The total is $13.0\,\mathrm{min}$, so $\hat{\lambda} = 10/13.0 = 0.769\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}$, a mean outage of $1.3\,\mathrm{min}$. The Fisher information is $-\mathbb{E}[\partial^2\ell/\partial\lambda^2] = N/\lambda^2$, so the standard error is about $\hat{\lambda}/\sqrt{N} = 0.24\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}$: ten outages fix the rate to roughly $30\%$.

**Bernoulli.** A Monte Carlo campaign of $N$ runs produces $k$ failures, each run failing independently with probability $p$. The likelihood is $p^k(1 - p)^{N-k}$, so $\ell = k\ln p + (N - k)\ln(1 - p)$ and $\partial\ell/\partial p = k/p - (N - k)/(1 - p) = 0$ gives $\hat{p} = k/N$, the failure fraction, with Fisher information $N/(p(1 - p))$ and variance $p(1 - p)/N$. The Monte Carlo lesson builds its sizing rules on this: $23$ failures in $2000$ runs gives $\hat{p} = 0.0115$ with standard error $\sqrt{0.0115 \times 0.9885/2000} = 0.0024$.

## Fitting a Gauss-Markov process to a bias record

The previous lesson estimated $T$ by eye from the autocorrelation plot. Maximum likelihood does it properly. The exact discrete Gauss-Markov model is $b_{k+1} = \phi\,b_k + n_k$ with $n_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_n^2)$ independent, a first-order autoregression, AR(1). Given $b_k$, the next value is Gaussian with mean $\phi b_k$ and variance $\sigma_n^2$, so conditioning each sample on its predecessor gives the log-likelihood of the record $b_0, \ldots, b_{N-1}$ as

$$
\ell(\phi, \sigma_n^2) = -\frac{N - 1}{2}\ln(2\pi\sigma_n^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma_n^2}\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}\big(b_{k+1} - \phi\,b_k\big)^2
$$

plus the log-density of $b_0$, which is negligible for a long record. This is a Gaussian least-squares problem in $\phi$: the score equation $\sum_k b_k(b_{k+1} - \phi b_k) = 0$ gives

$$
\hat{\phi} = \frac{\sum_{k} b_k\,b_{k+1}}{\sum_{k} b_k^2}, \qquad
\hat{\sigma}_n^2 = \frac{1}{N - 1}\sum_{k}\big(b_{k+1} - \hat{\phi}\,b_k\big)^2,
$$

and by the invariance property $\hat{T} = -\Delta t/\ln\hat{\phi}$ and $\hat{\sigma}^2 = \hat{\sigma}_n^2/(1 - \hat{\phi}^2)$. The estimate of $\phi$ is the lag-one sample autocorrelation, normalised by the lag-zero one, so the previous lesson's plot and this lesson's estimator agree at lag one; the difference is that this one comes with a variance. The Fisher information for $\phi$ is $\sum b_k^2/\sigma_n^2 \approx N\sigma^2/\sigma_n^2 = N/(1 - \phi^2)$, so $\operatorname{sd}(\hat{\phi}) \approx \sqrt{(1 - \phi^2)/N}$, and propagating through $T = -\Delta t/\ln\phi$ with $\Delta t \ll T$ gives

$$
\frac{\operatorname{sd}(\hat{T})}{T} \approx \sqrt{\frac{2T}{N\,\Delta t}} = \sqrt{\frac{2T}{t_{\text{record}}}}.
$$

The relative precision of the correlation time depends only on how many correlation times the record contains. To know $T$ to $10\%$ you need a record about $200$ correlation times long; to know it to $1\%$, $20\,000$. This is why bench tests for bias characterisation run overnight.

::: example Fitting σ and T to a ten-hour bias record
A simulated ten-hour record of the $\sigma = 3\,^\circ/\mathrm{h}$, $T = 100\,\mathrm{s}$ bias process at $\Delta t = 1\,\mathrm{s}$, that is $N = 36\,000$ samples, gives $\sum b_k b_{k+1}/\sum b_k^2 = 0.99041$, so $\hat{\phi} = 0.99041$ and $\hat{T} = -1/\ln(0.99041) = 103.7\,\mathrm{s}$. The residual variance is $\hat{\sigma}_n^2 = 0.1790\,(^\circ/\mathrm{h})^2$, so $\hat{\sigma}_n = 0.423\,^\circ/\mathrm{h}$ and $\hat{\sigma} = 0.423/\sqrt{1 - 0.99041^2} = 3.06\,^\circ/\mathrm{h}$. Against the truth of $100\,\mathrm{s}$ and $3\,^\circ/\mathrm{h}$, both are within $4\%$. The predicted relative standard error of $\hat{T}$ is $\sqrt{2 \times 100/36\,000} = 0.075$, or $7.5\,\mathrm{s}$, so the $3.7\,\mathrm{s}$ miss is half a standard error, unremarkable. A one-hour record would have given $\pm 24\%$, which is why a correlation time quoted from a short test deserves suspicion. Note that the estimate of $\sigma$ is much better determined than that of $T$: the variance is estimated from every sample, the correlation time only from the roughly $360$ independent stretches the record contains.
:::

```python
import numpy as np

def fit_gauss_markov(b, dt):
    """Maximum likelihood (conditional) fit of an AR(1) / Gauss-Markov model."""
    phi = np.dot(b[:-1], b[1:]) / np.dot(b[:-1], b[:-1])
    resid = b[1:] - phi * b[:-1]
    sigma_n2 = np.mean(resid**2)
    T = -dt / np.log(phi)
    sigma = np.sqrt(sigma_n2 / (1 - phi**2))
    return T, sigma

# with b from the previous lesson's gauss_markov(3.0, 100.0, 1.0, 36_000):
# print(fit_gauss_markov(b, 1.0))   # ~(104, 3.06): truth is (100, 3.0)
```

::: note
Maximum likelihood is what you get from Bayes' theorem with a flat prior. With an informative prior $p(\theta)$, maximising the posterior $p(\theta \mid \text{data}) \propto L(\theta)\,p(\theta)$ gives the **maximum a posteriori** (MAP) estimate. For a Gaussian prior $\mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2)$ on a Gaussian mean, the log-posterior is the log-likelihood plus $-(\mu - \mu_0)^2/2\sigma_0^2$, and maximising it yields the inverse-variance combination of the prior and the data: the prior acts as one more measurement with variance $\sigma_0^2$. The Kalman filter is exactly this, applied recursively: yesterday's posterior becomes today's prior, today's measurement enters through its likelihood, and the update is the MAP estimate. Every gain, covariance and residual in the estimation track descends from the likelihood function defined at the start of this lesson.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
Five independent samples from $\mathcal{N}(\mu, 4)$ are $3.1$, $2.4$, $4.0$, $3.3$ and $2.7$. Find the maximum likelihood estimate of $\mu$, its Fisher information and the Cramér–Rao standard deviation.
:::

::: answer
$\hat{\mu} = (3.1 + 2.4 + 4.0 + 3.3 + 2.7)/5 = 15.5/5 = 3.10$. With $\sigma^2 = 4$ and $N = 5$, $I(\mu) = N/\sigma^2 = 1.25$, so the bound on any unbiased estimator's variance is $1/1.25 = 0.8$ and the standard deviation is $\sqrt{0.8} = 0.894$. The sample mean attains it exactly.
:::

::: check
Event counts in five equal intervals are $3$, $5$, $2$, $4$, $6$, modelled as Poisson with mean $\lambda$ per interval, $p(k \mid \lambda) = \lambda^k e^{-\lambda}/k!$. Derive the MLE of $\lambda$ and its approximate standard error.
:::

::: answer
$\ell(\lambda) = \sum_i (k_i\ln\lambda - \lambda - \ln k_i!) = (\sum k_i)\ln\lambda - N\lambda + \text{const}$, so $\partial\ell/\partial\lambda = \sum k_i/\lambda - N = 0$ gives $\hat{\lambda} = \bar{k} = 20/5 = 4.0$. The second derivative is $-\sum k_i/\lambda^2$, with expectation $-N\lambda/\lambda^2 = -N/\lambda$, so $I(\lambda) = N/\lambda$ and the standard error is $\sqrt{\lambda/N} \approx \sqrt{4/5} = 0.894$. The Poisson mean equals its variance, so this is also $\sigma/\sqrt{N}$.
:::

::: check
By what percentage does the maximum likelihood variance estimate underestimate $\sigma^2$ on average for $N = 8$ samples, and what does this say about the claim "the MLE is the best estimator"?
:::

::: answer
$\mathbb{E}[\hat{\sigma}^2_{\text{ML}}] = \frac{N-1}{N}\sigma^2 = \frac{7}{8}\sigma^2$, an underestimate of $12.5\%$ in variance (about $6\%$ in standard deviation). The MLE is best in the specific sense of making the data most probable and of being asymptotically efficient; it is not guaranteed unbiased at finite $N$. "Best" always needs a criterion, and for small samples the bias-corrected $s^2$ is preferred for the variance.
:::

::: check
Two altimeters read $x$ with independent Gaussian errors of $\sigma_1 = 2\,\mathrm{m}$ and $\sigma_2 = 6\,\mathrm{m}$. Write the weighted least-squares weights and the standard deviation of the combined estimate. What happens to the weights if you mistakenly use unweighted least squares?
:::

::: answer
The information is $1/4 + 1/36 = 10/36$, so the weights are $(1/4)/(10/36) = 0.9$ on the first altimeter and $0.1$ on the second, and the combined standard deviation is $\sqrt{36/10} = 1.90\,\mathrm{m}$, slightly better than the $2\,\mathrm{m}$ of the good sensor alone. Unweighted least squares uses $0.5$ and $0.5$, giving $\sqrt{0.25 \times 4 + 0.25 \times 36} = 3.16\,\mathrm{m}$: worse than ignoring the poor sensor entirely, because the noisy measurement was allowed to pull as hard as the precise one.
:::

::: check
A bias record sampled at $\Delta t = 0.5\,\mathrm{s}$ gives $\hat{\phi} = 0.980$. What is the maximum likelihood estimate of the correlation time, and which property of the MLE lets you say so without redoing the maximisation?
:::

::: answer
$\hat{T} = -\Delta t/\ln\hat{\phi} = -0.5/\ln(0.980) = 24.7\,\mathrm{s}$. This is the MLE of $T$ by the invariance property: $T$ is a one-to-one function of $\phi$, and the MLE of a function of a parameter is that function of the MLE. The likelihood surface has the same maximum whichever parameterisation you plot it in; only the coordinate along the axis has changed.
:::

## Summary

| Symbol or formula | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| $L(\theta) = \prod_i p(x_i \mid \theta)$, $\ell = \ln L$ | Likelihood and log-likelihood of the observed data as a function of $\theta$ |
| $\hat{\theta}_{\text{ML}} = \arg\max_\theta \ell(\theta)$, $\partial\ell/\partial\theta = 0$ | Maximum likelihood estimate; score equation |
| $\hat{\mu} = \bar{x}$, $\hat{\sigma}^2_{\text{ML}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum(x_i - \bar{x})^2$ | Gaussian MLEs; variance biased by $(N-1)/N$ |
| $I(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}[\partial^2\ell/\partial\theta^2]$ | Fisher information; additive over independent samples; $N/\sigma^2$ for a Gaussian mean |
| $\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\theta}) \geq 1/I(\theta)$ | Cramér–Rao bound; efficient estimators attain it |
| Consistent, $\hat{\theta} \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, 1/I)$ asymptotically, $g(\hat{\theta})$ is the MLE of $g(\theta)$ | Properties of the MLE |
| $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = (\mathbf{H}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{H})^{-1}\mathbf{H}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{z}$, $\mathbf{P} = (\mathbf{H}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{H})^{-1}$ | Weighted least squares: the MLE for $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{H}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{v}$, $\mathbf{v} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{R})$ |
| $\hat{\lambda} = 1/\bar{t}$; $\hat{p} = k/N$ | Exponential rate; Bernoulli probability |
| $\hat{\phi} = \sum b_k b_{k+1}/\sum b_k^2$, $\hat{T} = -\Delta t/\ln\hat{\phi}$ | AR(1) fit of a Gauss-Markov process; $\operatorname{sd}(\hat{T})/T \approx \sqrt{2T/t_{\text{record}}}$ |
| MAP: $\arg\max L(\theta)p(\theta)$ | Maximum likelihood with a prior; the Kalman update |

Maximum likelihood produces a point estimate and, through the Fisher information, a variance for it. The next lesson turns that variance into a statement with a probability attached, the confidence interval, and into a decision procedure, the hypothesis test, and shows what both do and do not mean.
