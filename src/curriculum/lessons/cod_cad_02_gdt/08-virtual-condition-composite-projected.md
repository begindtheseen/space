Drive into a parking garage and you pass under a bar hanging on chains. It says "Clearance 2.1 m". Nobody at the entrance measures your van. The bar does the job: if the van passes under it, the van fits in the garage. If it hits the bar, it does not. One fixed boundary answers the whole question, with no numbers and no arithmetic.

Last lesson you learned bonus tolerance: a hole made bigger than its smallest size may sit farther from its true position. That sounds like it would make inspection harder — every hole has its own allowed zone. The surprise of this lesson is the opposite. With the Ⓜ modifier, the space a bolt can count on inside the hole never changes size. It is a fixed boundary, like the garage bar, and a simple steel gauge can check it.

That boundary is called the **virtual condition**. After it, this lesson adds two more tools every spacecraft drawing uses. **Composite position** lets a bolt pattern be loose on the part but tight within itself. The **projected tolerance zone** handles threaded holes, where a tiny tilt in the hole becomes a big miss at the far end of the bolt.

## The boundary that never moves

Take the avionics bracket hole from last lesson: $\varnothing\,6.6$ to $6.8\,\mathrm{mm}$, position $\varnothing\,0.25$ at MMC. Now ask a new question. Whatever size this hole is made, and wherever it sits inside its allowed zone, what round space is *always* clear, straight through, at true position?

**At MMC, $6.6$.** The zone is $0.25$ across, so the hole's axis can drift up to $0.125$ from true position, in any direction. Picture the hole sliding around by that much. The space that stays clear in *every* one of those positions is a cylinder at true position whose diameter is the hole minus the full zone: $6.6 - 0.25 = 6.35\,\mathrm{mm}$.

**At LMC, $6.8$.** Now the bonus is $0.2$, so the zone is $0.45$. Clear space: $6.8 - 0.45 = 6.35\,\mathrm{mm}$.

The same number. The bigger hole wanders farther, and the extra wandering eats exactly the extra size. That fixed cylinder is the **virtual condition**, often shortened to **VC** — the worst-case boundary made by the feature's MMC size and its geometric tolerance acting together. You can [[see it as the space all the hole positions share|vc-picture]].

For a hole, the boundary is inside the hole, so the tolerance is subtracted. For a shaft or pin, the boundary is a sleeve around it, so the tolerance is added:

$$
\mathrm{VC}_{\text{hole}} = \text{MMC} - \text{geometric tolerance}
$$

$$
\mathrm{VC}_{\text{shaft}} = \text{MMC} + \text{geometric tolerance}
$$

Read "VC sub hole" as "the virtual condition of the hole". The geometric tolerance here is the number printed in the frame, the one that applies at MMC.

::: key Virtual condition
The worst-case boundary generated by the combination of the feature size at MMC and its geometric tolerance. It is the number a mating part or a functional gauge must clear, which is what makes MMC controls checkable with a hard gauge.
:::

::: note Why the boundary does not move
Let $M$ be the hole's MMC size and $t$ the tolerance in the frame. Make the hole at some size $d$, bigger than $M$. With Ⓜ its zone is $t + (d - M)$. The axis may drift half of that, and the hole's radius is $\frac{d}{2}$. The clear space at true position has radius equal to the hole's radius minus the drift:

$$
\frac{d}{2} - \frac{t + d - M}{2} = \frac{M - t}{2}
$$

The $d$ cancels. So the clear diameter is $M - t$ for every size of hole. For a shaft, the same steps give a sleeve of diameter $M + t$. This cancellation is exactly why Ⓜ and a fixed gauge belong together.
:::

::: example Will the pins go into the bracket?
A tracker mounting plate has two locating pins that must enter two holes in the avionics bracket. The bracket holes are $\varnothing\,6.6$ to $6.8$ with position $\varnothing\,0.25$ at MMC. The pins are $\varnothing\,5.9$ to $6.0$ with position $\varnothing\,0.1$ at MMC. Both use the same basic spacing. Will every good pair of parts assemble?

**Hole VC.** MMC is the smallest hole, $6.6$. $\mathrm{VC} = 6.6 - 0.25 = 6.35\,\mathrm{mm}$. Every good hole leaves a clear cylinder at least this big at true position.

**Pin VC.** MMC is the largest pin, $6.0$. $\mathrm{VC} = 6.0 + 0.1 = 6.10\,\mathrm{mm}$. Every good pin stays inside a sleeve this big at true position.

**Compare.** A sleeve of $6.10$ always fits inside a clear space of $6.35$. So yes: every pin that passes inspection goes into every hole that passes inspection.

**How much margin?** $6.35 - 6.10 = 0.25\,\mathrm{mm}$ of diameter to spare. The designer could loosen either tolerance by up to that amount and still guarantee assembly.

**Sanity check.** The worst case is a fat pin ($6.0$) at the edge of its zone meeting a small hole ($6.6$) at the edge of its zone. Their centers can be at most $0.05 + 0.125 = 0.175$ apart. The pin's surface then reaches $0.175 + 3.0 = 3.175$ from the hole's center, which is less than the hole's radius, $3.3$. It fits, with $0.125$ of radius — $0.25$ of diameter — left over. The same answer by a second road.
:::

::: warning Subtract for holes, add for shafts
It is easy to add the tolerance to a hole ("the hole gets $0.25$ more room"). Think about which side the boundary is on. A hole's worst case closes *in* on the bolt, so its boundary is smaller than its MMC. A pin's worst case swells *out*, so its boundary is bigger than its MMC. If your hole VC came out larger than the hole, you added when you should have subtracted.
:::

## Functional gauges

Now back to the garage bar. A **functional gauge**, or **hard gauge**, is a steel fixture that checks a feature's virtual condition by fit alone. For the bracket, it is a plate with:

- a flat face that plays datum A (the bracket's mounting face sits on it),
- a rail and a stop that play datums B and C, seated in order,
- one fixed pin at each hole's true position, at the basic spacing, each pin exactly $\varnothing\,6.35$ — the hole's virtual condition.

The inspector seats the bracket on A, slides it against B, then against C, and lowers it over the pins. If it drops on, every hole's position is good, bonus and all, whatever size each hole happens to be. No numbers, no calculator. The bonus is checked automatically because a bigger hole has more room around the same $6.35$ pin.

The gauge checks only position. Size is checked separately, with a **[[go and no-go gauge|gauge-plate]]** or a bore gauge, to confirm each hole is between $6.6$ and $6.8$.

This is why Ⓜ matters so much on production hardware. A gauge like this can check a bracket in seconds on the shop floor, while a coordinate measuring machine takes minutes and a trained operator. And it tests the real function: the gauge pins behave like the worst-case mating part. A frame at RFS has no fixed boundary like this, so it cannot be checked with a fixed-pin gauge; it needs measurement.

::: warning A gauge that does not go on is not always a reject
A hole that is undersize — below $6.6$ — can also stop the gauge from going on, even if it is perfectly placed. So when a gauge fails, check size before calling it a position failure. And the reverse: the gauge going on does not prove size is good. A hole drilled at $7.2$ goes over the pins easily and is still scrap.
:::

The other boundary, the worst case on the *outside* of the hole, has its own name, **[[resultant condition|resultant-condition]]**, and matters when you care about wall thickness rather than fit.

## Composite position: loose pattern, tight holes

Picture a tablecloth with a pattern of four buttons sewn on. You can slide the whole cloth around the table a little, and nobody minds. But if one button moves relative to the others, the cloth no longer lines up with the buttonholes on its cover. Where the *pattern* sits is loose. Where the buttons sit *relative to each other* is tight.

Bolt patterns are often like that. A star tracker has four mounting feet at a fixed spacing. The four holes in its bracket must match that spacing closely, or the bolts will not all go in. But where the pattern sits on the bracket, relative to the bracket's edges, matters much less; a few tenths of a millimeter of shift barely changes anything, and the tracker's final alignment is measured after installation anyway.

A single position frame cannot say both things. **Composite position** can. It is one position symbol that spans **two rows**, with a different tolerance and different datums in each row:

`| ⌖ | ⌀0.8 Ⓜ | A | B | C |`
`|   | ⌀0.15 Ⓜ | A |`

(On a drawing the symbol box is one tall box shared by both rows.)

- **The upper row** is the **pattern-locating** tier — its zones, one per hole, sit at the true positions measured from datums A, B and C. It says where the *pattern* is on the part. Its tolerance is the big one: $\varnothing\,0.8$.
- **The lower row** is the **feature-relating** tier — its zones are held at the basic spacing between the holes and kept perpendicular to A, but the whole group of zones may shift and turn together on the part. It says where the holes are *relative to each other*. Its tolerance is the small one: $\varnothing\,0.15$.

Each hole's axis must lie inside **both** of its zones at once: its big zone, fixed to the part, and its small zone, which floats as a group. In the lower row, datums control only orientation — here A keeps the small zones square to the mounting face. Even a datum repeated there from the upper row would only stop the group from turning; it never pins down where the group sits. [[The picture shows both sets of zones|composite-picture]].

::: example Checking a four-hole tracker pattern
A tracker bracket's four holes carry the composite frame above. Take every hole at MMC, so there is no bonus. A CMM reports each hole's axis miss from its true position (in millimeters, $x$ then $y$):

hole 1: $(0.32,\ 0.12)$, hole 2: $(0.27,\ 0.05)$, hole 3: $(0.36,\ 0.14)$, hole 4: $(0.29,\ 0.09)$.

**Upper tier, $\varnothing\,0.8$ to A, B, C.** Turn each miss into a zone diameter, $2\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$:

hole 1: $2\sqrt{0.32^2 + 0.12^2} = 0.684$. Hole 2: $0.549$. Hole 3: $0.773$. Hole 4: $0.607$.

All are at most $0.8$. The pattern is placed well enough on the part.

**Lower tier, $\varnothing\,0.15$, floating.** The small zones may move together. Every hole is off by about the same amount in the same direction, so slide the group by the average miss. Average $x$: $\frac{0.32 + 0.27 + 0.36 + 0.29}{4} = 0.31$. Average $y$: $\frac{0.12 + 0.05 + 0.14 + 0.09}{4} = 0.10$.

What is left over for each hole, after that slide:

hole 1: $(0.01,\ 0.02)$, so $2\sqrt{0.01^2 + 0.02^2} = 0.045$. Hole 2: $(-0.04,\ -0.05)$, giving $0.128$. Hole 3: $(0.05,\ 0.04)$, giving $0.128$. Hole 4: $(-0.02,\ -0.01)$, giving $0.045$.

All are at most $0.15$. One placement of the floating group that works is enough, so the lower tier passes too.

**Verdict.** The part passes. The pattern sits about $0.3\,\mathrm{mm}$ off on the bracket, which the design can live with, while the holes agree with each other to about a tenth of a millimeter, which the tracker's feet need.

**Sanity check.** Try a single frame of $\varnothing\,0.15$ to A, B, C instead. Hole 2, the best one, needs $0.549$. Every hole fails, and a bracket that bolts up perfectly would be scrapped. Composite position exists to prevent exactly that.
:::

::: warning The lower tier does not locate the pattern
The lower row is not a second, tighter location requirement. Its zones float. If you check the lower tier against the true positions from B and C, you will reject good parts. And if the lower row *does* repeat B, the meaning changes, because B would then stop the small zones from turning. Designers choose that on purpose, sometimes with [[two separate frames|two-single-segment]] instead of one composite frame.
:::

## Projected tolerance zones

Stick a pencil into a lump of clay, and tilt it by a hair. At the clay's surface you can barely see the tilt. At the pencil's tip, fifteen centimeters up, the tip has swung far off. A small angle, times a long lever, makes a big miss.

Threaded holes have exactly this problem. A position frame on a hole normally controls the hole's axis *inside the part*, through the depth of the hole. But in a threaded hole, the bolt or **[[stud|stud-and-dowel]]** follows the thread's axis and sticks *out* of the part, through the mating part above it. What matters is where the bolt is at the mating part, not where the thread is at its bottom. The same is true of a press-fit dowel pin: it is fixed in its hole and sticks out into the next part.

::: example The lever arm of a tilted thread
A threaded hole is $15\,\mathrm{mm}$ deep with position $\varnothing\,0.2$ in the usual way, inside the part. The bolt passes up through a mating plate $12\,\mathrm{mm}$ thick. How far off can the bolt be at the top of the mating plate?

**Worst tilt inside the zone.** The axis may start $0.1$ to one side at the bottom of the thread and cross to $0.1$ on the other side at the surface. It moves $0.2\,\mathrm{mm}$ sideways over $15\,\mathrm{mm}$ of depth.

**Slope.** $\frac{0.2}{15} = 0.0133$, about $0.76^\circ$ of tilt.

**Continue the line.** Going another $12\,\mathrm{mm}$ up, the axis moves a further $12 \times 0.0133 = 0.16\,\mathrm{mm}$ in the same direction.

**Total at the top.** It was already $0.1$ off at the surface, so now it is $0.1 + 0.16 = 0.26\,\mathrm{mm}$ from true position. As a zone diameter that is $2 \times 0.26 = 0.52\,\mathrm{mm}$.

**Amplification.** $\frac{0.52}{0.2} = 2.6$. The mating plate sees a bolt more than two and a half times as far off as the frame seemed to promise. A mating hole sized for a $\varnothing\,0.2$ miss can jam.

**Sanity check.** The lever outside the part, $12\,\mathrm{mm}$, is almost as long as the thread, $15\,\mathrm{mm}$. So the extra miss, $0.16$, should be almost as big as the full zone width, $0.2$. It is.
:::

In general, with zone $t$, thread depth $L$ and height $h$ above the surface, the miss grows to

$$
t_{\text{at } h} = t\left(1 + \frac{2h}{L}\right)
$$

Check it: $0.2 \times (1 + \frac{24}{15}) = 0.2 \times 2.6 = 0.52$. Short, shallow threads under thick mating parts are the worst.

The fix is to move the zone to where the interference really happens. A **projected tolerance zone** — a position zone moved out of the hole, above the part's surface — is called out with a circled P, Ⓟ, followed by a **projected height**:

`| ⌖ | ⌀0.2 Ⓜ Ⓟ 12 | A | B | C |`

Read it as "position, diameter zero point two at MMC, projected twelve, to A, B, C". The $\varnothing\,0.2$ zone now starts at the surface and runs $12\,\mathrm{mm}$ up, and the thread's axis, extended out of the hole, must stay inside it. The [[picture shows the tilted axis leaving the zone|projected-picture]]. The height is normally the thickest the mating part can be, so the zone covers the whole length where the bolt meets it.

::: key Projected tolerance zone
For threaded or press-fit holes, where the misalignment that matters is at the far end of the protruding fastener, not at the part surface. The zone is projected out to the mating part thickness so the assembly actually fits.
:::

::: warning The projected zone is above the part, not in the hole
Ⓟ does not make the hole's zone deeper. It moves the zone *out*, into air, over the length where the bolt passes through the next part. An inspector checks it by threading in a gauge pin or a stud and measuring the pin's axis at the projected height.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
A locating pin is $\varnothing\,11.85$ to $12.00\,\mathrm{mm}$ with position $\varnothing\,0.05$ at MMC. What is its virtual condition, and what does that number mean for the hole it must enter?
:::

::: answer
For a shaft, VC is MMC plus the tolerance. MMC is the largest pin, $12.00$, so $\mathrm{VC} = 12.00 + 0.05 = 12.05\,\mathrm{mm}$. Every good pin stays within a perfect sleeve of $12.05$ at true position. So the mating hole's own virtual condition must be at least $12.05$; then every good pin enters every good hole.
:::

::: check
A hole is $\varnothing\,9.0$ to $9.2$ with position $\varnothing\,0.1$ at MMC. Show that a gauge pin of one fixed size works for a hole made at $9.0$ and one made at $9.2$. What size is the pin?
:::

::: answer
At $9.0$ (MMC) the zone is $0.1$, and the clear space is $9.0 - 0.1 = 8.9$. At $9.2$ the bonus is $0.2$, the zone is $0.3$, and the clear space is $9.2 - 0.3 = 8.9$. The same. The gauge pin is $\varnothing\,8.9\,\mathrm{mm}$ — the hole's virtual condition, $\text{MMC} - \text{tolerance}$ — fixed at true position.
:::

::: check
A four-hole pattern carries composite position: upper row $\varnothing\,0.8$ to A, B, C; lower row $\varnothing\,0.15$ to A. A careless setup shifts every hole by exactly $0.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ in $x$ and nothing else. Which tier fails, and why?
:::

::: answer
The upper tier fails. Each hole is $0.5$ off, so each needs a zone of $2 \times 0.5 = 1.0$, more than $0.8$. The lower tier passes: the floating small zones slide $0.5$ together, and every hole then sits exactly in the middle of its small zone. The holes still agree perfectly with each other; the pattern is just in the wrong place on the part.
:::

::: check
A press-fit dowel is pressed $10\,\mathrm{mm}$ deep into a hole whose position is $\varnothing\,0.1$, controlled only inside the hole. The dowel sticks out $20\,\mathrm{mm}$ into a mating part. How big can the dowel's miss be at its tip, as a zone diameter? What callout fixes it?
:::

::: answer
Use $t\left(1 + \frac{2h}{L}\right)$ with $t = 0.1$, $h = 20$, $L = 10$: $0.1 \times (1 + 4) = 0.5\,\mathrm{mm}$. Step by step: the worst tilt moves $0.1$ over $10\,\mathrm{mm}$, a slope of $0.01$. Over $20\,\mathrm{mm}$ that adds $0.2$ to the $0.05$ already off at the surface, for $0.25$, which is a diameter of $0.5$ — five times the frame's number. The fix is a projected tolerance zone, Ⓟ $20$, so the $\varnothing\,0.1$ zone applies over the $20\,\mathrm{mm}$ where the dowel meets the mating part.
:::

::: check
Why can a functional hard gauge check a position tolerance at MMC, but not one at RFS?
:::

::: answer
At MMC the worst-case boundary — the virtual condition — is the same for every size of hole, because bonus exactly trades extra size for extra drift. A fixed steel pin at that size at true position therefore tests every hole fairly. At RFS the zone does not grow with size, so a larger hole is still held to the small zone, and a fixed pin cannot tell a good large hole from a badly placed one. That needs a measurement of the axis, usually on a CMM.
:::

## Summary

| Idea | Meaning | Formula or fact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Virtual condition, hole | clear space always inside the hole | MMC minus geometric tolerance |
| Virtual condition, shaft | sleeve the shaft always stays in | MMC plus geometric tolerance |
| Assembly guaranteed | pin fits hole for all good parts | pin VC at most hole VC |
| Functional gauge | pins at VC on datum simulators | checks position with bonus; size checked separately |
| Composite position, upper row | pattern-locating | zones fixed to all datums, larger |
| Composite position, lower row | feature-relating | zones float as a group, smaller, orientation datums only |
| Projected zone Ⓟ | zone moved above the surface | for threaded and press-fit holes |
| Tilt amplification | miss at height $h$ | $t\left(1 + \frac{2h}{L}\right)$ |

::: context gauge-plate Go, no-go, and the gauge's own tolerance
A **go/no-go gauge** for a hole is a pin with two ends. The "go" end is the hole's MMC size and must slide in; the "no-go" end is its LMC size and must not. Two pushes check size without reading a number. A functional position gauge works the same way but for location. Gauges are made to tolerances too, much tighter than the part's, and they are usually made so that any gauge error rejects a borderline part rather than accepting a bad one. Gauge makers treat that as a design rule, because a gauge that passes bad hardware is worse than no gauge.
:::

::: context resultant-condition The other worst case
Virtual condition is the worst case on the side of the mating part. **Resultant condition** is the worst case on the other side. For a hole at MMC it is the biggest the hole can reach outward: its LMC size plus the stated tolerance plus the full bonus. For the bracket hole, $6.8 + 0.25 + 0.2 = 7.25\,\mathrm{mm}$. No bolt cares about that number, but the metal around the hole does: it tells you how close the hole's wall can come to an edge or a neighboring pocket. That is the worry LMC was designed for in the last lesson.
:::

::: context two-single-segment Composite versus two stacked frames
A drawing can also stack **two separate position frames**, each with its own symbol, on one pattern. They look alike but read differently. In a composite frame, the lower row's datums control only orientation, so the small zones may slide and turn. In two stacked single-segment frames, each row is a full requirement on its own, so if the lower one names A and B, the small zones may slide along B but may not turn relative to it. Designers pick stacked frames when the pattern's rotation (its "clocking") matters as much as its spacing — as it can for a sensor whose rotation about its boresight shows up in the attitude solution.
:::

::: context stud-and-dowel Things fixed in a hole that stick out
A **stud** is a rod threaded at both ends: one end screws permanently into the part, and a nut goes on the other end after the mating part is slid over it. A **dowel pin** is a precise smooth pin pressed into a hole so tightly it stays put; its free end locates the next part exactly. A bolt screwed into a tapped hole behaves the same way while it is tightened. In all three, the part in the hole sets the angle, and the protruding length multiplies it. Engines, gimbal brackets and avionics trays use all three.
:::
