Everything in the previous four lessons was legal mechanism: statutes, regulatory definitions, the reach of a single rule. This lesson is about logistics — the ordinary, practical question of where, in a real hiring process, any of that mechanism actually surfaces. The answer is simple to state and worth understanding in full, because it changes how you should think about the earliest conversations of any application to a company building export-controlled hardware: eligibility is confirmed at the recruiter screen, the first live conversation of the process, before any substantial engineering time has been spent on you.

## Why it is asked first, not last

A typical hiring pipeline for an engineering role runs through several stages after an application is submitted: a recruiter screen, a hiring-manager or technical phone screen, one or more rounds of deeper technical evaluation — a take-home exercise, a panel, a virtual or in-person onsite — and, eventually, an offer. Every stage after the first consumes real time from people who are not in human resources: engineers reviewing a take-home submission, a panel of interviewers blocking hours out of their week, a hiring manager and a committee weighing a final decision. All of that time is a cost the company pays regardless of the outcome.

Export-control eligibility is not something a strong interview, a compelling portfolio, or an enthusiastic referral can change — the previous lessons established exactly why: it is federal law, not a merit judgment. A candidate who does not meet it is not a marginal hire who might be persuaded through; they are, for this specific company and role, not a hire at all, however strong their skills are. Given that, asking the question after two rounds of technical interviews would waste the time of everyone involved, on both sides. Asking it at the very first live conversation means that time is spent only on candidates for whom the rest of the process could possibly end in an offer. This is ordinary screening logic, the same reason a recruiter confirms basic logistics — work authorization generally, salary expectations, location — early rather than late. It is not a signal of hostility toward any group of applicants; it is where the highest-cost mistake would occur if the question were asked anywhere else.

::: key
Eligibility is confirmed at the recruiter screen — the first live conversation in the pipeline, typically a call of roughly twenty to thirty minutes — precisely because every later stage costs real engineering time that would be wasted on a candidate who cannot ultimately be hired for the role.
:::

## What "confirmed" means at this stage, and what comes later

At the recruiter screen, confirmation means self-attestation: you are asked directly, in plain language close to the posting's own paragraph, and you answer honestly. This is not the point where documents are produced or verified — nobody is asking to see a green card or a naturalization certificate on a twenty-minute call. It is the point where the basic fact pattern is established, so that both sides know whether continuing makes sense.

Formal documentary verification happens later and rests on a separate, universal requirement that has nothing to do with ITAR specifically: every employer in the United States, for every employee regardless of role or industry, must complete Form I-9 employment eligibility verification, examining specific documents that prove identity and authorization to work, generally within the first few days of employment. That is a Department of Homeland Security requirement that applies to a retail cashier exactly as it applies to a GNC engineer. The ITAR self-attestation at the recruiter screen and the I-9 documentation check at onboarding are two different things, run by two different parts of the process, for two different legal reasons — the first is specific to export-controlled roles and happens early because of what later stages cost, the second is universal to all US employment and happens at hiring because federal law requires it of every employer. A company handling export-controlled technical data will typically also confirm export-control status more formally as part of onboarding, on top of the universal I-9 step, but the recruiter screen is where you first learn, and the company first learns, whether the rest of the process is worth running.

::: warning A silent application form is not the same as never being asked
An online application that only collects a résumé and does not ask an eligibility question yet has not skipped the requirement — it has not yet reached the point in the process where it is asked. Expect the question at the first live conversation with a recruiter, even if the application itself said nothing about it.
:::

## This is an industry pattern, not a SpaceX quirk

Nothing about the timing above is specific to one company. Every US company that designs, builds, or tests hardware on USML Category IV or Category XV faces the identical statutory requirement under the Arms Export Control Act, and the efficiency logic for asking early applies to every one of them equally. A candidate interviewing across several US launch or spacecraft companies in the same search should expect a structurally identical question at a structurally identical point in each pipeline — not because the companies coordinate on hiring practice, but because they are all subject to the same regulation and face the same cost structure if they ask about it late. The wording will vary company to company, and some may fold the question into a written application step rather than a live call, but the underlying fact — that it is resolved early, before significant technical evaluation — holds across the industry, not only at one employer.

::: example A pipeline, stage by stage, with the two checks marked
A candidate applies for a GNC role at a launch vehicle company. The stages run, in order: application submitted online; recruiter screen, a short call covering background, motivation, logistics, and export-control eligibility; a technical phone screen with an engineer from the team; a further round of technical evaluation, which might be a take-home exercise, a panel, or both; a hiring decision; an offer, if extended; and, once the offer is accepted and a start date approaches, onboarding paperwork, which includes Form I-9 employment eligibility verification and, for this kind of role, formal export-control documentation.

The export-control question is resolved at the second stage — the recruiter screen — by a direct, honest answer. The I-9 check happens at the final stage, well after the eligibility question has already been settled, and it verifies work authorization generally rather than export-control status specifically. A candidate who is confused about why a recruiter is asking a legal-sounding question so early has the order backwards: the question is early precisely so nobody spends the four stages in between on a role that cannot end in an offer.
:::

::: example Two honest answers, and why vagueness helps neither side
A recruiter asks a candidate directly whether they are a U.S. citizen or national, a lawful permanent resident, a refugee, or an asylee, or otherwise authorized. One candidate, a lawful permanent resident, answers plainly: yes, green card holder. The conversation moves on to the next topic within a sentence.

A second candidate, on an F-1 student visa with Optional Practical Training, is tempted to answer vaguely — mentioning strong qualifications, a willingness to figure out visa questions later, or asking whether the company sponsors work authorization generally, without directly addressing the export-control question asked. This does not change the underlying fact pattern from the previous four lessons: an F-1/OPT candidate does not hold one of the four statuses, and general work-visa sponsorship is a different question from export-control eligibility entirely. A direct, honest "no, not currently one of the four statuses" answer, followed by a genuine question about whether the company ever pursues the residual authorization path from lesson three, gives the recruiter accurate information to act on and gives the candidate a real answer in minutes rather than an ambiguous outcome weeks later. Vagueness here costs the candidate time without changing the result.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
Why does asking the export-control question at the recruiter screen, rather than at the offer stage, serve both the company's and the candidate's interests?
:::

::: answer
It serves the company's interest by avoiding wasted engineering time on interview stages that cannot lead to an offer for an ineligible candidate. It serves the candidate's interest equally well: an honest early answer means an ineligible candidate learns the outcome for that specific role within a twenty- to thirty-minute call rather than after investing hours in take-home exercises and interview preparation for a role that was never going to result in an offer, freeing that time for opportunities that could actually work out.
:::

::: check
A candidate has only submitted an online application and has not yet had a live conversation with anyone at the company. Is it accurate to say the company has "not asked about eligibility yet," and does that mean the requirement does not apply to this role?
:::

::: answer
It is accurate that the question has not been asked yet in this specific candidate's process, but that reflects the pipeline's stage, not an absence of the requirement. The requirement is a property of the role — set by what the role involves under export-control law — not something that only exists once a recruiter happens to say it aloud. Expect it to surface at the first live conversation.
:::

::: check
Explain the difference between what is being checked at a recruiter screen's eligibility question and what is being checked by Form I-9 at onboarding.
:::

::: answer
The recruiter screen's eligibility question is a self-attested check, specific to export-controlled roles, confirming whether the candidate holds one of the four U.S.-person statuses or is otherwise authorized under ITAR — asked early because of what later interview stages would otherwise cost if run on an ineligible candidate. Form I-9 is a universal, document-based check required by federal law for every U.S. employer and every employee, regardless of role or industry, verifying identity and general work authorization, completed near the start of employment rather than during screening. They test related but distinct things, run at different points for different legal reasons.
:::

::: check
A candidate interviews with three different US launch vehicle companies in the same month and notices that all three ask a nearly identical export-control question at the recruiter-screen stage. What explains the similarity, given that the companies do not coordinate their hiring processes with each other?
:::

::: answer
All three companies are subject to the identical statutory requirement under the Arms Export Control Act and ITAR, since all three build hardware on the same controlled categories of the US Munitions List. The efficiency logic for asking early — avoiding wasted evaluation time on an ultimately ineligible candidate — applies equally to each of them, independent of any coordination between the companies. The similarity comes from all three facing the same law and the same cost structure, not from shared hiring practice.
:::

::: check
A candidate who is not currently a U.S. person is asked the eligibility question at a recruiter screen. What is the most useful way to respond, and why is answering vaguely a worse strategy even for that candidate's own interests?
:::

::: answer
The most useful response is a direct, honest statement of the actual status, optionally followed by a genuine question about whether the company ever pursues an individual authorization for exceptional cases. Vagueness does not change the underlying fact pattern — the candidate's status is what it is regardless of how the answer is phrased — so answering evasively only delays an outcome that will not change, at the cost of the candidate's own time and the possibility of redirecting effort toward a role or an employer where they are actually eligible.
:::

## Summary

| Stage | What is checked | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Application | Nothing export-control-specific yet | — |
| Recruiter screen | Export-control eligibility, by self-attestation | ITAR, 22 CFR § 120.62 and related provisions |
| Technical stages | Skill and fit, not eligibility | Role requirements |
| Offer | Terms of employment | Company decision |
| Onboarding | Formal work-authorization documents | Form I-9, plus company export-compliance onboarding |
