Think about a theme park ride with a height sign at the entrance: "You must be this tall to ride." The sign is at the *front* of the line, not at the seat. Imagine if it were at the seat instead. Kids would wait an hour in the sun, reach the car, get measured, and be sent away. Everyone's time — theirs, their parents', the ride staff's — would be wasted. So the check goes first.

The previous four lessons were legal machinery: statutes, definitions, the reach of one rule. This lesson is about the everyday question of *where* in a real hiring process all of that shows up. The answer is short. Eligibility is confirmed at the **[[recruiter screen|recruiter]]** — the first live conversation in the process — before anyone has spent real engineering time on you.

That fact changes how you should treat the earliest conversation with any company that builds export-controlled hardware. It is not small talk. It is the height sign.

## The stages of a hiring process

A hiring process is often called a **[[pipeline|funnel]]**, because candidates go in at one end and move through stages one after another, and fewer come out at each step. For an engineering role it usually runs like this after you apply online:

1. **Recruiter screen** — a short call with a recruiter, the person whose job is to find and move candidates through the process.
2. **Technical phone screen** — a call with an engineer or the **hiring manager**, the person who will be your boss.
3. **Deeper technical evaluation** — one or more rounds: a **[[take-home exercise|take-home]]**, a panel of interviewers, a virtual or in-person **onsite** (a day of interviews at the company).
4. **Decision and offer.**
5. **Onboarding** — the paperwork and setup before and right after your first day.

Every stage after the first uses the time of people who are not recruiters. Engineers review your take-home. A panel blocks out hours of their week. A hiring manager and a committee weigh a decision. The company pays for all of that time whatever the outcome.

## Why it is asked first, not last

Now add what earlier lessons established. Export-control eligibility is federal law, not a judgment of how good you are. A strong interview, a brilliant portfolio or an enthusiastic referral cannot change it. A candidate who does not meet it is not a borderline hire who might be persuaded through. For this company and this role, they are not a hire at all, however skilled.

So think it through. If the question came after two rounds of technical interviews, all that time would be thrown away — the company's and the candidate's. If it comes at the first live conversation, the expensive stages are spent only on people for whom an offer is actually possible.

This is ordinary screening logic. It is the same reason a recruiter checks basic logistics early — work authorization in general, salary expectations, location. It is not a sign of hostility toward any group of applicants. It sits where a late answer would cause the most expensive waste.

::: key
Eligibility is confirmed at the recruiter screen — the first live conversation in the pipeline, typically a call of roughly twenty to thirty minutes — precisely because every later stage costs real engineering time that would be wasted on a candidate who cannot ultimately be hired for the role.
:::

### How the question is asked

Expect it to sound close to the posting's own paragraph. A recruiter might ask something like: "Are you a US citizen or national, a lawful permanent resident, a refugee or an asylee?" Sometimes it is shorter — "Are you a US person for export-control purposes?" — and sometimes it comes as a written question on a form before the call. The wording varies. The substance is always those four statuses, plus the residual path from lesson three.

## What "confirmed" means now, and what comes later

At the recruiter screen, "confirmed" means **[[self-attestation|self-attestation]]**: you are asked directly and you answer honestly, in your own words. Nobody asks to see a green card or a naturalization certificate on a twenty-minute call. The point is to establish the basic facts, so both sides know whether going on makes sense.

Checking actual documents happens later, and it rests on a separate requirement that has nothing to do with ITAR. Every employer in the United States, for every employee, in every industry, must complete **[[Form I-9|form-i9]]** — employment eligibility verification. The employer examines specific documents that prove who you are and that you may work in the US, and it must finish this within your first few days on the job. It is a Department of Homeland Security requirement. It applies to a store cashier exactly as it applies to a GNC engineer.

So there are two different checks, run at two different times, for two different legal reasons:

| | Recruiter screen question | Form I-9 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it asks | Are you one of the four US-person statuses (or otherwise authorized)? | Who are you, and may you work in the US at all? |
| How | Your own honest answer | Real documents, examined by the employer |
| When | First live conversation | Around your first day |
| Why then | Later stages are expensive | Federal law requires it of every employer |
| Who faces it | Candidates for export-controlled roles | Every new employee in the country |

A company handling controlled technical data will usually also confirm export-control status more formally during onboarding, on top of the I-9. But the recruiter screen is where you first learn — and the company first learns — whether the rest of the process is worth running.

::: warning A silent application form is not the same as never being asked
An online application that only collects a résumé and asks nothing about eligibility has not skipped the requirement. It has not yet reached the point where the question is asked. Expect it at your first live conversation with a recruiter, even if the application said nothing about it.
:::

## An industry pattern, not one company's quirk

None of this timing is special to SpaceX. Every US company that designs, builds or tests hardware in **[[USML Category IV or XV|usml-categories]]** faces the identical requirement under the Arms Export Control Act. The logic for asking early applies to all of them equally.

So if you interview at several US launch or spacecraft companies in the same search, expect a very similar question at a very similar point in each process. That is not because the companies coordinate their hiring. It is because they all live under the same law and face the same cost of asking late. The wording will differ, and some companies put the question in a written application step instead of a live call. But the underlying fact holds across the industry: it is settled early, before serious technical evaluation.

::: example A pipeline, stage by stage, with the two checks marked
A candidate applies for a GNC role at a launch vehicle company. The stages, in order:

1. Application submitted online.
2. Recruiter screen — a short call covering background, motivation, logistics and export-control eligibility.
3. Technical phone screen with an engineer from the team.
4. Further technical evaluation — a take-home exercise, a panel, or both.
5. Hiring decision.
6. Offer, if extended.
7. Onboarding, once the offer is accepted and a start date approaches. This includes Form I-9 and, for this kind of role, formal export-control documentation.

**Where the export-control question is settled:** stage 2, by a direct, honest answer.

**Where the I-9 happens:** stage 7, well after eligibility is already settled. It checks work authorization in general, not export-control status in particular.

Count the stages in between: 3, 4, 5 and 6 — four stages. A candidate puzzled that a recruiter asks a legal-sounding question so early has the order backwards. The question comes early precisely so that nobody spends those four stages on a role that cannot end in an offer.
:::

::: example Two honest answers, and why vagueness helps neither side
A recruiter asks directly: "Are you a US citizen or national, a lawful permanent resident, a refugee or an asylee, or otherwise authorized?"

**Candidate one** is a lawful permanent resident. She answers plainly: "Yes — I'm a green card holder." The conversation moves on within a sentence.

**Candidate two** is on an **[[F-1 student visa with Optional Practical Training|f1-opt]]**. He is tempted to answer vaguely: he mentions strong qualifications, says he is willing to figure out visa questions later, or asks whether the company sponsors work visas in general — without answering the question he was asked.

Check that against the earlier lessons. F-1 with OPT is not one of the four statuses. And whether a company sponsors work visas is a completely different question from export-control eligibility. So vagueness changes nothing about the facts.

The better answer is direct: "No, I'm not currently one of those four statuses." He can follow it with a real question: "Does the company ever pursue individual authorization for a role like this?" — the residual path from lesson three. That gives the recruiter accurate information to act on. It gives the candidate a real answer in minutes, instead of an unclear outcome weeks later. Vagueness costs him time and leaves the result exactly where it was.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
Why does asking the export-control question at the recruiter screen, rather than at the offer stage, serve both the company's and the candidate's interests?
:::

::: answer
It serves the company by not wasting engineering time on interview stages that cannot lead to an offer for an ineligible candidate. It serves the candidate equally well: an honest early answer means an ineligible candidate learns the outcome for that role within a twenty- to thirty-minute call, instead of after hours of take-home work and interview preparation for a role that was never going to produce an offer. That frees the time for opportunities that could actually work out.
:::

::: check
A candidate has only submitted an online application and has not yet spoken to anyone at the company. Is it accurate to say the company has "not asked about eligibility yet," and does that mean the requirement does not apply to this role?
:::

::: answer
It is accurate that the question has not been asked yet in this candidate's process — but that reflects the stage of the pipeline, not a missing requirement. The requirement belongs to the role, set by what the work involves under export-control law. It does not come into existence only when a recruiter says it out loud. Expect it at the first live conversation.
:::

::: check
Explain the difference between what the recruiter screen's eligibility question checks and what Form I-9 checks at onboarding.
:::

::: answer
The recruiter screen question is a self-attested check, specific to export-controlled roles, of whether the candidate holds one of the four US-person statuses or is otherwise authorized under ITAR. It comes early because later interview stages are expensive to run on an ineligible candidate. Form I-9 is a document-based check that federal law requires of every US employer for every employee, whatever the role or industry. It verifies identity and general work authorization and is completed around the start of employment, not during screening. They test related but different things, at different points, for different legal reasons.
:::

::: check
A candidate interviews with three different US launch vehicle companies in the same month. All three ask a nearly identical export-control question at the recruiter screen. What explains the similarity, given that the companies do not coordinate their hiring?
:::

::: answer
All three are subject to the same requirement under the Arms Export Control Act and ITAR, because all three build hardware in the same controlled categories of the US Munitions List. The logic of asking early — not wasting evaluation time on a candidate who cannot be hired — applies equally to each, with no coordination needed. The similarity comes from the same law and the same cost structure, not from shared hiring practice.
:::

::: check
A candidate who is not currently a US person is asked the eligibility question at a recruiter screen. What is the most useful way to respond, and why is a vague answer worse even for the candidate's own interests?
:::

::: answer
The most useful response is a direct, honest statement of the actual status, optionally followed by a real question about whether the company ever pursues individual authorization in exceptional cases. Vagueness does not change the facts — the candidate's status is what it is however the answer is worded. An evasive answer only delays an outcome that will not change, at the cost of the candidate's own time and of the chance to redirect effort toward employers and roles where they are actually eligible.
:::

## Summary

| Stage | What is checked | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Application | Nothing export-control-specific yet | — |
| Recruiter screen (about 20–30 min) | Export-control eligibility, by self-attestation | ITAR, 22 CFR § 120.62 and related provisions |
| Technical stages | Skill and fit, not eligibility | Role requirements |
| Offer | Terms of employment | Company decision |
| Onboarding | Formal work-authorization documents | Form I-9, plus company export-compliance onboarding |

So far everything has been about the ITAR gate itself. The next lesson turns to three things that sometimes ride alongside it in a posting — a security clearance, drug and alcohol testing, and a stated expectation of long hours — and sorts out which of them is a government process and which are the employer's own conditions.

::: context recruiter What a recruiter does
A **recruiter** is the person whose job is to find candidates and steer them through a company's hiring process. An **in-house** recruiter works for the company itself; an **agency** recruiter works for an outside firm that companies pay to find people. Recruiters are usually not engineers. They are not judging your Kalman filter on the first call. They are checking the facts that decide whether the rest of the process can happen — eligibility, location, timing, salary range — and whether you seem like a real fit for the team's needs.
:::

::: context funnel Why it is called a funnel
Many people enter at the top; far fewer come out at the bottom. Each stage lets through only some of the candidates from the stage before. The eligibility question sits at the top, where it is cheapest to ask.

::: context take-home What a take-home exercise is
A **take-home** is a technical problem the company sends you to solve on your own time — for a GNC role it might be writing a small simulation, tuning a controller, or analyzing a data set, then writing up your approach. It can take several hours or more. Engineers on the team then read and grade it. That reviewing time is one of the costs the company avoids by checking eligibility first.
:::

::: context self-attestation Your word first, documents later
To **attest** is to state that something is true; **self-attestation** means you state it about yourself, without proof in hand. It works because the proof comes later: at onboarding, documents are checked. An honest answer on the first call and the paperwork at the end must match. A candidate who claims a status they do not hold will be found out at the document stage, usually with an offer withdrawn and a burned bridge — after wasting everyone's time, which is the very thing the early question exists to prevent.
:::

::: context form-i9 Where Form I-9 comes from
Form I-9 was created by the Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986, which made employers responsible for checking that every new hire may work in the US. The new employee fills in the first section by their first day of work. The employer then examines the documents — a US passport, for example, or a combination like a driver's license plus a Social Security card — and completes its section within three business days of the start date.

::: context usml-categories A reminder from lesson one
The **US Munitions List** (USML) is the list of defense articles ITAR controls, split into numbered categories. Category IV covers launch vehicles, missiles and rockets; Category XV covers spacecraft and related items. A company whose work falls in these categories handles controlled technical data every day, which is why its postings carry the export-control paragraph and its recruiters ask about it early.
:::

::: context f1-opt What F-1 and OPT mean
An **F-1** visa lets an international student study full-time at a US college or university. **Optional Practical Training**, or OPT, lets that student work in their field for a limited time, typically around graduation — a year, with a possible extension for science and engineering graduates. It is real permission to work in the US. It is not US-person status, so for ITAR purposes an F-1 student on OPT is a foreign person, however long they have studied here.
:::
