Say you have worked through this module honestly and found that none of the four statuses fits you. Then this lesson and the next are the two that matter most for you. They follow the same rule as everything before them: say what is true, point to what you can check, and make nothing up.

This is not a pat on the back to soften bad news. It is a map. Several of the roads on it lead to real, well-paid careers in guidance, navigation and control (GNC). Each one deserves the same careful reading you gave the gate itself.

First, close off one wrong turn. When one door shuts, the natural move is to try the door next to it: "I'll apply to a smaller US rocket company instead." That is usually not an escape. Any company that builds a real orbital rocket — the kind described in the first lesson of this module — is building an item on **[[Category IV of the US Munitions List|usml-categories]]**. So it faces exactly the same law SpaceX does, the Arms Export Control Act. Size, age and a relaxed careers page change nothing. A newer, smaller launch company is not a side door. It is the same door with a different sign on it.

Real alternatives do exist. They sit outside that kind of company, and this lesson names them by category, so that you can judge a real posting instead of a rumor.

## Inside the United States, but outside Categories IV and XV

Think of a school with two sets of rules: a strict set for the chemistry lab and a looser set for the library. Both are rules. But the library's are easier to meet.

The US works a bit like that. Not everything near space sits on the Munitions List. Recall from the first lesson that during **[[Export Control Reform|export-control-reform]]** in the 2010s, a lot of commercial satellite and component hardware without sensitive capability moved to the Commerce Department. There it is governed by the **EAR** (said letter by letter, "E-A-R") — the *Export Administration Regulations*, Commerce's own rulebook, which uses a list called the Commerce Control List instead of the Munitions List.

The EAR is a real set of controls, not an empty one. It still blocks release to people from countries under full US sanctions or arms embargoes. Some space items that moved to Commerce still need a license before they can be shown to most foreign nationals, so check company by company. But in general, the EAR's rules about *who* may see what are much less strict than ITAR's. Companies that work only with EAR-governed or uncontrolled space hardware are a real category of US employer where a wider range of statuses and visas can work.

A second, larger category sits outside the space industry altogether. At its heart, GNC is the mathematics of working out where a system is and steering it toward a target when you are never quite sure. That same mathematics runs:

- self-driving cars and robots;
- industrial process control, the automatic control of factories and plants;
- avionics for commercial (non-military) aircraft;
- simulation and modeling software sold to many industries;
- **[[quantitative finance|quant-finance]]**, which uses the same estimation theory as this course's Kalman-filter and optimal-control modules.

None of these is free of export control by default. Some robot and drone hardware has its own Munitions List entries, and any single program can carry its own restrictions. But as a group, they are not gated by the launch-vehicle rule this module has spent six lessons on. A non-US-person's skill moves into them without any change.

::: key
A different US launch or spacecraft company that actually builds Category IV or Category XV hardware is not an alternative to the ITAR gate — it is the identical gate. Genuine US-based alternatives are companies working in EAR-governed or uncontrolled hardware, and non-space industries — autonomous vehicles, robotics, industrial control, avionics, simulation software, quantitative finance — that use the same GNC mathematics without USML-specific restriction.
:::

## Europe: two different gates, not one missing gate

Europe holds two very different kinds of space employer, and they ask two different questions.

The first is the **[[European Space Agency|esa-not-eu]]** (ESA, said "E-S-A" or "ee-sa"). ESA is not a way around a US-person rule, because it has a rule of its own. Its vacancy notices require the candidate to be a national of an ESA member state, an associate member, or Canada as a cooperating state. Each notice prints the exact list of eligible nationalities in its footer. That list does not match European Union membership exactly, so check it on every posting instead of assuming. A candidate from outside Europe does not pass merely by being outside the United States. ESA asks a nationality question — shaped differently from ITAR's, but every bit as real.

The second is European industry. Big companies such as Airbus Defence and Space, Thales Alenia Space, ArianeGroup and OHB, plus a growing set of European rocket builders such as Isar Aerospace, Rocket Factory Augsburg and PLD Space, generally hire on the basis of the **[[right to work|right-to-work]]** in the country where the job is — that is, whether you hold, or can get, legal permission to work there. That is a visa question, not a nationality question. For most engineers it is a much more reachable gate than the four-status test.

This does not make Europe control-free. The European Union has its own Dual-Use Regulation — rules for items that have both civilian and military uses — and each member state has its own arms-export list. On top of that, a European company handling US-origin technology under an American export license inherits whatever access limits that license carries. The European employer has no power to waive those.

So the useful difference is this. In most of European industry, the question is usually "can you legally work here?", which the country's normal immigration system can answer. The ITAR and ESA question is different: "are you a particular kind of person — by birth or by a status you gained?"

::: example Two European postings, read the way this module taught you
A GNC engineer from outside the EU, who is not a US person, looks at two postings.

**Posting one:** an ESA vacancy for a guidance and control engineer. Its footer lists eligible nationalities: ESA member and associate states, plus Canada. The candidate's nationality is not on the list.

- Step 1: what kind of test is it? A nationality test.
- Step 2: does the candidate pass it? No.
- Result: not eligible, however skilled. This is the same shape of answer as a non-US-person under 22 CFR § 120.62 (read "twenty-two C-F-R section one-twenty point sixty-two"), the US-person definition from lesson two.

**Posting two:** a guidance system engineer at a European rocket company. It says the successful candidate must have, or be able to obtain, the right to work in the country where the role is based.

- Step 1: what kind of test is it? A work-permission test.
- Step 2: does the candidate pass it? That depends on the country's own rules for skilled workers, not directly on the candidate's nationality.
- Result: possibly eligible — a very different question, and for many candidates a reachable one.

Sanity check: both postings are European, both are space jobs, and both use the word "eligibility". Yet they give opposite answers once you read what each one actually asks. That is the skill.
:::

## The United Kingdom: its own rules, mostly organized around the right to work

The UK controls military and dual-use exports under the Export Control Act 2002 and the Export Control Order 2008. These are run by the **Export Control Joint Unit** (ECJU), a government team shared by several departments. It works from a combined list: the UK Military List plus separate dual-use lists.

Ordinary hiring for UK space engineering — at long-standing firms such as Surrey Satellite Technology (SSTL) and at newer companies across the UK's growing launch and small-satellite scene — is generally organized around the right to work in the UK. A qualified engineer can gain that through the UK's points-based **[[skilled-worker visa|skilled-worker-visa]]**. Nationality is not the base test.

As in the rest of Europe, this is a real, separate set of controls, not an absence of one. A particular role touching UK-controlled technology, or US-origin technology under a US license, can carry its own access limit on top of general work permission. But the gate most candidates actually meet is a work-permission question, answered through ordinary immigration — not a status test with the same shape as ITAR's.

## Canada: a status-based gate that is narrower than it first looks

Canada has its own system for controlled defense goods and technology: the **[[Controlled Goods Program|controlled-goods-program]]**. Anyone who examines, possesses or transfers a "controlled good" in Canada must be registered. For an individual, registration generally requires Canadian citizenship or permanent residence, and ordinarily living in Canada.

That is a status-based test with real teeth — close to ITAR's in shape — for the roles that actually touch controlled goods. The program has specific exemptions. One covers an officer, director or employee of a person already registered under the US ITAR system, which shows how tightly the two countries' systems are linked. That exemption is for a company's compliance office to apply correctly. An individual candidate should not count on it without professional advice.

The narrower point matters more for planning. A large share of space engineering work does not trigger registration at all: ground-systems software, mission operations, data processing, and roles at companies whose product is not itself a controlled good. Those roles are open to anyone with ordinary Canadian work permission.

Canada's wider immigration system is built around points-based skilled-worker programs. It is generally seen as a more predictable, application-based route to permanent residence than the US employment-based green card system. That matters here for a specific reason: permanent residence is exactly the status the Controlled Goods Program asks of individuals.

**[[MDA|mda-canadarm]]**, the company behind the Canadarm program and a long-standing part of Canadian space, is one sign that a real space industry exists there. It is named as evidence the sector exists, not as a promise of open jobs.

::: example The same controlled-goods question, asked of two roles at one company
A Canadian space company posts two openings.

**Role one:** designing a radio-frequency (RF) payload subsystem that appears on Canada's controlled-goods list.

- Does the person examine or possess a controlled good? Yes, directly.
- So registration is required, and with it the general expectation of Canadian citizenship or permanent residence.

**Role two:** mission-operations software — tools for the ground segment, meaning the computers and antennas on Earth that run a mission. The work never involves examining, possessing or transferring a listed controlled good.

- Does the person examine or possess a controlled good? No.
- So the program does not apply, and hiring rests on ordinary Canadian work permission.

Same company, two roles, two different gates. Sanity check: this is the lesson-three habit — read what the specific requirement says, not what the company's name suggests — now used outside the United States.
:::

## Australia: its own version of ITAR, with the same shape of exception

Australia's Defence Trade Controls Act 2012 is widely described, including by Australian legal commentators, as the country's own version of ITAR. Controlled goods and technology sit on the **Defence and Strategic Goods List** (DSGL), and supplying or transferring them generally needs a permit.

Australia's space sector has grown a lot since the Australian Space Agency was set up in 2018. Companies such as Gilmour Space Technologies, Fleet Space Technologies and Southern Launch make up a real, if still young, local launch and space industry. They are named as evidence the sector exists, not as a promise of open roles at any given time. As in the UK and most of European industry, ordinary engineering hiring is generally organized around the right to work in Australia, gained through its skilled-visa system, for roles that do not directly touch DSGL-controlled technology.

One recent change is worth knowing, but not worth leaning on. Amendments in 2024, carrying out Australia's **[[AUKUS|aukus]]** commitments, created a license-free environment for many DSGL-controlled transfers among Australian, UK and US citizens, permanent residents, and government or company bodies. This is trade between governments and companies made easier. It is not a personal-eligibility rule that a job seeker can call on. It does nothing for someone who is not a citizen or permanent resident of Australia, the UK or the US. It is here so you recognize the word, not as a path to plan around.

::: warning Elsewhere is not automatically easier — it is differently organized
Reading "Europe" or "Canada" or "Australia" as one word meaning "no gate" is the very mistake this module has trained you out of, now made in a new country. Each place above has its own real export-control system. Some — ESA, and Canada's Controlled Goods Program most of all — use a status-based test with the same shape as ITAR's.

The true difference is not rules versus no rules. It is that, for most ordinary engineering roles in most of these places, the test is **[[work permission rather than nationality|two-kinds-of-gate]]** or citizenship. With time and the right qualifications, most skilled engineers can eventually clear that gate through the normal immigration system. The US ITAR test's four fixed statuses do not bend that way for a non-US-person at all.
:::

::: key
If you are not a US person, the technical curriculum still transfers completely. The target set changes: European, UK, Canadian, Australian and Japanese space companies, academic and research roles, and non-ITAR commercial work, with long-horizon immigration as a separate and slow track.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
A non-US-person candidate decides that SpaceX's requirement is a company policy, so a different, smaller US launch company will probably be more flexible. Judge this reasoning using what lesson one established.
:::

::: answer
The reasoning gets the source of the requirement wrong. It is not a SpaceX policy choice. It follows from building a USML Category IV article under the Arms Export Control Act, a law that applies the same way to every US company doing that kind of work, whatever its size or age. A smaller US launch company building real orbital-class hardware faces the same legal requirement, not a relaxed version of it.
:::

::: check
Explain why the European Space Agency and a European industrial company such as Airbus Defence and Space can have very different eligibility requirements for the same kind of engineering role.
:::

::: answer
ESA is a multinational agency. Its staff eligibility is set by its own nationality-based membership rules, printed on each vacancy — a status-based gate in shape. A European industrial company is a private employer working under national and EU export-control law, but its ordinary hiring gate for most engineering roles is whether you can legally work in the country. That is a visa or right-to-work question, answered through the country's immigration system, not through agency membership rules. Both sit inside "the European space sector", but they are governed by different frameworks.
:::

::: check
A candidate reads that Canada's Controlled Goods Program requires citizenship or permanent residence for individual registration, and concludes that no space role in Canada is reachable without Canadian status. Is that right?
:::

::: answer
No. Registration is required only for people who examine, possess or transfer an actual controlled good. A large share of space work — ground-systems software, mission operations and other roles with no direct access to a listed controlled good — does not trigger it at all, and is reachable with ordinary Canadian work permission. The gate is role by role, not a blanket rule across the whole Canadian space sector.
:::

::: check
What does Australia's 2024 AUKUS-related licensing change actually do, and why does it not directly help a job seeker who is not a citizen or permanent resident of Australia, the UK or the US?
:::

::: answer
It creates a license-free environment for many transfers of DSGL-controlled goods and technology among Australian, UK and US citizens, permanent residents, and government or company bodies, making defense trade and cooperation between those three countries and their industries easier. It is a trade arrangement between three specific countries, not a personal-eligibility rule. So it changes nothing about the hiring eligibility of a candidate who is not a citizen or permanent resident of one of those three.
:::

::: check
State, in general terms, the one pattern that repeats across ESA, the UK, Canada and Australia in this lesson, and the one exception to it that appears twice.
:::

::: answer
The pattern: for most engineering roles, eligibility in these places depends on the right to work there legally, which each country's own immigration system can grant — not on a fixed status that cannot bend. The exception: ESA's nationality-based staff eligibility, and Canada's Controlled Goods Program registration for roles that touch controlled goods. Both are status-based gates, closer in shape to ITAR's four-status test than to an ordinary work-permission question.
:::

::: context usml-categories What "Category IV" means
The US Munitions List is split into numbered categories, written in Roman numerals, one for each family of military items. **Category IV** covers launch vehicles, guided missiles, ballistic missiles, rockets and closely related parts. **Category XV** (fifteen) covers spacecraft and their equipment.

A rocket that can reach orbit and a missile that can cross an ocean share most of their hardware and nearly all of their guidance mathematics. That is why a civilian launch vehicle sits in the same category as missiles, and why every orbital launch company in the US carries the same obligation.
:::

::: context export-control-reform When satellites moved to Commerce
Through the 1990s and 2000s, nearly all satellite hardware sat on the Munitions List. US satellite makers argued this was costing them sales abroad, since buyers could get similar parts elsewhere without ITAR paperwork.

A government-wide effort called **Export Control Reform** answered that. In 2014, a large share of commercial satellite hardware without sensitive capability moved to the Commerce Control List, mostly into entries numbered in the **9x515** family. Launch vehicles did not move. They stayed in Category IV.

Being on the Commerce list still means a license may be needed before some items are shown to foreign nationals — so "moved to Commerce" means "different rules", not "no rules".
:::

::: context quant-finance Why a trading desk wants a guidance engineer
A **quant** — short for quantitative analyst — builds mathematical models of markets. One core task is estimating a hidden quantity, such as the "true" trend in a noisy price, from a stream of imperfect measurements.

That is the same problem as estimating a spacecraft's position from noisy sensor readings. The Kalman filter, which you will meet later in this course, was built for navigation and is used in finance too. The mathematics does not care whether the noisy signal is a star tracker or a stock price.

::: context esa-not-eu ESA and the EU are different clubs
It is easy to assume the European Space Agency is part of the European Union. It is not. ESA is a separate organization set up by treaty between governments, and its membership overlaps with the EU's without matching it.

The United Kingdom, Norway and Switzerland, for example, are ESA members but not EU members. Canada, not in Europe at all, takes part through a long-standing cooperation agreement. That is why an ESA vacancy's footer is the only reliable guide to who can apply — you cannot work it out from the EU map.
:::

::: context right-to-work What "the right to work" means
Every country decides who may take a job inside its borders. Its own citizens always can. Everyone else needs permission, usually a **work visa** or a **work permit** — a document tied to a job, a skill level or a points score.

"Right to work" is the plain phrase for holding that permission. It is a different thing from nationality. Nationality is about who you *are*; right to work is about what you are *allowed to do* in that country right now. You cannot change your nationality by applying for a job, but you can often gain the right to work by landing one.
:::

::: context skilled-worker-visa How the UK's skilled-worker visa works
The UK's **points-based system** scores applicants against set requirements instead of relying on case-by-case judgment. For the skilled-worker route, the core requirements are a job offer from an employer the government has licensed to sponsor visas, a job at a high enough skill level, a salary above the set threshold, and a sufficient level of English.

The thresholds change over time, so the UK government's own pages are the place to check current figures. The point for this lesson is the shape: a qualified engineer with a job offer has a clear, rules-based route in, which is not true of the ITAR test.
:::

::: context controlled-goods-program Canada's Controlled Goods Program
The program was set up under Canada's Defence Production Act and is run by a federal department, Public Services and Procurement Canada. It registers businesses and people who handle "controlled goods" — a list that closely tracks military and some space items, many of them also on the US Munitions List.

The close match is deliberate. Canada and the US have long traded defense goods across their shared border with fewer barriers than most countries, and Canada's program is one way it shows the US that controlled items stay protected once they arrive.

::: context mda-canadarm MDA and the Canadarm
The **Canadarm** was the robotic arm flown on the Space Shuttle, a Canadian contribution that became a national symbol. Its successor, **Canadarm2**, has worked on the International Space Station since 2001 and is pictured on Canada's five-dollar note. **Canadarm3** was designed for NASA's planned Gateway station around the Moon.

MDA's robotics business grew out of that line of work. Moving a large arm precisely, without shaking the station it is bolted to, is a control problem — the same family of mathematics this course teaches.
:::

::: context aukus What AUKUS is
**AUKUS** (said "AW-kus") is built from the initials of its three members: Australia, the United Kingdom and the United States. Announced in 2021, it is a security partnership whose best-known part is helping Australia acquire nuclear-powered submarines. A second part covers shared advanced technology.

Sharing technology that fast needed each country to loosen its export rules toward the other two. Australia's 2024 amendments were its side of that bargain. The benefit flows to the three countries' citizens, permanent residents and organizations, not to anyone else.
:::

::: context two-kinds-of-gate Two kinds of gate, one map
