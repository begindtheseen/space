This lesson finishes the pair the last one started, and it finishes the module. It covers three more doors. The first is Japan's space sector. The second is university research, which opens through a specific, named legal rule. The third is the long, slow path toward one of the four US-person statuses.

Then it makes the argument this whole module has been building toward. Wherever the last seven lessons leave you personally, none of them changes what the rest of this course is worth.

## Japan: a real sector, with its own legal and practical texture

Japan's space program has two parts, much like the US. There is a national agency, **[[JAXA|jaxa]]** (said "JACK-sa"), the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency. And there is an industrial base. Its leaders include Mitsubishi Heavy Industries, which built the **[[H-IIA and builds the H3|japanese-rockets]]** launch vehicles, and IHI Aerospace. Around them is a newer generation of companies such as ispace and Astroscale.

The clearest written nationality rule in this sector is narrow. It applies to JAXA's **astronaut corps** — the people selected to fly. Every astronaut application round has required Japanese nationality, along with other criteria such as relevant work experience and language ability. That is a solid fact about one career path. It is not a rule about every engineering job at JAXA or in Japanese industry, and you should not read it as one.

For ordinary engineering jobs — at JAXA outside the astronaut corps, or at Mitsubishi Heavy Industries, IHI Aerospace or the newer companies — the gate for a non-Japanese candidate is Japan's standard **[[work-visa system|japan-work-visa]]**. The usual category covers engineering and specialist work. It needs a qualifying job offer and relevant qualifications, not Japanese nationality.

The more common barrier is not a law at all. It is **language**. Japanese engineering organizations usually work in Japanese, and many roles expect working fluency to do the job well, whatever the visa rules say. That barrier is real, and it deserves an honest mention. But it is a practical barrier, not a legal eligibility rule like ITAR or ESA's nationality list.

## Academia: a different mechanism, not a friendlier version of industry

Every other door so far has been a variation on one theme: some country's version of a status gate or a work-permission gate. University research is built differently. A specific, named exclusion in the regulations applies to it directly. It is not merely that universities are more relaxed.

Here is an everyday picture. A recipe printed in a cookbook is not a secret, and nobody needs permission to read it. A restaurant's private recipe, kept in a locked drawer, is a different matter. Export control treats research in a similar way: what is openly published is treated differently from what is kept private.

Both ITAR and the Commerce Department's EAR recognize the **fundamental research exclusion** — the rule that openly published university research is generally not controlled technical data. It is rooted in a 1985 presidential directive, **[[NSDD-189|nsdd-189]]** (National Security Decision Directive 189). That directive defines **fundamental research** as basic and applied research in science and engineering whose results are ordinarily published and shared broadly within the scientific community, as distinct from proprietary research and industrial development.

The first lesson of this module already showed two related carve-outs. Information already in the **public domain** — openly available to anyone — is excluded from "technical data". So are general scientific and engineering principles commonly taught in schools and universities. Fundamental research is the university extension of that same idea. Research done openly at an accredited institution, meant for publication without a sponsor reviewing or approving it first, is generally not treated as controlled technical data at all.

That means a non-US-person can usually take part in it, be supervised in it, and co-author its results — on work that would be squarely controlled inside an ITAR-registered company.

### The exclusion can be lost, project by project

The exclusion is not automatic, and it has conditions. It depends on the university *not* accepting restrictions that undo it. There are two kinds:

1. **A publication restriction** — letting a sponsor (the organization paying for the research) review or restrict publication before it happens.
2. **A personnel restriction** — agreeing to exclude certain people, commonly foreign nationals, from taking part in the research.

Some government-sponsored or defense-related projects come with one or both. A university is fully entitled to accept them when it chooses. But a lab that accepts either one has lost the fundamental research exclusion for that project. Its work can then be every bit as export-controlled as the same work at a company.

So the exclusion is real, and it is **project-specific**. An open PhD program in orbital mechanics or entry guidance is a different legal situation from a sponsor-restricted lab in the same department. Only that university's **[[export-control office|compliance-office]]** — a standing office at nearly every US research university — can tell you which situation a particular position is in.

::: key
The fundamental research exclusion (rooted in NSDD-189) generally excludes openly published, unrestricted university research from ITAR's technical-data controls, letting non-US-persons participate fully in it. It is forfeited, project by project, whenever a university accepts sponsor restrictions on publication or on who may participate — so the same door can be open in one lab and closed in the next lab down the hall, verified case by case through that university's own compliance office, not assumed from the fact that it is a university.
:::

::: warning A PhD program is not automatically outside export control
Enrolling in a doctoral program does not, by itself, put your work outside ITAR's reach. What matters is whether your specific project has accepted publication or personnel restrictions from a sponsor. A classified or sponsor-restricted project inside a university can be exactly as controlled as a similar project at a company. The exclusion applies to open, publication-bound projects. Ask a prospective advisor and the university's export-control office directly, before assuming either answer.
:::

::: example Two labs in the same department
A university aerospace department hosts two research groups working on neighboring problems. We check each against the NSDD-189 definition, one condition at a time.

**Lab one** studies entry, descent and landing guidance algorithms. It is funded by federal research grants.

- Does the funding include a publication-review clause? No.
- Does it restrict who may join the group? No.
- Are results published? Yes — they go to open journals and conferences as a matter of course.
- Result: this is fundamental research, ordinarily published and shared broadly. The exclusion applies, and the work is not controlled technical data. A non-US-person graduate student is admitted, supervised and co-authors papers like any other student.

**Lab two**, one floor down, works on a closely related guidance problem under a sponsor's contract.

- Does the contract let the sponsor review every paper before submission? Yes — a publication restriction.
- Does it limit project access to US citizens? Yes — a personnel restriction.
- Result: both conditions that forfeit the exclusion are present. The work is not fundamental research, however similar its mathematics looks to lab one's. A non-US-person is shut out in practice — not by ITAR directly, but by restrictions the university accepted, which removed the exclusion's protection.

Sanity check: same department, same general subject, two different legal situations. The only way to tell them apart was to read each project's funding terms. The word "university" settled nothing.
:::

## The long-horizon immigration path

There is a path from outside the four statuses toward one of them. Real people walk it successfully. It is also slow, technical, and dependent on personal facts this module cannot know about you.

Think of it as a **[[staircase|immigration-staircase]]**, where you cannot skip steps. Its shape, in the broadest terms:

1. **A starting legal basis.** Common starting points are an employer-sponsored temporary work visa such as an **[[H-1B|h1b-lottery]]** — itself often subject to a yearly lottery — or a visa for extraordinary ability, or a family relationship. A separate starting point is a refugee or asylum claim, judged on its own legal merits and unconnected to any job.
2. **A separate petition.** From that basis, a further process — usually an employer-sponsored or family-based **immigrant petition**, a formal request to let someone stay permanently — can lead toward **lawful permanent residence**.
3. **The status itself.** Lawful permanent residence — the green card — is one of the four statuses this module opened with.

How long does it take? That depends on the visa category, the applicant's country of birth, current backlogs in that category, and the facts of the individual case. All of these change over time and vary widely between cases. Stating a specific number of years here would be a guess dressed up as a fact. That is exactly the invented precision this module has avoided throughout, and exactly the kind of question a study app cannot answer responsibly.

::: note This module explains the gate; it does not advise on your own immigration case
Nothing in this lesson, or this module, is legal or immigration advice, and nothing here predicts how long any path takes or how likely it is to succeed for a given person. U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) is the authoritative public source for current immigration categories, requirements and procedures. Where your own situation is at stake — which visa category fits your facts, whether a path is realistic for your country of origin, what a timeline might look like for your case — the right source is a qualified immigration attorney, not this course and not a general description written for every reader at once.
:::

::: example Two shapes the same long path can take
Two people, neither currently a US person, both hope to do GNC work one day at a US ITAR-registered employer.

**Person one** is an international graduate student.

- Step 1: completes a PhD in a fundamental-research lab.
- Step 2: works afterward on **[[Optional Practical Training|opt]]** (OPT), a period of work allowed on a student visa.
- Step 3: is sponsored by a US employer for an H-1B visa and enters the yearly lottery.
- Step 4: if selected, and later sponsored for an employment-based immigrant petition, the years that follow can eventually lead to lawful permanent residence.

Each step has its own requirements and its own uncertainty — including a lottery whose outcome nobody can promise.

**Person two** already qualifies for asylum in the United States on the merits of their own circumstances, entirely separate from any employer or job search.

- Step 1: applies for asylum.
- Step 2: if asylum is granted, that person is, from the moment of the grant, an asylee under 8 U.S.C. § 1158 (read "eight U-S-C section eleven fifty-eight") — one of the four statuses from the start of this module.

No employer sponsorship is needed at any point in that process.

Sanity check: these are two different paths, reaching the status by different routes on different bases. Neither is a template for every reader. What they share is that both are built from real, named legal steps, not from hope — and both are exactly the kind of case an immigration attorney, not this course, should assess for a real person.
:::


## What none of this changes

Here is the argument the first seven lessons, and this lesson's first three sections, have been building toward.

Guidance, navigation and control is a body of mathematics and engineering practice:

- orbital mechanics;
- rigid-body dynamics and attitude kinematics — how a spinning, tumbling body moves;
- classical and modern control theory;
- Kalman filtering and state estimation;
- guidance law design and trajectory optimization;
- flight software architecture;
- the verification and Monte Carlo methods that show a vehicle is ready to fly.

Every one of those subjects is the same subject whether the vehicle launches for a US company, a European consortium, a UK small-satellite firm, a Canadian robotics program, an Australian launch startup, a Japanese industrial giant, or a university lab publishing its results openly. A **[[Kalman filter's covariance update|kalman-bridge]]** does not know which country's flag is on the vehicle it is tracking. A powered-descent guidance law — the steering rule for a landing burn — converges the same way in Toulouse, in Adelaide and in Hawthorne.

Export control changes not a single equation in any technical module of this course. It governs *who may access* certain information about certain vehicles. It says nothing at all about *what* that information is or how it works.

::: key
Export-control status decides who may see controlled information about a specific vehicle. It does not change the mathematics: orbital mechanics, control, estimation and guidance are the same subject in every country and every lab, so the technical curriculum transfers completely whichever door you take.
:::

So the honest answer to "what should I do with the next **[[two thousand hours|two-thousand-hours]]**?" does not split as sharply as it might feel like it should, seven lessons into a module about a hard legal gate.

- **If you clear the gate,** the rest of this course is your direct path into the roles this module opened with. The only work left is the work itself.
- **If you do not clear it today,** the rest of this course is exactly as valuable. The target set of employers moves — from one country's launch industry to the wider set of companies, countries and research institutions named across this lesson and the last. But the guidance law you derive, the filter you tune and the simulation you build are every bit as real, as rigorous and as employable, wherever they end up being used.

The unsentimental version of the advice this module's exercises ask you to write down comes in three parts:

1. Know exactly which of the four statuses you hold, so you are not guessing about your own situation.
2. Know exactly what each alternative in these two lessons requires, so you are not chasing a door that was never open.
3. Then spend the two thousand hours on the mathematics and the engineering — because that part of the plan never depended on where you were born.

::: context jaxa How JAXA came to be
**JAXA** stands for Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency. It was formed in 2003 by merging three older organizations: one focused on space science, one on aeronautics research, and one on developing launch vehicles and satellites.

That history is why JAXA does such a wide mix of work — asteroid sample-return missions such as Hayabusa2, rocket development with industry, aircraft research and astronaut training — all under one name.
:::

::: context japanese-rockets Japan's workhorse rockets
The **H-IIA** first flew in 2001 and became Japan's main large launcher for more than two decades, before flying its fiftieth and final mission in 2025. Its successor, the **H3**, was designed to be cheaper to build and fly. The H3's first launch attempt in 2023 failed; its second, in 2024, reached orbit.

::: context japan-work-visa Japan's engineering visa
Japan's immigration system sorts foreign workers into named **statuses of residence**. The one most engineers use has a long official name: "Engineer / Specialist in Humanities / International Services". It covers work that draws on a university education or equivalent professional experience.

To get it, you normally need a job offer from a Japanese employer, and the employer usually helps with the paperwork. Notice what is missing from that list: Japanese nationality. That is what makes this a work-permission gate, not a status gate.
:::

::: context nsdd-189 A directive from 1985
A **National Security Decision Directive** was a formal instruction from the US President to the government's agencies. NSDD-189 was issued in 1985, during a period of worry that Cold War secrecy rules might creep into university labs and choke open science.

Its answer was a clear line. Open research whose results are meant to be published should stay open, and if something truly needs protecting, the proper tool is classification — marking it secret by the formal process — not restrictions on who may study or publish. The directive is short, and its definition of fundamental research is still the one universities quote today.
:::

::: context compliance-office Who to ask on campus
Nearly every US research university has an office — often called export control, research compliance, or research security — whose job is to review research contracts before the university signs them. They read the clauses about publication and about who may take part, and they decide whether a project keeps the fundamental research exclusion.

For a student, that makes them the most reliable source on a specific lab. A friendly email asking "does this project carry publication or participation restrictions?" is a normal question to them, and asking it before you accept a position is wise, not rude.

::: context h1b-lottery Why the H-1B has a lottery
The **H-1B** is a temporary US work visa for "specialty occupations" — jobs that need at least a bachelor's degree in a specific field, which includes most engineering. Congress caps new H-1B visas each year at 65,000, plus 20,000 more for people with an advanced degree from a US university.

Employers usually register far more candidates than there are places. When that happens, US immigration authorities choose among the registrations — historically by a random lottery, and the selection rules have been revised over time. So a strong engineer with a real job offer can still miss out in a given year, through no fault of their own — one reason this path cannot be planned to the month.
:::

::: context opt Working on a student visa
International students in the US usually hold an **F-1** student visa. **Optional Practical Training** (OPT) lets them work in their field of study, generally for up to 12 months after finishing a degree. Graduates in science, technology, engineering and mathematics (STEM) can apply for a 24-month extension, for up to three years in all.

OPT is permission to work for a while. It is not one of the four statuses, so an ITAR-controlled role stays closed during it.
:::

::: context kalman-bridge A preview of the Kalman filter
A **Kalman filter** combines a prediction ("where the vehicle should be, given how it was moving") with a measurement ("where the sensors say it is"), and weighs each by how much it can be trusted. The **covariance** is the filter's running record of how uncertain it is.

Every time a measurement arrives, the covariance update shrinks that uncertainty by a formula that depends only on the numbers — never on who wrote the code or where. You will build one yourself in the estimation modules later in this course, and it will work the same way for a rocket in any country.
:::

::: context two-thousand-hours Why "two thousand hours"
A full-time work year is roughly 2,000 hours: 40 hours a week for about 50 weeks. So "two thousand hours" stands for a serious, year-sized block of effort — the scale of commitment that self-study toward GNC work asks for.

The number is a sense of scale, not a promise. Its point is that the hours are large and precious, which is why this module spent a few of them first on finding out whether the door you are aiming at can open for you. A later career module comes back to what a couple of thousand hours of self-study does, and does not, build.
:::
