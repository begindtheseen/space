Imagine two signs at a swimming pool. One says "No diving — shallow water." The other says "Please shower before swimming." Both are printed in the same font on the same wall. But one is about physics: the water really is too shallow, and no lifeguard can make it deeper for you. The other is a house rule the pool chose, and a pool could decide to drop it.

Job postings are full of signs like these. This lesson is about telling them apart.

Read the SpaceX export-control paragraph once more, all the way to the end. The applicant must be a U.S. citizen or national, a lawful permanent resident, a refugee, or an asylee, "or be eligible to obtain the required authorizations from the U.S. Department of State." That last part is not filler. It names a real legal path alongside the four statuses. The first half of this lesson explains what that path is — and why it almost never changes the outcome for an ordinary engineering applicant.

The second half takes up a question that runs under the whole module. When you read a requirement in a posting, how do you know whether it is a law that cannot be argued with, or a company's own choice layered on top? The two look the same on the page. They mean very different things for you. Telling them apart is probably the most useful skill in this module.

## What the fifth clause refers to

The State Department's export office, DDTC, can give a specific foreign person permission to receive specific technical data, for a specific, defined piece of work. (A **foreign person** is anyone who is not a U.S. person — anyone without one of the four statuses.) That permission comes as an export **[[license|license]]** or agreement:

- a **[[Technical Assistance Agreement|taa]]** (**TAA**) for broader arrangements between companies;
- or, when a company wants to employ one named foreign person, an individual license — commonly on the form called a **[[DSP-5|dsp-forms]]**, DDTC's standard license for unclassified technical data.

The application names the person, the technical data or service involved, and what it will be used for. DDTC reviews it and decides. None of this is automatic. None of it is quick the way renewing a visa can be quick.

Three facts about this process matter more than the fact that it exists.

1. **The employer applies, not the candidate.** A job seeker cannot go to DDTC on their own and become "eligible," the way one applies for a visa. The company has to decide to pursue it, prepare the application, and see it through.
2. **The outcome is discretionary.** **Discretionary** means the decision is up to the agency's judgment — no rule forces a yes. DDTC can approve, deny, or approve with conditions. A company starting the process has no guarantee of the result or the timing.
3. **It is specific to one person, one scope of work, and often one program.** It does not create a general eligibility that a person carries from job to job, the way a green card does.

### The math from the employer's chair

Put those three facts together and the business logic becomes clear. For each open job — a **[[requisition|requisition]]**, in hiring language — the company compares two paths:

- **Path 1:** apply for a discretionary government approval for one named person, paying the cost, the delay, and the risk of a no.
- **Path 2:** keep searching for a qualified U.S. person. For most GNC specialties there are enough of them in the job market that the search is not hopeless.

For an ordinary mid-level GNC opening, Path 2 almost always wins.

The comparison changes only when the candidate cannot be replaced by the people available. That can happen in a narrow specialty where very few people anywhere have the experience. It can happen at a company that already keeps licensing staff and paperwork running for other reasons, such as a joint venture with a foreign partner. It can happen on a program where the company has already decided the investment is worth it. Those situations exist. They are the exception, not the doorway a typical applicant should plan around.

So the fifth clause is accurate, not decorative. The path it names is real, and pretending otherwise would be its own inaccuracy. But accurate and realistic are different things. If none of the four statuses applies to you, the honest summary is this: the clause is a door that exists on paper, opened case by case at the employer's initiative and expense. It is not a plan you can carry out by applying persuasively enough.

::: key
The residual clause refers to an individual, employer-sponsored DDTC authorization (commonly a TAA or a DSP-5-type license) naming a specific foreign person for a specific, defined scope of controlled work. The employer applies; DDTC decides; approval is discretionary and scoped narrowly. It is a real mechanism, rarely exercised for an ordinary engineering requisition because of its cost and uncertainty relative to hiring an already-eligible candidate.
:::

## Three different things wearing the same sentence

Read enough postings and hiring conversations in this industry and you will meet requirements that sound alike but come from three different places. Picture them as [[three layers|three-layers]] stacked on each other.

### Layer 1: what the regulation requires

This layer is narrow and mechanical. Controlled technical data may not be released to a foreign person without proper authorization. At the level that matters to a hiring decision, that is the whole rule.

It does not, by itself, say an employer must hire only citizens. It does not say a role needs a security clearance. It does not set a visa policy. Any of the four statuses from the previous lesson satisfies it completely and equally. In the regulation's eyes a green card holder is not a second-choice hire next to a citizen: both may see the same controlled technical data.

This layer cannot be waived. It is federal law, and no employer has the power to set it aside, even for a candidate it badly wants. A referral from a friend inside the company cannot get around it. No portfolio, degree, test score, or willingness to relocate substitutes for it, because it does not measure skill at all — it measures status.

::: key
Why is this gate non-negotiable? It is a US government export-control requirement, not a company preference. The employer cannot waive it, a referral cannot bypass it, and no level of demonstrated skill substitutes for it.
:::

### Layer 2: what an employer adds on top

This layer is set by the company, not by law. Every posting is full of it. The **[[basic qualifications|posting-layout]]** — a degree in a certain field, a number of years of experience, a programming language — are the employer's choice of minimum. The **preferred qualifications** are its wish list. Rules about working on site, about which teams hire at which level, or about how many interview rounds there are, are the employer's choices too.

These choices vary between companies, between teams at the same company, and over time, in ways ITAR itself never does. And because the company chose them, they can bend. A hiring manager may decide that a candidate with a strong portfolio and no master's degree is worth an interview. A candidate may make a case that experience in another industry is equivalent. An exceptional applicant may be waved past a line that stops others.

Sometimes a posting narrows the citizenship line itself — "U.S. citizens only" instead of the four statuses. Be careful with that one. It usually traces to something beyond ITAR that genuinely requires citizenship: most often a security clearance, or the terms of a government contract. Employers do not have a free hand here. The same anti-discrimination law that ITAR borrowed its "[[protected individual|protected-individual-limits]]" list from generally bars employers from preferring citizens over permanent residents, refugees, and asylees, unless a law, regulation, executive order, or government contract requires it. So a citizens-only line is best read as "something else on this role needs citizenship," not as a restatement of ITAR.

### Layer 3: what a license could permit

This is the fifth-clause mechanism described above: real, narrow, started by the employer, and rarely the working path for a standard hire.

### Why keeping them apart pays off

The reason to separate the three layers is practical.

- Layer 1 cannot be negotiated, appealed to a hiring manager, or outweighed by a great interview.
- Layer 2 is worth reading closely rather than assuming. A posting written in the regulation's own terms — the four statuses — is stating the legal floor. A posting that asks for more is telling you about that employer or that role. Most of Layer 2 is where effort, evidence, and a strong application actually change outcomes.
- Layer 3 is worth knowing about, so you understand the posting's full sentence instead of mentally deleting its last clause. It is not something to build a plan on.

::: key
What ITAR requires: technical data must not be released to a foreign person without authorization; any of the four U.S.-person statuses satisfies this fully and equally. What an employer additionally requires: a business choice, set by the company, that can be narrower than the regulation and varies by employer. What a license could permit: real, employer-initiated, rarely pursued for an ordinary hire.
:::

::: key
ITAR versus employer preference: an employer preference can be argued with, outweighed by evidence or waived for an exceptional candidate. An export-control restriction cannot. Knowing which is which decides where to spend effort.
:::

::: warning One posting's wording is not the industry's floor
A posting phrased more narrowly than the standard four-status language — for instance, one that names only citizenship — tells you something about that employer or that role, most often that it also carries a clearance requirement. It does not tell you the law has changed. It does not tell you what a different employer, or a different role at the same employer, requires. Read each posting's actual words rather than assuming they match the last one you read.
:::

::: example Two postings, two different sources for a similar-looking line
Two hypothetical postings from the same launch company:

- **Posting 1**, a GNC engineer role on satellite attitude control, prints the standard four-status language word for word: citizen or national, lawful permanent resident, refugee, or asylee.
- **Posting 2**, a role supporting a national-security payload program, says the candidate must be a U.S. citizen and must be able to obtain and maintain a Top Secret clearance.

Step 1: which layer is Posting 1 stating? The four statuses match 22 CFR § 120.62 exactly, with nothing added. So Posting 1 is stating the ITAR floor, Layer 1, directly.

Step 2: which layer is Posting 2's citizenship line? It is not a stricter reading of ITAR. It follows from the clearance requirement sitting on top, because clearance is, in practice, generally limited to citizens (Lesson 6 covers this in full).

Step 3: apply it to a green card holder. Against Posting 1: eligible. Against Posting 2: screened out — but by the clearance's own citizenship expectation, not by ITAR's U.S.-person definition, which the green card holder meets without difficulty.

Sanity check: the same person gets two different answers from the same company, and the difference is fully explained by a requirement that appears in one posting and not the other. Seeing which requirement is doing the work tells you where the real boundary sits.
:::

::: example Weighing the fifth clause from an employer's chair
A hiring manager has one open requisition for a specialist in **entry guidance** — steering a vehicle as it falls back through the atmosphere. Two finalists remain:

- **Finalist 1** is a U.S. citizen with solid, but not exceptional, experience in the specific area.
- **Finalist 2** is a Canadian citizen, and not a U.S. person under ITAR, with rare, directly relevant **[[flight heritage|flight-heritage]]** that Finalist 1 lacks.

Step 1: list what hiring Finalist 2 would take. The company applies to DDTC for an individual authorization naming that person, for a defined scope of work. There is no guarantee of approval and no fixed timeline. The role sits open while it waits.

Step 2: list what hiring Finalist 1 would take. An offer. The requisition closes at once with a fully authorized employee.

Step 3: weigh them. The manager pursues the license only if Finalist 2's specific expertise is worth the cost, the wait, and the risk of a no. That is a judgment made role by role. It is not a standing offer to every strong applicant who is not a U.S. person.

Sanity check: would the answer change for an ordinary opening with several qualified U.S. persons in the pool? Yes — then the calculation almost never favors the license. It tips only when the expertise truly cannot be found elsewhere, which is uncommon and not something a candidate can assume about their own case.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
A candidate reads the fifth clause of a posting — "or be eligible to obtain the required authorizations from the U.S. Department of State" — and concludes that applying anyway is worthwhile because the company itself might sponsor an authorization. Evaluate this reasoning.
:::

::: answer
The reasoning treats a rare, employer-started, discretionary process as if it were a normal step in hiring. The clause is legally accurate — such authorizations exist. But the employer, not the candidate, decides whether to seek one, and does so only when a specific candidate's value plainly outweighs the cost and uncertainty of a discretionary DDTC approval. For an ordinary requisition with other qualified U.S. persons available, that essentially never happens. Applying does no harm, but it should not be the basis of a plan, and the candidate should not expect the clause to work as a general fifth route to eligibility.
:::

::: check
Distinguish, in one sentence each, what ITAR itself requires, what an employer might additionally require, and what a license could in principle permit.
:::

::: answer
ITAR requires that controlled technical data not be released to a foreign person without authorization, and any of the four U.S.-person statuses satisfies that fully. An employer might add its own requirements — degree, years of experience, on-site work, or a narrower citizenship line tied to something like a clearance or a government contract — as choices or obligations of its own, not as restatements of ITAR. A license or agreement (commonly a TAA or an individual DSP-5-type authorization) could in principle let a specific foreign person see specific technical data, but only if the employer applies and DDTC approves.
:::

::: check
Why can an employer's own hiring preference sometimes be discussed or questioned, while the ITAR requirement itself cannot?
:::

::: answer
An employer's preference is a business decision the company chose, so the company can revisit it, explain it, or apply it differently case by case — though a candidate outside the company usually has limited power to change it. The ITAR requirement is federal law. No employer has the authority to waive, narrow, or reinterpret it for one candidate, however strong the case. So there is nothing to discuss with a hiring manager about it.
:::

::: check
A posting states only "U.S. citizens" with no mention of lawful permanent residents, refugees, or asylees, for a role with no stated clearance requirement. What are two possible explanations, and why does the difference matter to a lawful permanent resident deciding whether to apply?
:::

::: answer
First possibility: something the posting does not mention — a government contract term, or an unannounced clearance or program requirement — really does require citizenship for this role. Second possibility: the posting is loosely worded, and the employer would accept any of the four statuses if asked. (A company preferring citizens with no such requirement behind it is limited by anti-discrimination law, so that is the least likely reading.) The difference matters because in the first case a permanent resident is genuinely not eligible for this role, however ITAR reads in general, while in the second case they may well be eligible despite the wording. An outsider usually cannot tell which is true. The sensible move is to apply if otherwise well matched and let the recruiter screen settle the question, rather than assume either answer.
:::

::: check
Why does keeping "what the law requires" and "what the employer additionally chose" separate matter more to a job search than knowing the ITAR rule alone?
:::

::: answer
Because ITAR sets a floor, not a ceiling, on what a role can require. Knowing the floor tells you what is true everywhere and cannot be changed by any one company. But a specific posting can ask for more, for reasons tied to that company or role, and the only way to know what an opportunity requires is to read what that employer wrote. Mixing the two up leads to one of two mistakes: ruling yourself out of a role that only states the ITAR floor, or assuming you qualify for a role that adds a requirement the law alone does not impose. Separating them also tells you where effort pays: argue with Layer 2, never with Layer 1.
:::

## Summary

| Layer | Source | Can it be argued with? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The ITAR requirement itself | 22 CFR § 120.62 and related provisions; federal law | No — applies identically to every employer; no referral, portfolio or skill substitutes |
| An employer's added requirement | Company policy, set per role or organization-wide; a citizens-only line usually traces to a clearance or contract | Preferences, yes — they can be argued with, outweighed by evidence, or waived; not from outside the company for legal obligations |
| Authorization for a specific foreign person | DDTC license or agreement (e.g. a TAA or individual DSP-5-type authorization) | Employer-initiated only; discretionary; not a candidate-driven path |

The next lesson explains why none of these layers is solved by being physically inside the United States: the "deemed export" rule, under which releasing controlled technical data to a foreign person in a US office counts the same as sending it abroad.

::: context license A permission slip from the government
An export license is permission, in writing, to do something the law otherwise forbids. It is like a school permission slip: specific about who, what, and when. A license to share technical data names the person or company receiving it, the exact data covered, what it may be used for, and often how long the permission lasts. Step outside those limits and you are back to breaking the rule.
:::

::: context taa Agreements for teaching, not only shipping
ITAR controls **defense services** — helping a foreign person design, build, or operate a defense item — as well as data and hardware. A Technical Assistance Agreement is the contract DDTC approves when a US company will provide such services or share technical data with foreign parties. They are common for international programs, such as a US company working with a foreign satellite maker. They are heavy paperwork, which is part of why companies do not use them lightly.
:::

::: context dsp-forms Form numbers you may hear
DDTC's license applications are known by form number. The DSP-5 is the standard license for permanently exporting unclassified defense items or technical data, and it is the usual vehicle for authorizing a company to employ a named foreign person on controlled work. There are others, such as the DSP-85 for classified items and data. You will not fill any of these in yourself — the employer's export-control staff do — but hearing the numbers in a conversation will no longer be a mystery.
:::

::: context requisition Hiring jargon: the req
Inside a company, an open job is a **requisition**, usually shortened to "req." Opening one means a manager has approval and budget to hire one person for one role. A recruiter may be working a dozen reqs at once. Every week a req sits open is a week the team is short one person — which is why an uncertain, slow license rarely beats an immediate, fully eligible hire.
:::

::: context protected-individual-limits The same list protects job seekers
Lesson 2 showed that ITAR borrowed its "protected individual" list from 8 U.S.C. § 1324b, a law against hiring discrimination. That law's main job is to stop employers from treating citizens, permanent residents, refugees, and asylees differently because of citizenship status — with an exception when a law, regulation, executive order, or government contract requires it. So the very statute that defines who counts under ITAR also limits how far an employer can go past ITAR on citizenship. Other preferences, like degrees or experience, face no such limit.
:::

::: context flight-heritage What "flight heritage" means
A part, algorithm, or engineer with **flight heritage** has already flown on a real mission and worked. It is the space industry's strongest form of proof, because testing on the ground cannot fully copy launch, vacuum, and radiation. Heritage is scarce and valuable — which is exactly the kind of rare expertise that can, now and then, tip an employer toward the slow license path.
:::
