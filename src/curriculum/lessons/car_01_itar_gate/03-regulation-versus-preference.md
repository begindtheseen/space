Read the SpaceX export-control paragraph again, in full this time: applicant must be a U.S. citizen or national, a lawful permanent resident, a refugee, or an asylee, "or be eligible to obtain the required authorizations from the U.S. Department of State." That last clause is not throat-clearing. It names a real legal path that exists alongside the four statuses, and understanding what it actually is — and, equally important, why it almost never changes the outcome for an ordinary engineering applicant — is the subject of the first half of this lesson.

The second half takes up a question that runs underneath the entire module: when you read a requirement in a posting, how do you know whether you are looking at a law that cannot be argued with, or a company's own policy choice layered on top of it? The two look identical on the page. They are not identical in what they mean for you, and telling them apart is, among everything in this module, probably the single most useful skill to walk away with.

## What the fifth clause actually refers to

DDTC can authorize a specific foreign person to receive specific technical data, for a specific, defined scope of work. This happens through an export license or agreement — commonly a Technical Assistance Agreement (TAA) for broader arrangements, or, for the narrower case of a company wanting to employ one named foreign person, a long-standing DDTC licensing practice that authorizes that employment on an individual license (commonly a DSP-5, sometimes referred to alongside DSP-85 for related purposes). The application identifies the person by name, the technical data or defense service involved, and the end use. DDTC reviews it and decides whether to approve it. None of this is automatic, and none of it is quick in the way a visa renewal is quick.

Three things about this process matter more than its existence does. First, the **employer is the applicant**, not the candidate — a job seeker cannot independently petition DDTC to become "eligible" the way one applies for a visa; the company has to decide to pursue it, prepare the application, and see it through. Second, the outcome is **discretionary** — DDTC evaluates the specific request and can approve, deny, or condition it, so an employer starting this process has no guarantee of where it ends or when. Third, and most decisive in practice, it is **specific to one person, one scope, and often one program** — it does not create a general eligibility a person carries from job to job the way a green card does.

Put those three together and the economics become clear. For a company weighing whether to sponsor this kind of authorization for a given requisition, the calculation is a comparison: the cost, delay, and uncertainty of a discretionary government approval for one named individual, against the cost of continuing to search for a qualified U.S. person, of whom — for most GNC specialties — there are enough in the labor market that the search is not hopeless. For an ordinary mid-level GNC opening, that comparison almost always favors continuing the search. The calculation changes when the candidate is not interchangeable with the available alternatives: a narrow specialty where very few people anywhere have the relevant experience, a company that already maintains this kind of licensing infrastructure for other reasons (an existing joint venture, an established relationship with a specific foreign partner), or a role tied to a program where the company has already decided the investment is worthwhile. Those situations exist. They are the exception, not the on-ramp a typical applicant should plan around.

This is why the posting's fifth clause is accurate rather than decorative: the path it names is real, and denying that would be its own kind of inaccuracy. But accurate and realistic are different qualities. The honest statement of your options, if none of the four named statuses applies to you, is that this clause is a door that exists on paper and is opened, case by case, at the employer's initiative and expense — not a plan a candidate can execute by applying persuasively enough.

::: key
The residual clause refers to an individual, employer-sponsored DDTC authorization (commonly a TAA or a DSP-5-type license) naming a specific foreign person for a specific, defined scope of controlled work. The employer applies; DDTC decides; approval is discretionary and scoped narrowly. It is a real mechanism, rarely exercised for an ordinary engineering requisition because of its cost and uncertainty relative to hiring an already-eligible candidate.
:::

## Three different things wearing the same sentence

Read broadly across postings and conversations in this industry, and you will encounter requirements that sound alike but come from three genuinely different places.

**What the regulation actually requires** is narrow and mechanical: technical data may not be released to a foreign person without proper authorization. That is the whole rule, at the level that matters to a hiring decision. It does not, by itself, say an employer must hire only citizens. It does not say a role must carry a security clearance. It does not dictate a visa policy. Any of the four statuses from the previous lesson satisfies it completely and equally — a green card holder is not a second-choice hire relative to a citizen from the regulation's point of view; both are fully authorized to access the same controlled technical data.

**What an employer chooses to require beyond that** is a separate layer, set by the company, not by law. A company might restrict a given role to citizens only, even where a green card holder or asylee would satisfy ITAR without issue, for reasons of its own — administrative simplicity in a mixed compliance environment, a role that also touches classified work and therefore needs citizenship for clearance purposes regardless of ITAR, a blanket policy set for the whole organization rather than tailored per role, or a preference nobody has revisited. Whatever the reason, this layer is a business decision, not a legal floor, and it can and does vary between companies, between teams at the same company, and over time, in ways ITAR itself never does.

**What a license or exemption could in principle permit** is the fifth-clause mechanism described above — real, narrow, employer-initiated, and rarely the operative path for a standard hire.

The reason to keep these three apart is entirely practical. The first cannot be negotiated, appealed to a hiring manager, or outweighed by a strong interview — it is federal law, and no employer has the authority to waive it even if they wanted to. The second is, at least in principle, a matter of company policy, which means it is worth reading carefully rather than assuming: a posting phrased in the regulation's own terms (the four statuses) is stating the actual legal floor, while a posting that says something narrower, such as "U.S. citizens only," may be describing that specific employer's own overlay rather than what ITAR itself demands — and that overlay is not something a candidate outside the company can usually verify or contest, but it is worth recognizing as a choice rather than as a restatement of the law. The third is worth knowing exists, so that you understand a posting's full sentence rather than mentally deleting the last clause as filler, but it is not something to build a plan around.

::: key
What ITAR requires: technical data must not be released to a foreign person without authorization; any of the four U.S.-person statuses satisfies this fully and equally. What an employer additionally requires: a business choice, set by the company, that can be narrower than the regulation and varies by employer. What a license could permit: real, employer-initiated, rarely pursued for an ordinary hire.
:::

::: warning One posting's wording is not the industry's floor
Seeing a posting phrased more narrowly than the standard four-status language — for instance, one that names only citizenship — tells you something about that specific employer or that specific role, most often because it also carries a clearance requirement. It does not tell you that the underlying law has changed, and it does not tell you what a different employer, or a different role at the same employer, requires. Read each posting's actual wording rather than assuming it matches the last one you read.
:::

::: example Two postings, two different sources for a similar-looking line
A GNC Engineer posting for a Starlink attitude-control role states the standard four-status language verbatim, matching 22 CFR § 120.62 exactly: citizen or national, lawful permanent resident, refugee, or asylee. A separate posting, for a role explicitly supporting a national-security payload program, states that the candidate must be a U.S. citizen and must be able to obtain and maintain a Top Secret clearance.

The first posting is stating the ITAR floor directly — any of the four statuses satisfies it, with nothing added. The second posting's citizenship-only line is not a stricter reading of ITAR; it is a consequence of the clearance requirement layered on top, since clearance eligibility is, in practice, generally limited to citizens regardless of ITAR's broader four-status test (the next module lesson covers this relationship in full). A green card holder who cannot work the second role is being screened out by the clearance requirement's own citizenship expectation, not by ITAR's U.S.-person definition, which they would satisfy without difficulty. Recognizing which requirement is doing the work in each case tells you where the actual boundary sits.
:::

::: example Weighing the fifth clause from an employer's chair
A hiring manager has an outstanding requisition for an entry guidance specialist. Two finalists remain: one is a U.S. citizen with solid but not exceptional experience in the specific sub-field; the other is a landed immigrant in Canada — not a U.S. person under ITAR — with genuinely rare, directly relevant flight heritage that the first candidate does not have.

Pursuing the second candidate would mean the company applying to DDTC for an individual authorization naming that specific person, for a specific scope of work, with no guarantee of approval or timeline, and with the role sitting open in the meantime. Hiring the first candidate closes the requisition immediately with a fully authorized employee. Whether the manager pursues the license depends entirely on how much the second candidate's specific expertise is worth relative to that cost and uncertainty — a judgment made role by role, not a standing offer available to any strong non-U.S.-person applicant. For an ordinary opening where several qualified U.S. persons exist, the calculation almost never favors the license. It shifts only when the expertise genuinely cannot be found elsewhere, which is uncommon and not something a candidate can assume applies to their own case.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
A candidate reads the fifth clause of a posting — "or be eligible to obtain the required authorizations from the U.S. Department of State" — and concludes that applying anyway is worthwhile because the company itself might sponsor an authorization. Evaluate this reasoning.
:::

::: answer
The reasoning treats a rare, employer-initiated, discretionary process as though it were a standard part of the hiring pipeline. The clause is legally accurate — such authorizations exist — but the employer, not the candidate, decides whether to pursue one, and does so only when a specific candidate's value clearly outweighs the cost and uncertainty of a discretionary DDTC approval. For an ordinary requisition with other qualified U.S.-person candidates available, this essentially never happens. Applying is not harmful, but it should not be the basis of a plan, and the candidate should not expect the clause to function as a general fifth eligibility path.
:::

::: check
Distinguish, in one sentence each, what ITAR itself requires, what an employer might additionally require, and what a license could in principle permit.
:::

::: answer
ITAR requires that controlled technical data not be released to a foreign person without authorization, a condition any of the four U.S.-person statuses satisfies fully. An employer might additionally require something narrower — such as citizenship only, or a specific visa policy — as its own business choice, not a restatement of the law. A license or agreement (commonly a TAA or an individual DSP-5-type authorization) could in principle let a specific foreign person access specific technical data, but only if the employer applies for it and DDTC approves it.
:::

::: check
Why can an employer's own hiring preference sometimes be discussed or questioned, while the ITAR requirement itself cannot?
:::

::: answer
An employer's preference is a business decision the company itself chose to adopt, so it is, in principle, something the company could revisit, explain, or apply differently in different cases — though a candidate outside the company usually has limited ability to actually change it. The ITAR requirement is federal law; no employer has the authority to waive, narrow, or reinterpret it for a specific candidate, no matter how compelling the case, so there is nothing to discuss with a hiring manager about it.
:::

::: check
A posting states only "U.S. citizens" with no mention of lawful permanent residents, refugees, or asylees, for a role with no stated clearance requirement. What are two possible explanations, and why does the difference matter to a lawful permanent resident deciding whether to apply?
:::

::: answer
Two possibilities: the employer has adopted citizenship-only as its own policy choice beyond what ITAR requires, for reasons unrelated to what is stated in the posting, or the posting is imprecisely worded and the employer would, if asked, accept any of the four statuses. The difference matters because in the first case a lawful permanent resident is genuinely not eligible for that specific employer's role regardless of how the law reads generally, while in the second case they may be eligible despite the posting's imprecise wording. Since the reader usually cannot determine which is true from outside the company, the reasonable approach is to apply if otherwise well matched and let the recruiter screen resolve the ambiguity, rather than to assume either explanation.
:::

::: check
Why does keeping "what the law requires" and "what the employer additionally chose" separate matter more to a job search than knowing the ITAR rule alone?
:::

::: answer
Because ITAR sets only a floor, not a ceiling, on what an employer can require. Knowing the floor tells you what is true everywhere and cannot be changed by any single company — useful for understanding the field as a whole. But an individual company's actual posted requirement can be narrower, for reasons specific to that company or role, and the only way to know what a given opportunity actually requires is to read what that employer wrote, not to assume every posting restates the regulation. Conflating the two would lead you to either wrongly rule yourself out of a role that only restates the ITAR floor, or wrongly assume you qualify for a role where the specific employer has added a requirement the law itself does not impose.
:::

## Summary

| Layer | Source | Can it be argued with? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The ITAR requirement itself | 22 CFR § 120.62 and related provisions; federal law | No — applies identically to every employer |
| An employer's added requirement | Company policy, set per role or organization-wide | In principle yes, in practice rarely, and not from outside the company |
| Authorization for a specific foreign person | DDTC license or agreement (e.g. a TAA or individual DSP-5-type authorization) | Employer-initiated only; discretionary; not a candidate-driven path |
