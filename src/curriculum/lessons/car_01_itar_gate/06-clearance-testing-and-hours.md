Some postings for GNC roles carry more than the export-control paragraph. A role touching national-security payloads may add a line about obtaining and maintaining a Top Secret clearance. Nearly every posting at a hardware company states pre-employment and random drug and alcohol testing as a condition of employment. Many carry a line about being willing to work extended hours and weekends when needed to meet critical deadlines. None of these three is the ITAR requirement itself, and sorting out which bucket each one falls into — a separate government process, or an employer's own policy choice — is exactly the skill the third lesson of this module built, now applied to three concrete, recurring examples.

Getting this sorting right matters for a specific reason: it tells you what is actually being asked of you, and by whom. A requirement imposed by the federal government cannot be negotiated with a hiring manager. A requirement the employer chose is, at least in principle, a fact about that employer rather than a fact about the law — worth knowing plainly, but not worth mistaking for something else.

## Top Secret clearance: a separate government process layered on top

A security clearance is not an ITAR concept at all. It is authorization to access classified national-security information, governed by an entirely different body of executive-branch policy, adjudicated through a background investigation conducted on behalf of the government, not the employer. The standard entry point is Standard Form 86, the Questionnaire for National Security Positions — an extensive disclosure of personal history, foreign contacts, finances, and background — followed by a government investigation and an adjudication decision. None of this is run by the hiring company; the company sponsors the process, but the government decides the outcome, on its own timeline, which depends on the specifics of each case rather than on anything an employer or a study app can predict.

The eligibility standard for a clearance is narrower than the four U.S.-person statuses this module already covered. As a general rule, clearance eligibility is limited to U.S. citizens; lawful permanent residents, refugees, and asylees — every one of whom is fully and completely a U.S. person under 22 CFR § 120.62, with no qualification — are, in the ordinary case, not eligible for a personnel security clearance at all. This is precisely the distinction the accuracy of this entire module rests on: ITAR eligibility and clearance eligibility are related only in that clearance sits on top of ITAR eligibility for a subset of roles, not because they are the same test at different strengths. A green card holder can be completely, unambiguously eligible under ITAR and still not eligible for the specific subset of roles that also require a clearance — not because of any deficiency in their status, but because clearance asks a different, stricter question that their status does not answer.

Roles that add this requirement do so because the work itself touches classified material — commonly national-security payload programs, described in industry postings under names like Starshield rather than the commercial Starlink or Falcon lines. Holding a clearance does not substitute for ITAR eligibility, and meeting ITAR eligibility does not get you any distance toward clearance eligibility if citizenship is what stands between you and it. They are two separate gates, and a clearance-required role asks you to clear both.

::: key
A security clearance is a separate government process (SF-86, a background investigation, and adjudication), not an ITAR requirement, generally limited to U.S. citizens specifically — a narrower group than the full set of U.S. persons. Some GNC roles, often on national-security-adjacent programs, require it in addition to ordinary export-control eligibility, never instead of it.
:::

::: warning Meeting the four ITAR statuses is not enough for a clearance-required role
A lawful permanent resident, a refugee, or an asylee can be completely eligible under 22 CFR § 120.62 and still not qualify for a role that additionally requires a Top Secret clearance, because clearance eligibility is, as a general matter, limited to citizens. Read a posting's full requirement rather than stopping once the ITAR language looks satisfied — a clearance line changes what is actually being asked.
:::

## Drug and alcohol testing: the employer's own condition of employment

Pre-employment and random drug and alcohol testing is not an ITAR requirement, and nothing in the Arms Export Control Act or 22 CFR Parts 120 through 130 addresses it. It is the kind of requirement lesson three called an employer's own choice — common across safety-sensitive manufacturing and aerospace environments generally, stated plainly in the posting as a condition of employment, and applied independent of the law of the state where the facility happens to sit. A candidate working in a state where recreational use of a given substance is legal under state law is still subject to the employer's own testing policy as a condition of that specific job, because state legalization changes state law, not a private employer's own workplace rules.

This is a clean, concrete instance of the distinction lesson three built in the abstract: nothing about drug testing is handed down by DDTC or by the Arms Export Control Act. It is a policy choice the employer states directly, and — unlike the ITAR requirement — it is, in principle, a fact about that specific employer's practices rather than a fact true of the entire industry by law. In practice it is close to universal at hardware-manufacturing aerospace companies for safety reasons independent of ITAR, but its source is the employer's own stated policy, not federal export-control law.

## Extended hours and weekends: read it as description, not decoration

A posting's mission statement, its equal-opportunity language, and its list of preferred qualifications are the parts of a job posting closest to boilerplate — present in some form across nearly every posting in nearly every industry, written by legal and recruiting teams for reasons only loosely connected to the specific role. A line stating that the candidate must be willing to work extended hours and weekends when needed to meet critical deadlines is a different kind of sentence. It appears deliberately, is specific to how certain hardware companies describe their actual pace of work, and is consistent with publicly reported accounts of how programs at fast-moving launch companies actually run — Eric Berger's *Liftoff*, listed among this module's resources, is a detailed account of exactly this working style in SpaceX's early years, offered there explicitly as context rather than as a compliance document.

Treat this line as a real description of a real working condition, not as a formality to skim past. It belongs in your own decision about whether to accept a given offer, weighed the same way you would weigh salary, location, or team — and if it comes up in an interview, you should be able to answer honestly about how you feel about it rather than reciting it back performatively. Like drug testing, this is an employer's own stated expectation, not a legal requirement; unlike drug testing, whether it is a dealbreaker for you is entirely a personal judgment this module cannot make on your behalf.

::: key
Drug and alcohol testing and the extended-hours expectation are both employer policy, not ITAR requirements — stated directly in postings as real conditions of the job, worth taking at face value when deciding whether a specific offer is right for you, but not enforceable by, or connected to, export-control law.
:::

::: example Two roles, one ITAR test, two different additional requirements
A GNC Engineer, ADCS role on a commercial communications-satellite program states the standard four-status export-control language and nothing more specific about clearance. A lawful permanent resident who clears that language is fully eligible for the role as posted.

A GNC-adjacent role supporting a national-security payload program states the same export-control language, and adds: must be able to obtain and maintain a Top Secret clearance. The same lawful permanent resident who was fully eligible for the first role is not eligible for the second — not because their ITAR status changed, but because the second role adds a citizenship-generally-required clearance gate on top of it. Both postings are consistent with everything this module has taught; the second asks one further, separate question the first does not.
:::

::: example A testing policy that does not bend to state law
An aerospace manufacturer operates a factory in a state where recreational use of a specific substance is legal under state law. Its posting nonetheless states pre-employment and random drug and alcohol testing as a condition of employment, with no exception noted for substances legal under state law.

This is consistent, not contradictory: state legalization changes what state law enforcement can prosecute; it does not change what a private employer may require as a condition of a job offer it is free to extend or withhold. The company's policy is its own, stated directly in the posting, and — as with the export-control paragraph, though for entirely different underlying reasons — it is not something a candidate can negotiate around during a hiring process, however reasonable an argument from state law might sound.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
A lawful permanent resident is confident about applying to a clearance-required GNC role, reasoning that they are fully a U.S. person under ITAR. What is missing from this reasoning?
:::

::: answer
Being a full U.S. person under 22 CFR § 120.62 satisfies the ITAR requirement, but clearance eligibility is a separate, stricter test that, as a general rule, is limited to U.S. citizens specifically. A lawful permanent resident meets the first gate completely without necessarily meeting the second, since the two are governed by entirely different processes that happen to both apply to this particular role.
:::

::: check
Name the one government process among the three covered in this lesson, and explain why the other two are not government requirements at all.
:::

::: answer
Security clearance is the government process — conducted through SF-86, a background investigation, and adjudication by the government, independent of any specific employer. Drug and alcohol testing and the extended-hours expectation are both the employer's own stated policy: nothing in ITAR, the Arms Export Control Act, or any other federal export-control provision addresses either one. They appear in postings as conditions that specific companies choose to state, not as requirements imposed by export-control law.
:::

::: check
Why does a state legalizing a substance not change a private aerospace employer's drug-testing policy for that state's facilities?
:::

::: answer
State legalization changes what conduct state law enforcement may prosecute; it does not obligate a private employer to align its own workplace policies with it. Drug and alcohol testing here is the employer's own condition of employment, stated directly in the posting, and a private employer remains free to set stricter conditions than state law requires as a term of the job it is offering.
:::

::: check
A candidate treats the "extended hours and weekends" line in a posting as generic corporate language, similar to an equal-opportunity statement, and does not think further about it before accepting an offer. What is the risk in that assumption?
:::

::: answer
Unlike genuinely generic boilerplate, this line is a specific, deliberate description of an actual expected working pattern at companies that state it, consistent with publicly reported accounts of how such programs actually run. Treating it as decoration risks accepting an offer without having genuinely weighed a real condition of the job — one that belongs in the same decision as salary or location — against personal priorities, rather than encountering it as a surprise after starting.
:::

::: check
A candidate meets all four ITAR statuses and is applying to a role with no stated clearance requirement. Does the drug-testing line in the posting still apply to them, and on what basis?
:::

::: answer
Yes. Drug and alcohol testing is the employer's own stated condition of employment, independent of ITAR eligibility entirely — it applies to any candidate the company hires for that role, regardless of which of the four statuses they hold or whether the role carries any clearance requirement at all. ITAR eligibility, clearance, and drug testing are three separate conditions, and satisfying one says nothing about whether the others apply.
:::

## Summary

| Requirement | Source | Applies to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ITAR / U.S.-person status | Federal law (22 CFR § 120.62) | Every export-controlled role |
| Top Secret clearance | Separate federal process (SF-86, background investigation) | Specific roles, generally citizens only |
| Drug and alcohol testing | Employer policy | Stated per posting, independent of state law |
| Extended hours / weekends | Employer's stated expectation | Stated per posting; a real working condition, not boilerplate |
