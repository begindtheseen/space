Think about joining a school sports team. To play in the league at all, the league's rules say you must be the right age — the coach cannot change that. Then the coach adds rules of their own: practice is at 6 a.m., and phones stay in the bag. And for the travel team, a separate authority — the regional association — runs its own sign-up and checks. Three kinds of rule, from three different sources. Knowing which is which tells you what you can argue with and what you cannot.

Job postings for GNC roles work the same way. Some carry more than the export-control paragraph:

- A role touching national-security work may add a line about obtaining and maintaining a **Top Secret clearance**.
- Nearly every posting at a hardware company states **pre-employment and random drug and alcohol testing** as a condition of employment.
- Many carry a line about being willing to work **extended hours and weekends** when needed to meet critical deadlines.

None of these is the ITAR requirement itself. This lesson sorts each one into its bucket — a separate government process, or the employer's own condition — using the skill lesson three built, now applied to three concrete cases.

Getting the sorting right tells you what is being asked of you, and by whom. A requirement run by the federal government cannot be negotiated with a hiring manager. A requirement the employer chose is a fact about that employer, not about the law. It is worth knowing plainly, but not worth mistaking for something else.

## Top Secret clearance: a separate government process on top

A **security clearance** is the government's permission for a person to see **[[classified|classification-levels]]** information — national-security secrets. It is not an ITAR idea at all. It is governed by a completely different body of executive-branch policy.

Here is how it works, step by step:

1. The employer **sponsors** you. You cannot apply for a clearance on your own; a company or agency that needs you to have one starts the process.
2. You fill out **[[Standard Form 86|sf86]]** (SF-86, read "S-F eighty-six"), the Questionnaire for National Security Positions. It is a long, detailed disclosure of your personal history: where you have lived and worked, your foreign contacts, your finances, and much more.
3. The government runs a **background investigation** — checking records and interviewing people who know you.
4. The government makes an **[[adjudication|adjudication]]** decision: yes or no.
5. If granted, you must **maintain** it. That means staying under **continuous evaluation** afterward — ongoing checks, and reporting certain life events — not a one-time stamp.

The company sponsors the process, but the government decides the outcome, on its own timeline. A Top Secret investigation commonly takes many months. How long depends on the specifics of each case, not on anything an employer or a study app can predict.

### A narrower group than US persons

Clearance eligibility is narrower than the four US-person statuses. As a general rule, it is limited to **US citizens**. Exceptions for non-citizens exist on paper but are rare and limited. So lawful permanent residents, refugees and asylees — every one of whom is fully a US person under 22 CFR § 120.62 — are, in the ordinary case, not eligible for a clearance at all.

This is the distinction this whole module rests on. ITAR eligibility and clearance eligibility are **[[not the same test at different strengths|nested-groups]]**. Clearance sits on top of ITAR eligibility for a subset of roles, and it asks a different, stricter question. A green card holder can be completely eligible under ITAR and still not eligible for a clearance-required role. That is not a flaw in their status. Clearance asks something their status does not answer.

### Which roles add it

Roles add a clearance line because the work itself touches classified material. At SpaceX that means some roles in the GNC organization, notably on the SRE and **[[Starshield|starshield]]** side — the national-security programs, rather than the commercial Starlink or Falcon lines.

Two things never happen. Holding a clearance does not replace ITAR eligibility. And meeting ITAR eligibility gets you no closer to a clearance if citizenship is what stands in the way. They are two separate gates, and a clearance-required role asks you to pass both.

::: key
Some GNC-org roles, notably on the SRE and Starshield side, list the ability and willingness to obtain and maintain a Top Secret clearance. Clearance requires US citizenship and a detailed background investigation.

A security clearance is a separate government process (SF-86, a background investigation, adjudication, and continuous evaluation afterward), not an ITAR requirement. It is generally limited to US citizens — a narrower group than the full set of US persons — and it is required in addition to ordinary export-control eligibility, never instead of it.
:::

::: warning Meeting the four ITAR statuses is not enough for a clearance-required role
A lawful permanent resident, a refugee or an asylee can be completely eligible under 22 CFR § 120.62 and still not qualify for a role that also requires a Top Secret clearance, because clearance eligibility is, as a general matter, limited to citizens. Read a posting's full requirement. Do not stop reading once the ITAR language looks satisfied — a clearance line changes what is being asked.
:::

## Drug and alcohol testing: the employer's condition of employment

Postings state **pre-employment** testing (a test before you start, usually after an offer) and **random** testing (unannounced tests while you work there) for drugs and alcohol. This is a condition of employment, independent of state law where the facility sits.

It is not an ITAR requirement. Nothing in the Arms Export Control Act or in 22 CFR Parts 120 through 130 mentions it. It is the kind of requirement lesson three called the employer's own condition. It is common across **[[safety-sensitive|safety-sensitive]]** manufacturing and aerospace work generally, and stated plainly in the posting.

Where does it really come from, then? From the employer's own policy — sometimes shaped by the **[[terms of its government contracts|drug-free-contracts]]**, which can require a drug-free workforce program. Either way, its source is not export-control law. In principle it is a fact about that employer's practices, not a rule true of the whole industry by law. In practice it is close to universal at aerospace hardware companies, for safety reasons that have nothing to do with ITAR.

### What about states where a substance is legal?

A state legalizing a substance for adults changes what the state's police can arrest you for. It does not, by itself, stop an employer from making a clean test a condition of a job. So a candidate in a state where recreational use of something is legal is still subject to the employer's testing policy for that job.

Be exact about one detail. A few states have passed laws limiting how employers may test for or act on off-duty use of some substances. Those laws often carve out exceptions — for example, for jobs tied to federal contracts or security clearances, which describes much of aerospace. The practical rule for a candidate reading a posting is the one on the card: the testing is stated as a condition of employment, and it applies wherever the facility happens to be.

## Extended hours and weekends: read it as description, not decoration

Some parts of a job posting are close to **[[boilerplate|boilerplate]]** — standard text that appears, in some form, on nearly every posting in nearly every industry: the mission statement, the equal-opportunity language, the list of preferred qualifications. Legal and recruiting teams write them for reasons only loosely tied to the specific role.

A line saying the candidate must be willing to work extended hours and weekends when needed to meet critical deadlines is a different kind of sentence. It is put there deliberately. It is specific to how certain hardware companies describe their real pace of work. And it matches publicly reported accounts of how programs at fast-moving launch companies run. Eric Berger's *[[Liftoff|liftoff]]*, listed in this module's resources, describes exactly this working style in SpaceX's early years — as context, not as a compliance document.

Treat this line as a real description of a real working condition, not a formality to skim past. It belongs in your decision about whether to accept an offer, weighed like salary, location or team. If it comes up in an interview, answer honestly about how you feel about it, rather than reciting it back to sound keen.

Like drug testing, this is the employer's own stated expectation, not a legal requirement. Unlike drug testing, whether it is a dealbreaker for you is a personal judgment this module cannot make for you.

::: key
Postings state pre-employment and random drug and alcohol testing. This is a condition of employment, independent of state law where the facility sits.

Verbatim from postings: willing to work extended hours and weekends when needed to meet critical deadlines. Treat it as a real description of the job, not boilerplate.

Both are employer conditions, not ITAR requirements — real conditions of the job, worth taking at face value when you decide on an offer, but not enforced by, or connected to, export-control law.
:::

## Sorting the three

Put the three next to the ITAR gate and ask the same question of each: *who sets it?*

| Requirement | Who sets it | Can a hiring manager waive it? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ITAR US-person status | Federal export-control law | No |
| Top Secret clearance | The federal government's own process | No — the government decides |
| Drug and alcohol testing | The employer (sometimes via contract terms) | Not in a hiring process — it is company policy |
| Extended hours | The employer | It is a description of the job, not a test |

::: example Two roles, one ITAR test, two different extra requirements
**Role 1.** A GNC Engineer, ADCS posting on a commercial communications-satellite program. (ADCS, read "A-D-C-S", is the attitude determination and control system — the part that knows and controls which way the spacecraft points.) It states the standard four-status export-control language and nothing about clearance. A lawful permanent resident meets that language, so she is fully eligible for the role as posted.

**Role 2.** A GNC-adjacent role supporting a national-security payload program. It states the same export-control language, and adds: must be able to obtain and maintain a Top Secret clearance.

Check the same lawful permanent resident against Role 2:

- ITAR gate: passes, exactly as before — her status has not changed.
- Clearance gate: generally requires citizenship. She is not a citizen, so she does not pass.

Result: eligible for Role 1, not for Role 2. Sanity check: both postings are consistent with everything this module has taught. Role 2 asks one further, separate question that Role 1 does not.
:::

::: example A testing policy that does not bend to state law
An aerospace manufacturer runs a factory in a state where recreational use of a particular substance is legal under state law. Its posting still states pre-employment and random drug and alcohol testing as a condition of employment, with no exception for substances legal under state law.

Is that a contradiction? Work through it:

1. What did the state change? What its own law enforcement may prosecute adults for.
2. What does the posting set? A condition of a job offer the company is free to extend or withhold.
3. Do they conflict? Not in general: one is about criminal law, the other about the terms of a job. Where a state has added specific worker protections, they commonly exempt the kind of federal-contract and clearance work that aerospace companies do.

So the policy is consistent. It is the company's own, stated directly. Like the export-control paragraph — though for entirely different reasons — it is not something a candidate can negotiate around during hiring, however reasonable an argument from state law might sound.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
A lawful permanent resident feels confident about applying to a clearance-required GNC role, reasoning that they are fully a US person under ITAR. What is missing from this reasoning?
:::

::: answer
Being a full US person under 22 CFR § 120.62 satisfies the ITAR requirement. But clearance eligibility is a separate, stricter test that, as a general rule, is limited to US citizens. A lawful permanent resident meets the first gate completely without meeting the second. The two are governed by entirely different processes that both happen to apply to this particular role.
:::

::: check
Name the one government process among the three covered in this lesson, and explain why the other two are not export-control requirements at all.
:::

::: answer
The security clearance is the government process: the SF-86, a background investigation, adjudication by the government and continuous evaluation afterward, with the employer only sponsoring it. Drug and alcohol testing and the extended-hours expectation are both the employer's own stated conditions. Nothing in ITAR, the Arms Export Control Act or any other export-control rule addresses either one. They appear in postings because specific companies choose to state them (in the case of testing, sometimes because their contracts call for a drug-free workforce program), not because export-control law imposes them.
:::

::: check
Why does a state legalizing a substance not change a private aerospace employer's drug-testing policy for its facilities in that state?
:::

::: answer
Legalization changes what conduct the state's law enforcement may prosecute. It does not, by itself, require a private employer to match its workplace rules to it. Testing here is the employer's own condition of employment, stated in the posting, and the employer may set stricter conditions than state criminal law as a term of the job it offers. Where a state has added specific protections for off-duty use, those typically carve out federal-contract and clearance roles — the kind common in aerospace — so the posting's condition still applies.
:::

::: check
A candidate treats the "extended hours and weekends" line as generic corporate language, like an equal-opportunity statement, and does not think about it before accepting an offer. What is the risk?
:::

::: answer
Unlike real boilerplate, this line is a specific, deliberate description of an expected working pattern at the companies that state it, consistent with publicly reported accounts of how such programs run. Treating it as decoration means accepting an offer without weighing a real condition of the job — one that belongs in the same decision as salary or location — against your own priorities. The risk is meeting it as a surprise after you start.
:::

::: check
A candidate holds one of the four ITAR statuses and is applying to a role with no clearance requirement. Does the drug-testing line in the posting still apply, and on what basis?
:::

::: answer
Yes. Drug and alcohol testing is the employer's own condition of employment, independent of ITAR eligibility. It applies to anyone the company hires for that role, whichever of the four statuses they hold, and whether or not the role needs a clearance. ITAR eligibility, clearance and drug testing are three separate conditions; meeting one says nothing about the others.
:::

## Summary

| Requirement | Source | Applies to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ITAR / US-person status | Federal law (22 CFR § 120.62) | Every export-controlled role |
| Top Secret clearance | Separate federal process (sponsorship, SF-86, background investigation, adjudication, continuous evaluation) | Specific roles (e.g. SRE, Starshield); generally US citizens only |
| Drug and alcohol testing | Employer policy, sometimes shaped by contract terms | Pre-employment and random; stated per posting, independent of state law |
| Extended hours / weekends | Employer's stated expectation | Stated per posting; a real working condition, not boilerplate |

Everything up to here assumed the gate is passed, or explained exactly what happens when it is not. The next two lessons are for the second case: what a real set of alternatives looks like, treated with the same seriousness as everything before.

::: context classification-levels Three levels of secret
US classified information comes in three levels, set by how much damage to national security its release could cause. **Confidential** could cause damage; **Secret**, serious damage; **Top Secret**, exceptionally grave damage. Each level needs a matching clearance, and a Top Secret clearance brings the deepest investigation. Even then you only see what your job needs — the "need to know" rule.

::: context sf86 The SF-86, in plain words
The **SF-86** is a long government questionnaire — often well over a hundred pages once filled in — about your life: homes, jobs, schools, relatives, foreign travel and contacts, money problems, drug use, police records. Investigators then check it and interview people who know you. The most common trouble is not a bad past; it is leaving something out. Honesty on the form matters more than a spotless history, because hiding something is itself treated as a warning sign.
:::

::: context adjudication What "adjudication" means
To **adjudicate** is to judge and decide, from the Latin for "to judge". In clearance work, an adjudicator weighs the investigation's findings against a published set of thirteen guidelines — among them allegiance to the United States, foreign influence, financial problems, drug involvement and personal conduct. They look at the "whole person": one old mistake, honestly disclosed, is judged differently from a pattern that is still going on.
:::

::: context nested-groups Citizens inside US persons inside everyone
The groups nest, like circles inside circles. Every US citizen is a US person, but not every US person is a citizen.

::: context starshield What Starshield is
**Starshield** is SpaceX's business line for national-security customers, announced in 2022. It builds on the technology of Starlink, the company's commercial internet satellites, but for government missions. Because that work can involve classified information, some of the engineering roles supporting it list a Top Secret clearance on top of the usual export-control paragraph. The postings name such program groups by internal labels, like SRE; the label matters less than the clearance line itself.
:::

::: context safety-sensitive Why hardware work is "safety-sensitive"
A **safety-sensitive** job is one where a slip by an impaired worker could hurt people or wreck expensive equipment. In aerospace that includes lifting a stage with a crane, loading propellants at hundreds of degrees below zero, working near tanks under high pressure, or signing off flight software. Random testing exists because a single impaired shift in those settings can be catastrophic — which is why the policy is common across the industry whatever ITAR says.
:::

::: context drug-free-contracts Contracts can bring their own rules
When a company takes government money, the contract comes with clauses. Many federal contractors must keep a drug-free workplace policy, and defense contracts can include a clause requiring a drug-free workforce program, which may include testing people in sensitive positions. That is still not export-control law — it is a condition the company agreed to in order to win the work, and it flows down into its own employment policy.

::: context boilerplate Where "boilerplate" comes from
In the 1800s, US newspapers bought ready-made stories and ads from syndicates, delivered as metal printing plates that could not be edited — people compared them to the thick steel plates used to build steam boilers. "Boilerplate" came to mean standard text reused everywhere without change. The skill this lesson asks for is telling true boilerplate apart from a sentence someone put there on purpose.
:::

::: context liftoff A book about a hard-working start
*Liftoff*, by space journalist Eric Berger, published in 2021, tells how SpaceX got its first rocket, Falcon 1, to orbit after three failed launches. Much of it is about the pace: small teams, a remote island launch site, very long days to hit dates. Read it for the texture of the culture the hours line describes — but remember it is one reporter's account of a young company, not a description of every team today.
:::
