Think of a concert with a guest list at the door. The bouncer does not ask how much you love the band, how far you traveled, or whether a friend inside vouches for you. The bouncer checks one thing: is your name on the list? If it is, you walk in. If it is not, nothing else you say changes the answer.

The export-control paragraph in a SpaceX posting works like that list. Every posting for an export-controlled role carries it, and it reads:

> To conform to U.S. Government export regulations, applicant must be a (i) U.S. citizen or national, (ii) U.S. lawful permanent resident (aka green card holder), (iii) Refugee under 8 U.S.C. 1157, or (iv) Asylee under 8 U.S.C. 1158, or be eligible to obtain the required authorizations from the U.S. Department of State.

Similar wording appears at nearly every US company that designs, builds, or tests launch vehicles or export-controlled spacecraft. The previous lesson showed *what* is controlled: technical data, which is most of what a GNC engineer produces. This lesson is about *who* may receive it.

It covers the four named statuses on the list, and the legal definition of **U.S. person** that sits behind them, at 22 CFR § 120.62. The fifth part of the sentence — "or be eligible to obtain the required authorizations" — is real, and it gets the whole next lesson.

Get this lesson exactly right. The most common way people misjudge their own situation is by rounding a nearby status up to one of the four. A work visa is not on the list. A security clearance is not on the list. Years of living in the US, a pending green card application, or a job offer are not on the list. Several of those matter a great deal elsewhere in career planning. None of them meets *this* requirement. Mixing them up is the error that wastes the most time.

::: key
The verbatim SpaceX export-control paragraph: "To conform to U.S. Government export regulations, applicant must be a (i) U.S. citizen or national, (ii) U.S. lawful permanent resident (aka green card holder), (iii) Refugee under 8 U.S.C. 1157, or (iv) Asylee under 8 U.S.C. 1158, or be eligible to obtain the required authorizations from the U.S. Department of State."
:::

## Laws and rule books, one more time

The citations in that paragraph come in two kinds, and it helps to be able to tell them apart at a glance.

- **"8 U.S.C. 1157"** points into the **United States Code** — the laws Congress passed. Title 8 of the Code is immigration law. Read it aloud as "title eight, U-S-C, section eleven fifty-seven."
- **"22 CFR § 120.62"** points into the **Code of Federal Regulations** — the rule books agencies write under those laws. This one is inside ITAR.

So ITAR, a rule book, borrows its definition of who counts from immigration law. That borrowing is why a job posting about rockets cites immigration statutes.

## The four statuses, and where each comes from

ITAR defines a U.S. person at 22 CFR § 120.62. For an individual, it is anyone who is either:

- a **lawful permanent resident**, as defined in 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(20); or
- a **[[protected individual|protected-individual]]**, as defined in 8 U.S.C. § 1324b(a)(3).

(The same section also counts US-organized companies and government bodies as U.S. persons. That part does not matter for a job applicant.)

Those two cross-references, unpacked, give exactly the four statuses a posting names.

1. **U.S. citizen or national.** A **citizen** is someone who is American by birth or by **naturalization** — the legal process of becoming a citizen later in life. A **[[U.S. national|us-national]]** who is not a citizen is a much smaller group: mainly people born in American Samoa. Both count.
2. **U.S. lawful permanent resident** — a **[[green card|green-card]]** holder. This is a specific immigration status, with a card to prove it. It is not a general sense of having lived in the US a long time, and it is not having applied for one.
3. **Refugee under 8 U.S.C. § 1157.** Someone admitted to the United States through the federal refugee program. That program checks and approves people while they are still *outside* the country, and they arrive with the status already granted.
4. **Asylee under 8 U.S.C. § 1158.** Someone who applied for **asylum** — protection from persecution — and was granted it, usually after already arriving in the United States. The legal test is the same one used for refugees: a well-founded fear of persecution. Only the route is different. The [[difference between a refugee and an asylee|refugee-vs-asylee]] is where you apply, not how strong your case is.

### Why only two of the four carry citations

The "protected individual" definition in § 1324b(a)(3) comes from a different corner of immigration law: the rules that stop employers from discriminating in hiring because of national origin or citizenship status. ITAR reuses that list. It covers citizens and nationals, and it names refugees admitted under § 1157 and asylees granted under § 1158. It also mentions permanent residents, but with conditions attached — so ITAR adds its own separate reference to § 1101(a)(20) to make sure every green card holder counts.

The two citations a posting prints — § 1157 and § 1158 — are the laws that define refugee and asylee status. This module asks you to know both from memory. Citizenship and permanent residence usually appear in plain English with no number attached, because everyone recognizes them. All four rest on the same legal chain.

::: key
The four statuses: U.S. citizen or national; U.S. lawful permanent resident (green card holder); refugee under 8 U.S.C. § 1157; asylee under 8 U.S.C. § 1158. The regulatory definition sits at 22 CFR § 120.62, built from 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(20) (permanent residence) and 8 U.S.C. § 1324b(a)(3) (protected individual, covering citizens, nationals, refugees, and asylees).
:::

::: key
The four eligible statuses: US citizen or national; US lawful permanent resident; refugee under 8 U.S.C. 1157; asylee under 8 U.S.C. 1158. Plus the residual category of being eligible to obtain the required State Department authorizations.
:::

## What does not count, no matter how close it looks

### Work visas

A **visa** is permission to enter and stay in the US for a purpose. A **work visa** is permission to live and work here. It is not one of the four statuses, and it does not turn into one by being renewed, by seniority, or by a pending application.

That covers every common [[work visa|visa-letters]]:

- **H-1B** — the common visa for specialist professional jobs;
- **O-1** — for people of extraordinary ability;
- **L-1** — for transfers inside one company from an office abroad;
- **TN** — for Canadian and Mexican professionals under the USMCA trade agreement;
- **F-1** student status with **[[Optional Practical Training|opt]]** (OPT) — a period of work allowed after study.

Each of these lets a person live and work in the US, often for years. None of them makes that person a U.S. person under 22 CFR § 120.62. This is not a technicality. It is the whole point of the definition: a visa is permission to be here and work, while U.S.-person status is one of four specific statuses.

### Statuses that sound closer than they are

**Temporary Protected Status** (**TPS**) and **Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals** (**DACA**) both let people stay and work in the US. Neither is refugee status, asylee status, or permanent residence. Neither is on the list. See [[the note on these two programs|tps-daca]] for what each one is.

A **pending green card application** is not permanent residence — even one far along, even one with an approved petition underneath it. The [[green card process|green-card-steps]] has several stages, and only the last one, the actual grant, counts. "In process" and "granted" are different states. Only "granted" is on the list.

A **pending asylum application**, still waiting for a decision, is likewise not the same as being an asylee.

None of this judges how serious or legitimate these situations are. The regulation names four specific statuses that have already been granted. Being close to one of them is not the same as having it.

::: warning Dual citizenship does not weaken citizen status
Someone who holds U.S. citizenship and another country's citizenship at the same time is, in full, a U.S. citizen for this purpose. Citizenship is not partial or watered down by a second nationality. The line that matters is between having one of the four statuses and having none — not between "only American" and "American plus something else."
:::

## Two things this is not

Two mix-ups cause most of the confusion this module exists to prevent. Here they are, once, plainly.

**First: U.S.-person status is not the same as permission to work in the US.** Many people are legally allowed to work here — on a visa, on OPT, with a work permit tied to some other status — without holding any of the four statuses. Work authorization and export-control eligibility come from different laws that answer different questions. The posting's export-control paragraph asks the ITAR question, not the general "can you work here?" question.

**Second: U.S.-person status is not the same as a security clearance.** A **security clearance** is the government's approval for a person to see classified national-security information. Some GNC roles need both a clearance and U.S.-person status. Most need only U.S.-person status. Clearance is generally the stricter test — in practice usually limited to U.S. *citizens*, a smaller group than all U.S. persons — and it runs through a completely separate process.

So do not read a clearance line in one posting back into the ordinary export-control paragraph. And do not assume that meeting the export-control paragraph tells you anything about clearance. The only link is that clearance sits on top of U.S.-person status as an extra filter for some roles. Lesson 6 of this module covers that in full.

::: note Determine your own status from a primary source, not from this lesson alone
This lesson explains the categories accurately. Your own situation may still involve details — a status that changed, a status this module has not named, an unusual immigration history — that only a primary source can settle. The regulation is at 22 CFR § 120.62 in the current electronic Code of Federal Regulations. Authoritative information on what an immigration status is, and whether you hold it, comes from **U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services** (**USCIS**), the agency that grants those statuses. If your situation is truly unclear, the right next step is a qualified immigration attorney, not a guess. This module explains how the gate works. It cannot tell you which side of it you are on.
:::

::: example Four candidates, checked against the four statuses
A hiring manager reviews four hypothetical applicants for a GNC role on a launch vehicle program. For each one, walk down the list: citizen or national? Permanent resident? Refugee? Asylee?

**Candidate A** holds an H-1B visa, has worked in the US on it for eight years, and has a pending green card petition filed by a previous employer.
- Citizen or national? No.
- Permanent resident? Not yet — the petition is pending, not granted.
- Refugee? No. Asylee? No.
- Result: does not meet the requirement today, however long the tenure or strong the petition.

**Candidate B** was admitted to the US eighteen months ago as a refugee and has not applied for a green card.
- Refugee under 8 U.S.C. § 1157? Yes — the third status.
- Result: meets the requirement directly. There is no need to also hold or be seeking permanent residence.

**Candidate C** is on F-1 student status, working temporarily through OPT after earning a relevant engineering degree.
- None of the four applies.
- Result: does not meet the requirement. Strong grades do not substitute for a status on the list.

**Candidate D** holds a U.S. passport and also holds citizenship in a second country by birth.
- U.S. citizen? Yes — the first status, in full.
- Result: meets the requirement. The second citizenship does not enter into it.

Tally: two of the four clear the gate and two do not. Sanity check on the reasons: none of the four outcomes depended on skill, experience, or how good a case someone had for a *future* status. Only the status held today mattered — which is exactly how a guest list works.
:::

::: example Tracing a posting's two citations back to their statutes
A posting lists four items, and only the third and fourth carry a legal citation: "(iii) Refugee under 8 U.S.C. 1157, or (iv) Asylee under 8 U.S.C. 1158." Why only those two?

Step 1. Start from the regulation, 22 CFR § 120.62. It defines U.S. person through two references: § 1101(a)(20) for permanent residents and § 1324b(a)(3) for protected individuals.

Step 2. Open the protected-individual reference. It already bundles together citizens, nationals, refugees admitted under § 1157, and asylees granted under § 1158.

Step 3. Compare with the posting. The posting spells out the two statuses readers know least — refugee and asylee — and pins each to its statute. It states citizen, national, and permanent resident in plain English, because most readers recognize those on sight.

Check: does every status in the posting trace back to § 120.62? Citizen and national, yes, through § 1324b(a)(3). Permanent resident, yes, through § 1101(a)(20). Refugee and asylee, yes, through § 1324b(a)(3), which names §§ 1157 and 1158. The posting and the regulation agree. The posting is written for a general reader rather than for lawyers following a chain of cross-references.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
State the four statuses that satisfy the plainly named part of the eligibility requirement, and the two statutes a posting typically cites by number.
:::

::: answer
U.S. citizen or national; U.S. lawful permanent resident (green card holder); refugee under 8 U.S.C. § 1157; asylee under 8 U.S.C. § 1158. The two statutes a posting cites by number are 8 U.S.C. § 1157 (refugee) and 8 U.S.C. § 1158 (asylee). The regulatory definition behind all four is at 22 CFR § 120.62.
:::

::: check
An applicant holds an O-1 visa for extraordinary ability and has an approved I-140 immigrant petition, with a green card application still pending final adjudication. Does this applicant meet one of the four statuses today? Explain.
:::

::: answer
No. An O-1 visa is work authorization, not one of the four statuses. An approved immigrant petition (the I-140) plus a pending green card application is not the same as being a lawful permanent resident. Permanent residence exists only once it is actually granted. The applicant may well become a permanent resident later, but does not meet the requirement at the moment described.
:::

::: check
Explain, without appealing to authority, why a work visa and U.S. person status are conceptually different things rather than two names for the same idea.
:::

::: answer
They answer different questions. A work visa answers "may this person work in the United States?" — a matter of immigration and labor law, about entry and the job market. U.S.-person status under 22 CFR § 120.62 answers "may controlled technical data be released to this person without that counting as an export?" Different laws, different purposes, so meeting one says nothing about the other. A person can be fully authorized to work and still not be a U.S. person. The reverse also happens: a U.S. person may hold no separate work-authorization document at all, because citizenship and permanent residence already carry the right to work.
:::

::: check
A colleague says, "TPS is basically the same as asylum, so TPS holders should count as eligible too." Is this correct?
:::

::: answer
No. Temporary Protected Status is a separate, time-limited status given to nationals of countries the government has designated because of conditions like war or disaster. It is not asylee status under 8 U.S.C. § 1158. It does not require the individual finding of persecution that asylum does. And it is not one of the four statuses named in 22 CFR § 120.62 or in the posting. However similar the situations may feel, the regulation names four specific statuses, and TPS is not among them.
:::

::: check
Two applicants both currently hold H-1B status. One has an approved green card petition awaiting final processing; the other has not started the green card process at all. Does either currently meet the eligibility requirement, and does the difference between them matter to this specific determination?
:::

::: answer
Neither meets the requirement today. Permanent residence must actually be granted to count; an approved petition with the final step still pending is not enough. The difference between the two may matter a lot to how soon, if ever, each becomes eligible. But for today's check, both are in the same position: holding a work visa, not one of the four statuses.
:::

::: context us-national Nationals who are not citizens
A U.S. national owes loyalty to the United States and holds a U.S. passport, but is not a citizen. Today this mainly means people born in American Samoa (and tiny Swains Island next to it). Their passports carry a note saying the holder is a national, not a citizen. They can live and work anywhere in the US, and they become citizens through naturalization if they choose to.
:::

::: context green-card Why it is called a green card
The official name is the Permanent Resident Card, government form I-551. Earlier versions of the card were green, and the nickname stuck even through the years when the card was other colors. Holding one means you may live and work in the US permanently. After some years — usually five — a holder can apply to become a citizen.
:::

::: context refugee-vs-asylee Same test, different doorway
Refugees and asylees must both show a well-founded fear of persecution — because of race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or membership in a particular social group. The only difference is where they ask.

::: context visa-letters Why visas have letters
Most US visa names are the letter of the paragraph in immigration law that creates them. Section 1101(a)(15) of Title 8 lists visa types as subparagraphs (A) through (V): F for students, H for temporary workers, L for company transfers, O for extraordinary ability. TN is the exception — it comes from a trade agreement, first NAFTA and now USMCA. So "H-1B" is a legal address, not a code name.
:::

::: context opt Working after graduation on a student visa
Optional Practical Training lets an F-1 student work in their field after finishing a degree, usually for 12 months. Graduates in science, technology, engineering and mathematics can apply to extend it by up to 24 more months. Many international engineering graduates spend their first US job on OPT. It is real work authorization — but it is still not one of the four ITAR statuses.
:::

::: context tps-daca Two programs that let people stay, but not statuses on the list
TPS, created in 1990, lets nationals of designated countries — ones hit by war, disaster or similar crises — stay and work in the US for a limited, renewable period. DACA, started in 2012, delays deportation for some people brought to the US as children and lets them get work permits. Both are "permission to remain." Neither is refugee status, asylum, or permanent residence, so neither makes a person a U.S. person under ITAR.
:::
