Every SpaceX posting for an export-controlled role carries a paragraph that reads, in substance: to conform to U.S. Government export regulations, the applicant must be a U.S. citizen or national, a U.S. lawful permanent resident, a refugee under 8 U.S.C. § 1157, or an asylee under 8 U.S.C. § 1158, or be eligible to obtain the required authorizations from the U.S. Department of State. Comparable language appears at nearly every US company that designs, builds, or tests launch vehicles or export-controlled spacecraft. This lesson is about the first four items in that list — the plainly stated statuses — and about the regulatory definition of "U.S. person" that sits behind them at 22 CFR § 120.62. The fifth item, the residual "eligible to obtain authorization" clause, is real and worth understanding on its own terms, and the next lesson gives it the space it needs.

Get this lesson exactly right, because the single most common way people misjudge their own situation is by rounding a nearby status up to one of the four. A work visa is not on this list. A security clearance is not on this list. Years of US residence, a pending green card application, or an offer letter are not on this list. None of those things are irrelevant to a career in the field — several of them matter enormously elsewhere in this curriculum's career-planning modules — but none of them satisfy this specific requirement, and conflating them with the four statuses below is the error that costs people the most wasted effort.

## The four statuses, and where each comes from

ITAR defines "U.S. person" at 22 CFR § 120.62: a person who is a lawful permanent resident as defined by 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(20), or who is a "protected individual" as defined by 8 U.S.C. § 1324b(a)(3) — plus, separately, any corporation or governmental entity organized in the United States, which is not relevant to an individual job applicant. The regulatory definition is built from two statutory cross-references, and unpacking them gives you exactly the four categories a posting names:

1. **U.S. citizen or national.** A citizen by birth or naturalization, or the narrower category of a U.S. national who is not a citizen — a status that applies to people born in a small number of U.S. outlying territories such as American Samoa. Both are covered under the "protected individual" reference.
2. **U.S. lawful permanent resident** — a green card holder — defined by 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(20), referenced directly in 120.62's first clause. This is a specific, documented immigration status, evidenced by a permanent resident card, not a general sense of having lived in the US a long time or having applied for one.
3. **Refugee under 8 U.S.C. § 1157.** A person admitted to the United States under the federal refugee admissions program, which vets and admits people from outside the country who meet the legal definition of a refugee before they arrive.
4. **Asylee under 8 U.S.C. § 1158.** A person who applied for and was granted asylum, typically after arriving in the United States and establishing a well-founded fear of persecution under the same legal standard used for refugee status, decided through a different procedural track.

Categories 1, 3, and 4 all come from "protected individual" under 8 U.S.C. § 1324b(a)(3), which is itself borrowed from a different corner of federal law — the INA's anti-discrimination provisions on national origin and citizenship status in hiring — and repurposed by ITAR's definition of U.S. person. That is also why the two statutory citations postings actually print are § 1157 and § 1158: those are the specific provisions that define refugee and asylee status, and they are the two references this module's objectives ask you to be able to state from memory. Citizenship and lawful permanent residence are usually described in plain English in a posting without a citation attached, because they are the most familiar of the four, but they rest on the same regulatory chain.

::: key
The four statuses: U.S. citizen or national; U.S. lawful permanent resident (green card holder); refugee under 8 U.S.C. § 1157; asylee under 8 U.S.C. § 1158. The regulatory definition sits at 22 CFR § 120.62, built from 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(20) (permanent residence) and 8 U.S.C. § 1324b(a)(3) (protected individual, covering citizens, nationals, refugees, and asylees).
:::

## What does not count, no matter how close it looks

A work visa is authorization to work in the United States. It is not a status among the four above, and it does not become one through renewal, seniority, or a pending petition. This includes H-1B (the common specialty-occupation visa), O-1 (extraordinary ability), L-1 (intracompany transfer), TN (for Canadian and Mexican professionals under USMCA), and F-1 student status with Optional Practical Training. Every one of these authorizes someone to live and work in the United States, often for years, and none of them is "U.S. person" status under 22 CFR § 120.62. The distinction is not a technicality the regulation glosses over — it is the entire point of the definition. A visa is permission to be here and work; U.S. person status is a specific immigration or citizenship status that the four categories above exhaustively define.

The same caution applies to statuses that sound closer than they are. Temporary Protected Status (TPS) and Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals (DACA) grant permission to remain and work in the United States, but neither is refugee status, asylee status, or lawful permanent residence, and neither appears among the four categories. A pending green card application — even one far along in processing, even one with an approved underlying petition — is not lawful permanent residence until it is actually granted; "in process" and "granted" are different states under the definition, and only the second one counts. An approved asylum application that has not yet been formally granted, or a pending asylum application still under adjudication, is likewise not the same as being an asylee. None of this is a judgment about the seriousness or legitimacy of these statuses — the regulation names four specific, already-conferred statuses, and adjacency to one of them is not membership in it.

::: warning Dual citizenship does not weaken citizen status
A person who holds U.S. citizenship and the citizenship of another country at the same time is, in full, a U.S. citizen for this purpose. Citizenship is not partial or diluted by also holding a second nationality. The distinction that matters for eligibility is between having one of the four statuses and not having any of them — not between "only American" and "American plus something else."
:::

## Two things this is not, said once and plainly

Two conflations account for most of the confusion this module exists to prevent, and it is worth naming both directly rather than letting either linger.

First: U.S. person status is not the same thing as having permission to work in the United States generally. Plenty of people are legally authorized to work in the US — on a visa, on OPT, under a work permit tied to another status — without holding one of the four statuses above. Work authorization and export-control eligibility are governed by entirely different bodies of law answering entirely different questions, and a posting's export-control paragraph is asking the ITAR question, not the general work-authorization question.

Second, and covered fully in a later lesson: U.S. person status is not the same thing as holding, or being eligible for, a security clearance. Some GNC roles require both; most require only U.S. person status. Clearance eligibility is generally a stricter test — in practice usually limited to U.S. citizens specifically, a narrower group than the full set of U.S. persons — governed by an entirely separate process. Do not read a posting's mention of a clearance requirement back into the ordinary export-control paragraph, and do not assume that meeting the export-control paragraph tells you anything about clearance eligibility. They are related only in the sense that clearance sits on top of U.S. person status as an additional filter for a subset of roles; the fifth lesson in this module covers that relationship in full.

::: note Determine your own status from a primary source, not from this lesson alone
This lesson explains the categories accurately, but your own situation may involve details — dual or changing status, a status this module has not named, an unusual immigration history — that only a primary source can resolve with certainty. The regulation itself is at 22 CFR § 120.62 in the current electronic Code of Federal Regulations. Authoritative information on what a given immigration status actually is, and whether you hold it, comes from U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS). If your situation is genuinely unclear, the right next step is a qualified immigration attorney, not a guess. This module explains how the gate works; it does not, and cannot, tell you which side of it you are on.
:::

::: example Four candidates, checked against the four statuses
A hiring manager is reviewing four hypothetical applicants for a GNC role on a launch vehicle program.

Candidate A holds an H-1B visa and has worked in the United States on it for eight years, with a pending green card petition filed by a previous employer. Checked against the four statuses: not a citizen or national, not yet a lawful permanent resident (the petition is pending, not granted), not a refugee, not an asylee. Candidate A does not meet the requirement today, regardless of tenure or the strength of the pending petition.

Candidate B was admitted to the United States eighteen months ago as a refugee and has not applied for a green card. Checked against the four statuses: refugee under 8 U.S.C. § 1157 — the third category, satisfied directly, with no need to also hold or be pursuing permanent residence.

Candidate C is on F-1 student status, authorized to work temporarily through Optional Practical Training after graduating with a relevant engineering degree. Checked against the four statuses: none of the four applies. Strong academic credentials do not substitute for a status on the list.

Candidate D holds a U.S. passport and also holds citizenship in a second country by birth. Checked against the four statuses: U.S. citizen — the first category, satisfied in full; the second citizenship is not relevant to this determination.

Two of the four clear the gate; two do not, for reasons entirely independent of skill, experience, or the strength of a case for a future status.
:::

::: example Tracing a posting's two citations back to their statutes
A posting states the requirement as four items, and only the third and fourth carry an explicit statutory citation: "(iii) Refugee under 8 U.S.C. 1157, or (iv) Asylee under 8 U.S.C. 1158." Read alongside 22 CFR § 120.62, the full picture becomes clear: the regulatory definition of U.S. person references 8 U.S.C. § 1324b(a)(3) for "protected individual," a term that itself already bundles together citizens, nationals, refugees admitted under § 1157, and asylees granted status under § 1158. The posting's drafters chose to spell out only the two categories — refugee and asylee — that are less commonly known and benefit from an explicit statutory anchor, while stating citizen, national, and lawful permanent resident in plain English, since those are more widely understood on sight. The posting language and the regulation are consistent; the posting is written for a general audience rather than for lawyers reading a cross-reference chain.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
State the four statuses that satisfy the plainly named part of the eligibility requirement, and the two statutes a posting typically cites by number.
:::

::: answer
U.S. citizen or national; U.S. lawful permanent resident (green card holder); refugee under 8 U.S.C. § 1157; asylee under 8 U.S.C. § 1158. The two statutes typically cited by number in the posting itself are 8 U.S.C. § 1157 (refugee) and 8 U.S.C. § 1158 (asylee); the regulatory definition behind all four sits at 22 CFR § 120.62.
:::

::: check
An applicant holds an O-1 visa for extraordinary ability and has an approved I-140 immigrant petition, with a green card application still pending final adjudication. Does this applicant meet one of the four statuses today? Explain.
:::

::: answer
No. An O-1 visa is work authorization, not one of the four statuses, and an approved immigrant petition with a pending adjustment of green card status is not the same as being a lawful permanent resident — permanent residence exists only once it is actually granted. The applicant may well become a lawful permanent resident later, but does not meet the requirement at the point described.
:::

::: check
Explain, without appealing to authority, why a work visa and U.S. person status are conceptually different things rather than two names for the same idea.
:::

::: answer
A work visa answers the question "is this person authorized to work in the United States," which is a matter of immigration and labor law aimed at controlling the domestic labor market and border entry. U.S. person status under 22 CFR § 120.62 answers a different question entirely: "is this person, regardless of where they work, someone to whom controlled technical data may be released without that release counting as an export." The two questions are governed by different statutes for different purposes, so satisfying one has no bearing on the other. A person can be fully authorized to work and still not be a U.S. person, and — much less commonly, but the flip side is real — a U.S. person can hold that status without necessarily holding any particular work authorization document at all, since citizenship and permanent residence themselves confer the right to work.
:::

::: check
A colleague says, "TPS is basically the same as asylum, so TPS holders should count as eligible too." Is this correct?
:::

::: answer
No. Temporary Protected Status is a distinct, time-limited status granted to nationals of designated countries experiencing conditions such as armed conflict or disaster; it is not asylee status under 8 U.S.C. § 1158, does not require the individualized persecution finding that asylum does, and is not one of the four statuses named in 22 CFR § 120.62 or in the posting language. However close the practical situations may feel, the regulation names four specific statuses, and TPS is not among them.
:::

::: check
Two applicants both currently hold H-1B status. One has an approved green card petition awaiting final processing; the other has not started the green card process at all. Does either currently meet the eligibility requirement, and does the difference between them matter to this specific determination?
:::

::: answer
Neither currently meets the requirement. Lawful permanent residence must actually be granted, not merely approved-in-part or pending, to satisfy the second status. The difference between the two applicants may matter enormously to how soon, if ever, either becomes eligible — but for today's determination, both are in the identical position: holding a work visa, not one of the four statuses.
:::
