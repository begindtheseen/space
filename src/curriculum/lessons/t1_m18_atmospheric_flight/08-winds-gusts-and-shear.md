Everything in the last three lessons hung on a single disturbance input: the wind-induced angle of attack $\alpha_w$. On a real launch day that input is a profile of wind speed and direction from the pad to 30 km, measured by a balloon two hours before lift-off, changing while the count runs, and studded with shears and gusts no measurement can resolve. The jet stream that carries the strongest winds sits at 10 to 14 km — exactly where dynamic pressure peaks — so the atmosphere delivers its largest disturbance at the moment the vehicle is least able to afford one.

A GNC engineer needs the wind in three forms. For trajectory design and day-of-launch steering, the **mean profile**: how wind varies with altitude, statistically and on the day. For load and control analysis, **discrete features**: wind shear across a layer and idealised gust shapes that stress the loop in a repeatable way. For Monte Carlo simulation, **continuous turbulence**: the Dryden and von Kármán spectra, which describe random gustiness statistically and can be generated by filtering white noise. This lesson covers all three and connects each to the angle of attack it produces at ascent speeds.

## Mean wind profiles

Wind speed generally increases with altitude through the troposphere and reaches a maximum near the tropopause, where the jet stream lives, before falling off in the stratosphere. At mid-latitude launch sites the jet core sits between about 10 and 14 km. Speeds there are strongly seasonal: monthly mean winds at Cape Canaveral in winter are of order 30 to 40 m/s, the 95th-percentile envelopes used for design reach 60 to 75 m/s, and individual days exceed 90 m/s. Summer winds at the same altitude are typically half as strong. The direction is predominantly westerly, which is why launch azimuth and season together decide whether the strongest wind arrives as a headwind, a tailwind or a crosswind on the vehicle's pitch plane.

For design work the wind is described statistically. Range reference atmospheres tabulate, for each altitude and month, the mean vector wind and its covariance — the wind components at one altitude are treated as jointly normal, and correlated with the components at neighbouring altitudes. From these tables one builds **synthetic design profiles**: a smooth envelope at some probability level (95 % or 99 %), a shear build-up beneath the peak and a discrete gust on top, all combined so that the resulting load has a known exceedance probability. The alternative is to fly the vehicle through hundreds of measured historical profiles in a Monte Carlo and read off the load distribution directly; modern programmes do both.

The mean profile is what the day-of-launch steering update removes. Balloons or a radar wind profiler measure the profile; the pitch and yaw programs are recomputed so that the vehicle flies with its nose into the measured wind at every altitude; the residual angle of attack comes only from what changes between measurement and launch, from shear and gusts finer than the measurement resolves, and from the controller's own dynamics.

::: example What an unbiased jet-stream wind would do
Suppose the steering were *not* updated and the vehicle flew its nominal gravity turn straight into a 75 m/s crosswind at 12 km, where the airspeed is 420 m/s and $\bar{q} = 27.4\ \mathrm{kPa}$. The angle of attack is $\arctan(75/420) = 10.1^\circ$ and the load indicator $27.4 \times 10.1 = 277\ \mathrm{kPa\cdot deg}$, roughly three times a typical envelope. Even a moderate 40 m/s wind at 300 m/s airspeed (earlier in the climb, at lower $\bar{q}$) is $7.6^\circ$. Wind biasing is not a refinement; without it most winter days would be unflyable.
:::

## Wind shear

**Wind shear** is the rate of change of wind velocity with altitude, $\partial \mathbf{w}/\partial h$. A vehicle climbing at $\dot h$ experiences a shear layer as a *time* rate of change of the relative wind, $\dot{\mathbf{w}} = (\partial\mathbf{w}/\partial h)\,\dot h$, and therefore as a ramp in angle of attack. Design shears are usually specified as the change in wind speed across a layer of given thickness — the strongest shears occur immediately below the jet core, where the wind builds up over a kilometre or two — and a change of 20 to 30 m/s across 1 km is within the design envelope at the Cape, with the exercise's 30 m/s per kilometre representing a severe case.

What makes shear dangerous is speed. At 70 s in the Lesson 2 trajectory the vehicle climbs at $\dot h = 418 \sin 54^\circ = 338\ \mathrm{m/s}$, so it crosses a 1 km layer in 3.0 s. A 30 m/s change in the perpendicular wind at 420 m/s airspeed is

$$
\Delta\alpha = \arctan\frac{30}{420} = 4.1^\circ,
$$

arriving as a ramp of about $1.4^\circ/\mathrm{s}$ — comparable to the response time of an attitude loop with a 0.36 Hz crossover, so the loop is still reacting when the ramp ends. At $\bar{q} = 27.4\ \mathrm{kPa}$ the load indicator jumps by $27.4 \times 4.1 = 112\ \mathrm{kPa\cdot deg}$ before load relief can take any of it back. This is the spike the max-Q exercise asks you to inject and then relieve.

::: key
Wind shear $\partial w/\partial h$ becomes an angle-of-attack ramp $\dot\alpha \approx (\partial w_\perp/\partial h)\,\dot h / V$ on a climbing vehicle; 30 m/s over 1 km at 420 m/s airspeed is $4.1^\circ$ in about 3 s. Because the traverse time is comparable to the attitude loop's response time, the airframe sees most of the shear before load relief acts.
:::

::: warning
A shear specification is meaningless without its layer thickness. "30 m/s of shear" over 1 km is a gradient of $0.03\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$; over 100 m it would be ten times steeper and physically closer to a gust. Design documents state shear as a speed change over a stated thickness, and gradients are usually larger over thinner layers.
:::

## Discrete gusts

A **discrete gust** is an idealised, deterministic wind feature used to stress a control loop in a reproducible way. The standard shape is the **1-cosine gust**, a smooth bump that rises from zero to a peak $w_m$ and returns to zero over a distance $2d_m$:

$$
w_g(s) = \frac{w_m}{2}\left[1 - \cos\left(\frac{\pi s}{d_m}\right)\right], \qquad 0 \le s \le 2 d_m,
$$

with $s$ the distance travelled into the gust and $d_m$ the gust half-width. The peak $w_m$ occurs at $s = d_m$. Launch-vehicle design uses gusts of roughly 9 m/s superposed on the synthetic profile at the jet peak, with half-widths from tens to a few hundred metres, and also uses "embedded" gusts built into measured profiles. For a 9 m/s gust at 420 m/s the peak angle of attack is $9/420\ \mathrm{rad} = 1.2^\circ$; the point is not the size but the speed of onset.

::: example A 1-cosine gust crossed at ascent speed
Take $w_m = 9\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and $d_m = 150\ \mathrm{m}$, so the gust spans 300 m of path. At $s = 37.5$, 75, 150, 225 and 300 m the wind is 1.32, 4.50, 9.00, 4.50 and 0 m/s. At a climb rate of 338 m/s the whole gust passes in $300/338 = 0.89\ \mathrm{s}$, so the angle of attack goes from zero to $1.2^\circ$ and back inside a second — far faster than a 0.36 Hz attitude loop can follow. The airframe takes the full $1.2^\circ$ as a transient load ($33\ \mathrm{kPa\cdot deg}$ at 27.4 kPa) essentially unrelieved, and the loop's job is to damp the attitude motion the gust excites afterwards. Gust loads are added to the shear and mean-wind loads in the structural budget for exactly this reason.
:::

## Continuous turbulence: Dryden and von Kármán

Real gustiness is random, and for simulation it is modelled as a stationary random process with a specified **power spectral density** (PSD). Two spectra are standard, both written in terms of the spatial frequency $\Omega$ (radians per metre) of the turbulent eddies, a **scale length** $L$ characterising the eddy size, and the turbulence intensity $\sigma$ (the root-mean-square gust velocity). For the component $w$ perpendicular to the flight path, the one that becomes angle of attack:

$$
\text{Dryden:}\quad
\Phi_w(\Omega) = \sigma_w^2\,\frac{L_w}{\pi}\;\frac{1 + 3(L_w\Omega)^2}{\left[1 + (L_w\Omega)^2\right]^2},
$$

$$
\text{von Kármán:}\quad
\Phi_w(\Omega) = \sigma_w^2\,\frac{L_w}{\pi}\;\frac{1 + \tfrac{8}{3}(1.339\,L_w\Omega)^2}{\left[1 + (1.339\,L_w\Omega)^2\right]^{11/6}} .
$$

The along-path component $u$ has the simpler forms $\Phi_u = \sigma_u^2 (2L_u/\pi)/[1 + (L_u\Omega)^2]$ (Dryden) and $\sigma_u^2 (2L_u/\pi)/[1 + (1.339 L_u\Omega)^2]^{5/6}$ (von Kármán). A vehicle flying through frozen turbulence at speed $V$ converts spatial frequency to temporal frequency by $\omega = \Omega V$ — **Taylor's hypothesis** — so the spectrum seen by the vehicle is $\Phi(\omega) = \Phi(\Omega = \omega/V)/V$ and the break frequency is $V/L$.

The two differ at high frequency. Von Kármán falls as $\Omega^{-5/3}$, the slope Kolmogorov's theory predicts for the inertial range and the one measurements confirm; Dryden falls as $\Omega^{-2}$, slightly too steep. At thirty times the break frequency the von Kármán spectrum carries 1.7 times the Dryden energy, which matters for structural excitation but little for the rigid-body loop. The reason Dryden survives is practical: its spectrum is a **rational function** of $\Omega^2$, so it can be produced exactly by passing white noise through a linear filter,

$$
H_u(s) = \sigma_u\sqrt{\frac{2L_u}{\pi V}}\;\frac{1}{1 + \dfrac{L_u}{V}s},
\qquad
H_w(s) = \sigma_w\sqrt{\frac{L_w}{\pi V}}\;\frac{1 + \sqrt{3}\,\dfrac{L_w}{V}s}{\left(1 + \dfrac{L_w}{V}s\right)^2},
$$

which is two lines of code in any simulation. Von Kármán's fractional exponents have no exact filter and are approximated when a rational form is needed. Above about 600 m altitude the military specifications set $L_u = L_v = L_w \approx 533\ \mathrm{m}$ (1750 ft), with intensities from about 1–2 m/s for light turbulence to 5 m/s and more for severe.

::: example Turbulence at max-Q
Fly at $V = 420\ \mathrm{m/s}$ through Dryden turbulence with $L_w = 533\ \mathrm{m}$ and $\sigma_w = 3\ \mathrm{m/s}$. The break frequency is $V/L_w = 0.79\ \mathrm{rad/s} = 0.125\ \mathrm{Hz}$, and the filter time constant $L_w/V = 1.27\ \mathrm{s}$. The RMS angle of attack is $\sigma_\alpha = \sigma_w/V = 0.0071\ \mathrm{rad} = 0.41^\circ$, so a $3\sigma$ excursion is $1.2^\circ$ — 34 kPa·deg at 27.4 kPa, riding on top of whatever the shear has produced. Integrating the spectrum, 64 % of the gust variance lies below 0.3 Hz, 88 % below 1 Hz: most of the turbulence is within the attitude loop's bandwidth, where the controller responds to it and the gimbal works continuously, while the remaining tenth or so passes straight through to the structure and to the bending modes.
:::

::: key
Dryden and von Kármán are the two standard turbulence spectra, parameterised by intensity $\sigma$ and scale length $L$ (about 533 m at altitude), converted to the vehicle's time domain by $\omega = \Omega V$. Von Kármán has the physically correct $\Omega^{-5/3}$ high-frequency slope; Dryden is rational and can be generated exactly by filtering white noise, which is why simulations use it.
:::

## Winds in the simulation and on launch day

In a 3-DOF or 6-DOF ascent simulation the wind enters in one place: the air-relative velocity $\mathbf{V} = \mathbf{v} - \mathbf{w}(h, t)$, from which $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\bar{q}$ follow as in Lesson 4. The mean profile is a table in altitude, the shear is built into that table, discrete gusts are added as functions of altitude or time, and turbulence is the output of the Dryden filters driven by unit white noise. The load indicator $\bar{q}\alpha_T$ is then logged along the trajectory and its peak compared with the envelope, run after run, until the exceedance probability is known.

On launch day the profile is measured. Rawinsonde balloons take about an hour to rise to 20 km and report wind from GPS tracking; Doppler radar wind profilers give a profile through the jet every few minutes without launching anything. The measured profile feeds the steering update and a loads assessment: the ascent team computes the predicted $\bar{q}\alpha_T$ against the certified envelope, including allowances for the wind change expected before lift-off and for unresolved gusts and shear, and a predicted exceedance is a launch-commit-criteria violation — the reason a countdown can be scrubbed for "upper-level winds" on a clear, calm morning.

::: warning
Wind direction conventions bite. Meteorologists report the direction the wind blows *from*; a vector-wind table gives the components it blows *toward*. A 270° wind is a westerly, blowing eastward. Get this wrong and your wind bias steers the nose downwind, doubling the load instead of removing it.
:::

::: warning
Scale-length conventions differ between the military specifications. MIL-F-8785C writes the same Dryden spectrum with $2L$ in place of $L$, so its quoted scale lengths are half the values above for the same physical turbulence. Check which convention a spectrum formula uses before plugging in 533 m.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
A vehicle climbs at 300 m/s vertical speed with 380 m/s airspeed through a layer in which the crosswind increases by 24 m/s over 800 m. What angle-of-attack ramp does it experience, and over how long?
:::

::: answer
The layer takes $800/300 = 2.7\ \mathrm{s}$ to cross. The angle-of-attack change is $\arctan(24/380) = 3.6^\circ$, so the ramp rate is about $1.35^\circ/\mathrm{s}$. The shear gradient is $24/800 = 0.03\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ — the same gradient as the exercise's 30 m/s per kilometre.
:::

::: check
Why is the Dryden spectrum preferred for simulation even though the von Kármán spectrum matches measured turbulence better?
:::

::: answer
The Dryden PSD is a rational function of frequency, so it is exactly the output spectrum of white noise passed through a linear filter with real, finite-order transfer function — $H_w(s)$ above. That makes it trivial to generate a time history in a simulation and to include in a linear analysis. The von Kármán spectrum's exponents $5/6$ and $11/6$ correspond to fractional-order dynamics with no exact finite filter; it can only be approximated. The price is a high-frequency slope of $-2$ instead of $-5/3$, which under-predicts energy at frequencies well above $V/L$.
:::

::: check
For Dryden turbulence with $L_w = 533\ \mathrm{m}$, how does the break frequency seen by the vehicle change between 300 m/s and 600 m/s airspeed, and what does that mean for the attitude loop?
:::

::: answer
The break frequency is $V/L_w$: $300/533 = 0.56\ \mathrm{rad/s}$ (0.09 Hz) at 300 m/s and $600/533 = 1.13\ \mathrm{rad/s}$ (0.18 Hz) at 600 m/s. Faster flight compresses the same eddies into a shorter time, pushing turbulence energy toward and past the attitude loop's crossover (0.36 Hz in the running example). At the same time $\sigma_\alpha = \sigma_w/V$ halves, so the angle-of-attack excursions are smaller but faster — and dynamic pressure at 600 m/s is already falling, so the loads matter less.
:::

::: check
A 1-cosine gust has $w_m = 12\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and $d_m = 100\ \mathrm{m}$. What is the wind at $s = 50\ \mathrm{m}$, and how long does the gust last for a vehicle climbing at 350 m/s?
:::

::: answer
$w_g(50) = 6\,[1 - \cos(\pi \times 50/100)] = 6\,[1 - 0] = 6\ \mathrm{m/s}$, half the peak, since $s = d_m/2$ is the quarter-cycle point. The gust spans $2d_m = 200\ \mathrm{m}$, which at 350 m/s lasts $0.57\ \mathrm{s}$. At 420 m/s airspeed the peak angle of attack would be $12/420 = 0.029\ \mathrm{rad} = 1.6^\circ$.
:::

::: check
Explain why the day-of-launch steering update removes the mean wind but cannot remove wind shear or turbulence, and what handles the remainder.
:::

::: answer
The steering update reshapes the pitch and yaw programs using a profile measured an hour or more before launch; it can point the nose into whatever wind that profile shows at each altitude, with the resolution of the measurement (hundreds of metres) and the persistence of the wind over the countdown. Shear finer than that resolution, gusts of a few hundred metres and turbulence are not in the measured profile and, being random, could not be pre-programmed anyway. They become residual angle of attack, which the closed-loop attitude controller and its load-relief accelerometer feedback handle in real time — within the limits of a bandwidth that is slower than a gust and comparable to a shear traverse.
:::

| --- | --- |
| Jet stream | strongest winds at 10–14 km, coinciding with max-Q; 30–40 m/s mean, 60–75 m/s design envelopes in winter |
