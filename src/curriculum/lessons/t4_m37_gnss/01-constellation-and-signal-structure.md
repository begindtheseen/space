A GNSS receiver is a clock-reading instrument. Every satellite broadcasts, continuously, a signal that says in effect "this bit left me at exactly this time", and the receiver's whole job is to read that time off the signal, compare it with its own clock, and turn the difference into a distance. Everything in this module — the pseudorange, the four-unknown fix, dilution of precision, carrier-phase positioning, the tracking loops that hold lock on a launch vehicle — is built on the structure of that signal, so the structure is where the module starts.

The structure has three layers and you need all three. The carrier is a sine wave at one of a few fixed L-band frequencies. Spread onto it is a pseudorandom ranging code, a sequence of chips that acts as a ruler with marks a few hundred metres apart. Modulated onto the code, far more slowly, is the navigation message: where the satellite is, what its clock is doing, and what time it is. A receiver locks to the carrier to hold the signal, aligns the code to measure the time of transmission, and decodes the message to know where the transmitter was. The next lesson turns those ingredients into a measurement; this one makes sure you know what you are measuring.

## The constellation

GPS is the reference design and the numbers below are its numbers; Galileo, GLONASS and BeiDou differ in detail and are noted at the end. The baseline GPS constellation holds 24 satellites in six orbital planes inclined at $55^\circ$, four satellites per plane, with about 31 operational satellites in orbit at any time filling the extra slots. The orbits are near-circular with semi-major axis $a \approx 26{,}560\,\mathrm{km}$, so the altitude above the WGS-84 equatorial radius $R_E = 6{,}378\,\mathrm{km}$ is about $20{,}180\,\mathrm{km}$.

Two numbers follow from $a$ with $\mu = 3.986 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{m^3/s^2}$. The orbital speed is $\sqrt{\mu/a} = 3{,}874\,\mathrm{m/s}$, and the period is

$$
T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{\mu}} = 43{,}078\,\mathrm{s} = 11.97\,\mathrm{h},
$$

which is within four seconds of half a sidereal day ($86{,}164\,\mathrm{s}/2 = 43{,}082\,\mathrm{s}$). The altitude was chosen for that resonance: the ground track repeats every day, four minutes earlier each day, so the geometry a launch site sees at a given time of day is the same tomorrow. The half-sidereal period also sets the satellites' motion across the sky: their angular rate is twice the Earth's rotation rate, so relative to the ground they move eastward and a pass lasts about five hours from horizon to horizon.

From the satellite, the Earth subtends a half-angle of $\arcsin(R_E/a) = 13.9^\circ$. Keep that angle in mind: it bounds how far a line of sight can tilt from the satellite's nadir and still reach the ground, it is why orbit errors along-track project weakly onto the range, and it is what a spacecraft above the constellation has to work around. The straight-line range to a satellite runs from $20{,}180\,\mathrm{km}$ at the zenith to $\sqrt{a^2 - R_E^2} = 25{,}780\,\mathrm{km}$ at the horizon, so the signal's transit time is between $67.3$ and $86.0\,\mathrm{ms}$. During that transit the satellite moves about $300\,\mathrm{m}$ and the Earth turns through about $5\,\mathrm{\mu rad}$, which is $150\,\mathrm{m}$ at the satellite's radius — both effects appear in the navigation solution later in the module.

::: key
GPS: 24 baseline slots in six planes at $55^\circ$, $a \approx 26{,}560\,\mathrm{km}$ (altitude about $20{,}200\,\mathrm{km}$), speed $3.87\,\mathrm{km/s}$, period $11\,\mathrm{h}\,58\,\mathrm{min}$ — half a sidereal day, so the sky repeats daily. Range $20{,}200$ to $25{,}800\,\mathrm{km}$, transit time $67$ to $86\,\mathrm{ms}$. The Earth subtends $\pm 13.9^\circ$ from the satellite.
:::

## The carriers: L1, L2, L5

Every GPS frequency is an integer multiple of a fundamental $f_0 = 10.23\,\mathrm{MHz}$ generated by the satellite's atomic clock:

| Carrier | Multiple | Frequency | Wavelength |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| L1 | $154 f_0$ | $1575.42\,\mathrm{MHz}$ | $19.03\,\mathrm{cm}$ |
| L2 | $120 f_0$ | $1227.60\,\mathrm{MHz}$ | $24.42\,\mathrm{cm}$ |
| L5 | $115 f_0$ | $1176.45\,\mathrm{MHz}$ | $25.48\,\mathrm{cm}$ |

The wavelength is $\lambda = c/f$ with $c = 299{,}792{,}458\,\mathrm{m/s}$ exactly. The $19\,\mathrm{cm}$ L1 wavelength is the unit of the carrier-phase measurement later in the module; the ratio between the carriers is what the dual-frequency ionospheric correction exploits, because the ionosphere delays each carrier by an amount proportional to $1/f^2$ and the ratio $(f_1/f_2)^2 = (154/120)^2 = 1.647$ is known exactly.

L-band was chosen as a compromise. Lower frequencies suffer more ionospheric delay and need larger antennas; higher frequencies are attenuated more by rain and foliage and are harder to receive through a plume or a plasma sheath. L5, at $1176.45\,\mathrm{MHz}$, sits in a protected aeronautical band and carries a ten-times-faster code, which is why it matters for aviation integrity and for launch-vehicle receivers.

## The ranging codes: C/A and P(Y)

Onto each carrier the satellite modulates a pseudorandom binary sequence by binary phase-shift keying: a chip value of 1 flips the carrier phase by $180^\circ$, a 0 leaves it alone. The sequence is deterministic and known to every receiver, which is the whole point — the receiver generates its own copy and slides it in time until it lines up with the received one. The alignment is the measurement.

The **coarse/acquisition (C/A) code** on L1 has a chipping rate of $1.023\,\mathrm{Mcps}$, one tenth of $f_0$, and a length of $1023$ chips, so it repeats every $1\,\mathrm{ms}$. One chip lasts $1/1.023\,\mathrm{\mu s} = 977.5\,\mathrm{ns}$, and light travels

$$
\frac{c}{1.023 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}} = 293.1\,\mathrm{m}
$$

in that time. That is the chip length: the ruler has marks every $293\,\mathrm{m}$ (usually quoted as $300\,\mathrm{m}$), and a receiver that aligns the code to one per cent of a chip has measured to $3\,\mathrm{m}$. Because the code repeats every millisecond, the code alone tells you the transmit time modulo $1\,\mathrm{ms}$, that is modulo $300\,\mathrm{km}$ of range; the ambiguity is resolved by the navigation message, as described below.

The **precision code, P(Y)**, runs at $10.23\,\mathrm{Mcps}$ with a chip length of $29.3\,\mathrm{m}$ and a period of one week — about $6.19 \times 10^{12}$ chips, a different one-week segment for each satellite. It is broadcast on both L1 and L2. Since 1994 it has been encrypted by an additional code (the "Y" in P(Y)) so that only keyed military receivers can generate it; the modernised civil signals — L2C, L5 and L1C — give civilian receivers an open code on every carrier. On L1, the C/A code rides on the in-phase component and P(Y) on the quadrature component, $3\,\mathrm{dB}$ weaker.

Wide bandwidth buys precision. A code with faster chips has a sharper correlation peak, so the same signal-to-noise ratio gives a smaller timing error and a smaller multipath error. L5 chips at $10.23\,\mathrm{Mcps}$ with a code length of $10{,}230$ chips, still repeating every millisecond; its chip is a tenth the length of C/A, and its multipath performance is correspondingly better.

::: key
L1 at $1575.42\,\mathrm{MHz}$ ($154 \times 10.23\,\mathrm{MHz}$), L2 at $1227.60\,\mathrm{MHz}$, L5 at $1176.45\,\mathrm{MHz}$. The C/A code chips at $1.023\,\mathrm{Mcps}$, is $1023$ chips long, repeats every $1\,\mathrm{ms}$, and one chip is $293\,\mathrm{m}$ (about $300\,\mathrm{m}$) of range. P(Y) chips at $10.23\,\mathrm{Mcps}$ ($29.3\,\mathrm{m}$) and repeats weekly.
:::

## CDMA: how thirty satellites share one frequency

Every GPS satellite transmits on exactly the same L1 frequency, at the same time, and the receiver hears the sum. They are separated by **code-division multiple access**: each satellite has its own C/A code, and the codes are chosen so that a receiver correlating against satellite 7's code sees satellite 7's signal at full strength and every other satellite as a small, bounded residue.

The C/A codes are **Gold codes**, formed by combining the outputs of two ten-stage linear feedback shift registers. One register, G1, is the same for every satellite; the second, G2, is tapped at a satellite-specific pair of stages, and the exclusive-or of the two outputs is the code. Gold codes have the property that the cross-correlation between any two of them, and the autocorrelation of any one at a nonzero shift, takes only three values. For length $1023$ those values are $-65$, $-1$ and $+63$ against a peak of $1023$ — the worst case is $20\log_{10}(65/1023) = -23.9\,\mathrm{dB}$ below the peak. Here is the generator, with the correlation check, in a dozen lines:

```python
import numpy as np

G2_TAPS = {1: (2, 6), 2: (3, 7), 3: (4, 8), 4: (5, 9), 5: (1, 9), 6: (2, 10)}


def ca_code(prn):
    """The 1023-chip GPS C/A Gold code for one satellite, as 0/1 chips."""
    g1 = np.ones(10, int)
    g2 = np.ones(10, int)
    t1, t2 = G2_TAPS[prn]
    chips = np.zeros(1023, int)
    for i in range(1023):
        chips[i] = g1[9] ^ g2[t1 - 1] ^ g2[t2 - 1]
        f1 = g1[2] ^ g1[9]
        f2 = g2[1] ^ g2[2] ^ g2[5] ^ g2[7] ^ g2[8] ^ g2[9]
        g1 = np.roll(g1, 1)
        g1[0] = f1
        g2 = np.roll(g2, 1)
        g2[0] = f2
    return chips


s1 = 1 - 2 * ca_code(1).astype(float)   # 0/1 chips -> +1/-1 symbols
s2 = 1 - 2 * ca_code(2).astype(float)
auto = [s1 @ np.roll(s1, k) for k in range(1, 1023)]
cross = [s1 @ np.roll(s2, k) for k in range(1023)]
print(oct(int("".join(map(str, ca_code(1)[:10])), 2)))   # 0o1440
print(sorted(set(auto)), sorted(set(cross)))
# [-65.0, -1.0, 63.0] [-65.0, -1.0, 63.0]
```

The first ten chips of PRN 1 come out as octal $1440$, which is the value printed in the interface specification, and the correlation values are exactly the three the theory promises. The despreading gain is $10\log_{10}(1023) = 30.1\,\mathrm{dB}$: correlating over one full code period lifts the wanted signal by that much relative to noise and to other satellites' signals.

CDMA has a cost. A $-24\,\mathrm{dB}$ cross-correlation floor is harmless when all the satellites arrive at roughly equal power, as they do on the ground, where the spread is a few decibels. It becomes a hazard when one signal is $20$ or $30\,\mathrm{dB}$ stronger than another, because the strong satellite's cross-correlation can then exceed the weak satellite's own peak and the receiver acquires a phantom. That situation is rare on the ground and routine for a spacecraft above the constellation listening to side lobes, which the space-based lesson takes up. GLONASS avoided the problem entirely by giving each satellite its own frequency (frequency-division multiple access, $1602\,\mathrm{MHz} + k \times 0.5625\,\mathrm{MHz}$), at the price of a more complicated receiver front end and satellite-specific hardware biases; its modernised signals are CDMA like everyone else's.

## The received signal is below the noise

The interface specification guarantees a minimum received C/A power of $-158.5\,\mathrm{dBW}$ at the ground for a $3\,\mathrm{dBi}$ linearly polarised antenna near the horizon — that is $1.4 \times 10^{-16}\,\mathrm{W}$. Thermal noise at a reference temperature of $290\,\mathrm{K}$ has a spectral density of $N_0 = k_B T = 1.38 \times 10^{-23} \times 290 = 4.0 \times 10^{-21}\,\mathrm{W/Hz}$, or $-204.0\,\mathrm{dBW/Hz}$. The quantity every receiver engineer works in is the carrier-to-noise-density ratio

$$
\frac{C}{N_0} = -158.5 - (-204.0) = 45.5\,\mathrm{dB\text{-}Hz}.
$$

The C/A signal occupies about $2\,\mathrm{MHz}$ of bandwidth (its main spectral lobe is $\pm 1.023\,\mathrm{MHz}$ wide), and the noise in that bandwidth is $-204.0 + 10\log_{10}(2.046 \times 10^6) = -140.9\,\mathrm{dBW}$. The signal is therefore $17.6\,\mathrm{dB}$ *below* the noise as it enters the receiver. Nothing in the raw samples looks like a signal at all. Correlating against the known code over $1\,\mathrm{ms}$ collapses the noise bandwidth to $1\,\mathrm{kHz}$ and yields a post-correlation signal-to-noise ratio of $45.5 - 30 = 15.5\,\mathrm{dB}$; integrating coherently for a full $20\,\mathrm{ms}$ data bit gives $28.5\,\mathrm{dB}$. Every performance figure in the tracking-loop lesson — noise on the code measurement, the carrier-to-noise ratio at which lock is lost, the jamming margin — is written in terms of $C/N_0$, and $45\,\mathrm{dB\text{-}Hz}$ is the comfortable open-sky value to remember.

::: example Checking the link budget from the satellite end
A Block IIR satellite radiates about $27\,\mathrm{W}$ of L1 C/A power through an antenna with about $13\,\mathrm{dBi}$ of gain toward the edge of the Earth, an effective isotropic radiated power of $10\log_{10}(27) + 13 = 27.3\,\mathrm{dBW}$. Free-space path loss over a slant range $d = 25{,}000\,\mathrm{km}$ at $\lambda = 0.1903\,\mathrm{m}$ is

$$
L = 20\log_{10}\frac{4\pi d}{\lambda} = 20\log_{10}\frac{4\pi \times 2.5 \times 10^{7}}{0.1903} = 184.4\,\mathrm{dB},
$$

so an isotropic antenna on the ground receives $27.3 - 184.4 = -157.1\,\mathrm{dBW}$. Atmospheric and polarisation losses of a decibel or two bring it to the $-158.5\,\mathrm{dBW}$ specification. The point of the calculation is the number $184\,\mathrm{dB}$: the signal loses eighteen orders of magnitude on the way down, and the received power is about a tenth of a femtowatt. A one-watt jammer a few kilometres away, or a spacecraft trying to hear the same satellite from three times the distance, changes that budget by tens of decibels, which is why both cases get lessons of their own.
:::

## The navigation message

Multiplied onto the code, at $50$ bits per second, is the **navigation message**. Each bit lasts $20\,\mathrm{ms}$ — twenty repetitions of the C/A code, $20{,}460$ chips — and flips the sign of the code for its duration. The receiver, once it has aligned the code, recovers the bits from the sign of its correlator output.

The legacy message (LNAV) is organised in **frames** of $1500$ bits lasting $30\,\mathrm{s}$, each made of five **subframes** of $300$ bits lasting $6\,\mathrm{s}$. Every subframe opens with two words that make the whole system work as a clock:

- the **telemetry word** (TLM), which begins with the fixed eight-bit preamble $10001011$ that the receiver searches for to find the frame boundary;
- the **handover word** (HOW), which carries the **time of week** — the GPS time, in units of $6\,\mathrm{s}$, at which the *next* subframe begins — plus the subframe number and status flags.

The three remaining subframes carry the content, rotated over successive frames:

| Subframe | Content | Refreshed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | GPS week number, satellite health, clock correction polynomial ($a_{f0}, a_{f1}, a_{f2}$, reference time $t_{oc}$), group delay $T_{GD}$ | every frame |
| 2, 3 | Ephemeris: sixteen Keplerian and perturbation parameters ($\sqrt{A}$, $e$, $i_0$, $\Omega_0$, $\omega$, $M_0$, $\Delta n$, $\dot\Omega$, $\dot i$ and six harmonic corrections) with reference time $t_{oe}$ | every frame |
| 4, 5 | Almanac for all satellites, ionospheric (Klobuchar) coefficients, UTC offset and leap seconds, health summaries | one page per frame; 25 frames for the full set |

Because the full almanac cycles through 25 pages, a receiver switched on with no memory needs $25 \times 30\,\mathrm{s} = 12.5\,\mathrm{min}$ to collect it; the clock and ephemeris for a satellite already being tracked arrive within $30\,\mathrm{s}$, or $18\,\mathrm{s}$ if you catch subframe 1 as it begins. The ephemeris is a curve fit to the satellite's predicted orbit, nominally valid for four hours around $t_{oe}$ and normally replaced every two hours; the almanac is a coarse, long-lived version of the same thing for planning which satellites to search. The distinction matters at power-up: with an almanac, a rough position and time, the receiver knows which satellites are up and roughly at what Doppler, and acquisition takes seconds instead of minutes.

The modernised messages — CNAV on L2C and L5, CNAV-2 on L1C — carry the same information in a message-oriented format with forward error correction and higher data rates, but the logic is identical: the signal tells you the time it was transmitted, where the transmitter was, and how the transmitter's clock was wrong.

## Reading the transmit time off the signal

Now the three layers assemble into the thing the next lesson needs. When the receiver has locked to a satellite, it knows, for the sample it is looking at:

1. which subframe it is in and the time of week of that subframe, from the HOW — resolution $6\,\mathrm{s}$;
2. how many bits into the subframe it is — resolution $20\,\mathrm{ms}$;
3. how many code periods into the bit — resolution $1\,\mathrm{ms}$;
4. how many chips into the code period — resolution $977\,\mathrm{ns}$, about $293\,\mathrm{m}$;
5. the fraction of a chip, from the fine alignment of its code replica — resolution a few nanoseconds, a metre or so.

Adding these up gives the **time of transmission** of that sample by the satellite's clock, unambiguously, to nanosecond precision. It is an extraordinary thing: a counter running in a spacecraft $20{,}000\,\mathrm{km}$ away has been read remotely with ten digits of resolution using a signal seventeen decibels under the noise. The receiver also records the **time of reception** by its own clock. The difference, multiplied by $c$, is the pseudorange, and the reasons it is not quite a range are the subject of the next lesson.

::: example From chip count to transmit time
A receiver's channel for PRN 14 reports, at one of its sample epochs: HOW time of week $= 302{,}406\,\mathrm{s}$ for the current subframe; $17$ complete bits since the subframe began; $8$ complete code periods within the current bit; $611$ complete chips within the current code period; and a fine code phase of $0.376$ chip. The transmit time is

$$
t_{tx} = 302{,}406 + 17 \times 0.020 + 8 \times 0.001 + \frac{611.376}{1.023 \times 10^6} = 302{,}406.348\,597\,6\,\mathrm{s}.
$$

The chip term is $611.376 \times 977.5\,\mathrm{ns} = 597.63\,\mathrm{\mu s}$. If the receiver's clock read $302{,}406.421\,\mathrm{s}$ at the same epoch, the apparent travel time is $72.402\,\mathrm{ms}$, and multiplied by $c$ that is $21{,}706\,\mathrm{km}$ — plausibly a satellite at about $60^\circ$ elevation, but the number contains the receiver's clock error too. The fine code phase of $0.376$ chip is the part the receiver's code tracking loop measures; a one-per-cent error in it is $2.9\,\mathrm{m}$.
:::

::: warning
The C/A code repeats every millisecond, so code alignment alone gives the transmit time modulo $1\,\mathrm{ms}$ — a $300\,\mathrm{km}$ ambiguity. Receivers that start computing before they have decoded the handover word resolve it by assuming all travel times lie between $67$ and $86\,\mathrm{ms}$ and choosing the millisecond that fits; a wrong choice produces a fix that is off by exactly $299.79\,\mathrm{km}$ in one satellite's range. A spacecraft receiver, for which travel times are not in that window, cannot use the ground assumption and must decode the message or be told the time.
:::

## Doppler, and where it comes from

The carrier arrives shifted in frequency by the range rate: $f_d = -\dot\rho/\lambda$, where $\dot\rho$ is the rate of change of the range. For a user at rest on the ground, the largest range rate occurs with the satellite on the horizon, where the line of sight makes an angle of $90^\circ - 13.9^\circ = 76.1^\circ$ with the satellite's velocity vector, so

$$
|\dot\rho|_{\max} = 3{,}874\,\mathrm{m/s} \times \cos 76.1^\circ = 930\,\mathrm{m/s}, \qquad |f_d|_{\max} = \frac{930}{0.1903} = 4.9\,\mathrm{kHz}.
$$

The Earth's rotation carries an equatorial user along at $465\,\mathrm{m/s}$, reducing that in most geometries, and the conventional design window for a static receiver is $\pm 5\,\mathrm{kHz}$. The Doppler *rate* is gentle, below $1\,\mathrm{Hz/s}$, because the geometry changes slowly. On a launch vehicle the receiver's own velocity of several kilometres per second adds tens of kilohertz to the window and the Doppler rate rises by orders of magnitude, which is the theme of the launch-vehicle lesson. The code is Doppler-shifted too, by the same fractional amount: the C/A chipping rate is $1575.42/1.023 = 1540$ times lower than the carrier, so $4\,\mathrm{kHz}$ of carrier Doppler is $2.6\,\mathrm{chips/s}$ of code Doppler — a fact the tracking-loop lesson uses to let the carrier loop steer the code loop.

## Other constellations

Galileo flies 24 baseline satellites in three planes at $56^\circ$ inclination and $23{,}222\,\mathrm{km}$ altitude, transmitting E1 (sharing L1's centre frequency), E5a (sharing L5), E5b and E6, all CDMA with longer codes. GLONASS uses three planes at $64.8^\circ$ and $19{,}100\,\mathrm{km}$, with a period of $11\,\mathrm{h}\,16\,\mathrm{min}$, and historically FDMA. BeiDou mixes medium orbits with inclined geosynchronous and geostationary satellites. A modern receiver tracks all of them, which raises the visible count from eight to thirty and transforms the geometry and integrity monitoring discussed later in the module — provided it also carries the small inter-system time offsets as extra unknowns.

::: check
Why does correlating against a satellite's code for one millisecond raise it out of the noise, and by how much? What is the signal-to-noise ratio after correlation for a signal at $C/N_0 = 38\,\mathrm{dB\text{-}Hz}$?
:::

::: answer
The code spreads the signal's power over about $2\,\mathrm{MHz}$, where it sits $17.6\,\mathrm{dB}$ below the thermal noise in that bandwidth. Multiplying by an aligned replica removes the code and concentrates the signal power back into a bandwidth of roughly $1/T$, where $T$ is the integration time, while the noise remains spread; the gain over one code period is $10\log_{10}(1023) = 30.1\,\mathrm{dB}$. Equivalently, the post-correlation signal-to-noise ratio is $C/N_0$ times $T$: at $38\,\mathrm{dB\text{-}Hz}$ and $T = 1\,\mathrm{ms}$ ($-30\,\mathrm{dB\text{-}s}$) it is $8\,\mathrm{dB}$, thin but trackable; integrating over a $20\,\mathrm{ms}$ bit gives $21\,\mathrm{dB}$.
:::

::: check
A receiver has aligned the code and is counting chips but has not yet decoded the handover word. What does it know about the transmit time, and how do ground receivers get away with it?
:::

::: answer
It knows the transmit time modulo $1\,\mathrm{ms}$, because the code repeats every millisecond; in range terms it knows the range modulo $299.79\,\mathrm{km}$. A ground receiver resolves this by assuming the true travel time lies between $67$ and $86\,\mathrm{ms}$, a $19\,\mathrm{ms}$ window that contains exactly one consistent millisecond once the receiver clock is roughly set. The handover word, once decoded, gives the time of week directly and removes the assumption — and a receiver in orbit, whose travel times fall outside that window, needs it.
:::

::: check
Two satellites arrive at a receiver at $C/N_0$ of $48$ and $22\,\mathrm{dB\text{-}Hz}$. Is there a risk in acquiring the weaker one, and what property of the codes decides it?
:::

::: answer
Yes. The Gold codes' cross-correlation is bounded at $-23.9\,\mathrm{dB}$ below the autocorrelation peak. The strong satellite's signal, correlated against the weak satellite's code, produces a false peak at $48 - 23.9 = 24.1\,\mathrm{dB\text{-}Hz}$ equivalent — stronger than the weak satellite's true peak at $22\,\mathrm{dB\text{-}Hz}$. A receiver searching for the weak satellite can lock onto the cross-correlation of the strong one and report a pseudorange that belongs to the wrong satellite. Ground receivers rarely see a $26\,\mathrm{dB}$ spread; a spacecraft on side lobes sees it routinely, and must check candidate acquisitions against the expected Doppler and against the strong satellites' codes.
:::
