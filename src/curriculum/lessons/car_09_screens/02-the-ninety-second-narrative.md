Of everything in this module, the ninety-second narrative is the piece with the widest reach. You will give some version of it at the recruiter screen, again at the start of the technical phone screen when the engineer says *so, tell me about your background*, again at the onsite to five or six people in a row, and, in compressed form, every time you introduce yourself to someone at the company for the rest of the process. It is the single most-reused ninety seconds of the entire job search, and it is the one most easily left unprepared.

It is also, for an engineer who has done real work, the most annoying thing to write. The material is not the problem — you have the material. The problem is that the format punishes everything engineers are trained to do: qualify, enumerate, give context, establish scope, cover the edge cases. Ninety seconds does not have room for any of that, and an attempt to fit it in produces the characteristic failure, which is a narrative that is entirely true, entirely complete, and completely unmemorable.

This lesson gives the structure, the word budget, the ending rule, and a way to tell a good version from a bad one without needing anyone else's opinion.

## Why ninety seconds, and what that is in words

The constraint is not magic and no employer publishes it. It comes from this module's own exercise, which asks you to land between eighty-five and ninety-five seconds, and its value is structural rather than empirical: a budget forces you to cut, and cutting is the entire exercise. An unbounded answer to *tell me about yourself* is not a harder version of the same task, it is a different and much easier one, which is why candidates who have never timed themselves ramble.

Convert the budget into words, because words are what you can edit. Do not take a speaking rate from a book — measure your own. Read two hundred words of ordinary prose aloud at the pace you would actually use on a call, not faster, and time it. That gives you your rate. To see the shape of the answer: someone speaking at a hundred and thirty words a minute has about a hundred and ninety-five words to work with in ninety seconds; at a hundred and fifty, about two hundred and twenty-five. So the target is roughly two hundred words, which is about four short paragraphs, or a dozen sentences. That is much less than it feels like when you are writing it and much less than you will want.

Write to the word count, not to the clock. Then record it and check the clock, because the two diverge: a version that scans as two hundred words on the page will run long if it has three numbers and an acronym in it, since you will slow down to say them clearly. If your recording comes in at a hundred and five seconds, you do not fix it by speaking faster. You fix it by cutting twenty words.

::: key
The ninety-second narrative has four parts: where you started, the through-line that led to GNC, your single strongest piece of evidence, and the specific role family you want. It ends on a program and a technical problem, never on passion or flexibility. Target roughly two hundred words, measured against your own speaking rate, and land it between eighty-five and ninety-five seconds without rushing.
:::

## The four parts

### Where you started

One sentence. Its job is to give the listener a frame to hang everything else on, and its job is not to be interesting. A degree, a first job, a first project — whichever one actually starts the line you are about to draw. If your background is unusual, this is where the unusual fact goes, stated plainly and without defensiveness, because an unexplained gap or a non-obvious degree will otherwise occupy the listener's attention for the rest of the ninety seconds while they try to work it out.

### The through-line

Two or three sentences, and the hardest part to write. A through-line is a *reason* the sequence went the way it did, not the sequence itself. "I did mechanical engineering, then I did some controls work, then I moved into simulation" is a list. "I came in through structures, but the problems I kept being handed were closed-loop ones — I ended up owning the control side of the payload pointing because nobody else wanted the stability analysis, and that is what I have done since" is a through-line: it explains a direction rather than reciting a sequence.

The test is simple. If you could reorder the sentences without the paragraph becoming false, it is a list, not a through-line. Rewrite it.

Be honest here. Careers are not straight, and a through-line is not a claim that yours was — it is a claim that you can see the pattern in it now. A candidate who says "I spent two years in automotive and I do not pretend I planned it, but sensor fusion on a vehicle that has to work every time turned out to be the closest thing to flight software I could have done" has a stronger answer than one who manufactures a childhood dream.

### The single strongest piece of evidence

One thing. Not your best three, not a representative sample — the one artefact you would most want an engineer to interrogate. This is the hook, and the whole narrative is built so that the listener reaches for it.

It should be concrete enough to be checkable and small enough to say in a sentence: what it was, what you did on it, and one specific fact that shows depth. "A six-degree-of-freedom simulation" is not enough; "a six-degree-of-freedom simulation I wrote from scratch, including the Monte Carlo dispersion campaign that found the wind case that broke our controller" is, because the last clause is a fact that only someone who did the work would say.

Whatever you name here, you must be able to survive three questions on it. That is the subject of lesson five, and the two lessons interlock: your narrative points at a line on your résumé, and the résumé-depth interrogation is what happens when the interviewer follows the pointer. Never hook onto something you cannot defend.

### The role family

The last sentence, and the one candidates leave off. It names what you want, specifically enough that a recruiter can route it: a role family from this track's second module, a program or vehicle domain if you know it, and the technical problem you want to be near.

*"I am looking at GNC roles on the vehicle side — ascent and entry guidance specifically, because the part I want to be doing is trajectory design against real dispersions."*

Compare with the two endings that candidates default to. "I am open to anything in GNC" is meant to read as easy to work with; it reads as not knowing what you want, and it transfers the routing decision to someone with less information about you than you have. "I have always been passionate about space" is unfalsifiable, universally true of the applicant pool, and tells the listener nothing that distinguishes you from the person who called before you.

::: warning Ending on flexibility is not the safe option it feels like
Saying you are open to anything feels generous and low-risk. Its actual effect is that the person routing your application must guess, under time pressure, which requisition to check you against — and a guess made from an answer that named nothing will not reliably land on the role you would have been strongest for. Specificity does not close doors at this stage; it opens the right one.
:::

## The test for whether it works

You do not need a career coach to grade this. Play the recording to someone — ideally someone who does not work in aerospace — and ask them to tell you three things back:

1. What discipline you work in.
2. One thing you built or did.
3. Which kind of role you are looking for.

If they can return all three from one listen, the narrative works. If they can return one or two, you know exactly which part to rewrite. If they can return none, the narrative is a list.

Then run the second test, on yourself: read the last sentence alone, out of context. Does it name a program area and a technical problem? If it names a feeling, an attitude, or a willingness, the ending has failed regardless of how good the first eighty seconds were, because the ending is the part the listener acts on.

::: key
Two tests. First: a non-specialist who hears it once should be able to tell you back your discipline, one thing you did, and the role family you want. Second: the final sentence, read alone, must name a program area and a technical problem rather than a feeling or a flexibility.
:::

## Worked narratives

::: example The same candidate, twice
**Interviewer:** Thanks for making the time. To start — tell me a bit about your background.

**Weak version (about a hundred and ninety words, and it says almost nothing):**

"Sure. So I graduated in mechanical engineering, and I have always been really interested in aerospace — I think space is the most exciting thing happening right now. After graduating I joined a company doing precision motion systems, where I worked on a few different projects. I did some mechanical design, some testing, and towards the end I got involved with the control software, which I really enjoyed. I have also done a lot of self-study — I have worked through orbital mechanics, control theory, estimation, a lot of Python, and I have been learning C++. I built some personal projects too, a simulation and a few smaller things. I am a fast learner and I enjoy working on hard problems with good people. I am really excited about the possibility of working somewhere like this, and I am open to whatever GNC role would be the best fit for the team."

**Strong version (about two hundred words, same candidate, same facts):**

"I am a controls engineer, and I came into it sideways. I did mechanical engineering and joined a precision motion company expecting to do hardware, but the interesting problems there all turned out to be closed-loop — the mechanism was fine, the loop was what limited us. I ended up owning the control design for a two-axis pointing stage, which meant identifying the plant from measured frequency response data, designing the compensator, and dealing with a structural resonance at forty hertz that we could not move.

The thing I would most want you to ask me about is a six-degree-of-freedom launch vehicle simulation I wrote from scratch, in Python with a C++ inner loop. It has a full Monte Carlo dispersion campaign, and the reason it was worth building is that it found a wind-shear case where my gain schedule lost margin — I would never have found that analytically.

What I am looking for is GNC on the vehicle side. Ascent guidance and control specifically, because the problem I want to be working on is trajectory and controller design against real dispersions rather than nominal cases."

**What makes the difference:** the two versions describe the same person with the same experience. The weak one is a list of activities, all of them true, none of them ordered by importance — and it ends on flexibility, which is where a routing decision should have been. It contains four adjectives about the candidate ("really interested", "fast learner", "really excited") and no fact that only this candidate could have said.

The strong one opens by naming the discipline in six words, gives a through-line with a reason in it (the loop was the limit, not the mechanism), names one artefact with a checkable detail, and ends on a role family plus a technical problem. Notice the second paragraph's first sentence: it explicitly invites the interrogation. That is not a risk if you have done the work, and it is a very cheap way to steer the next twenty minutes onto ground you have prepared.
:::

::: example Fixing an ending without rewriting the narrative
A candidate has a strong first eighty seconds — clear discipline, a real through-line, a good artefact — and closes with:

"So yeah, that is me. I am really open to where I could be most useful. Obviously I would love to be on something like Starship, but honestly I would be happy anywhere in GNC, I just want to learn from the best people I can."

**Diagnosis:** three separate failures in four lines. "Open to where I could be most useful" hands the routing decision away. "Obviously I would love to be on Starship" names a program but attaches no technical problem to it, so it reads as wanting to be near something famous rather than wanting to solve something. "Happy anywhere in GNC" then withdraws the only specific thing that was said. And "learn from the best people" makes the closing note about what the candidate would receive rather than what they would do.

**Repair, keeping everything else identical:**

"What I am aiming at is GNC on a vehicle with an atmospheric flight phase — entry and landing guidance specifically. The part of that I have actually worked on is the dispersion side, and the part I have not is the real-time implementation, which is the gap I would most want to close in a first year."

**What changed:** the ending now names a role family, a flight regime and a named technical problem; it claims one thing the candidate has done and one thing they have not, which is credible in a way that unbroken confidence is not; and the last clause reframes the learning point as a specific gap rather than a general admiration. It is also shorter than what it replaced. Endings almost always are, once the hedging comes out.
:::

## Variants you should have ready

**The twenty-second version.** Sometimes the interviewer wants a sentence, not a story — you will hear it as *just quickly, what is your background?* Have a two-sentence version: discipline plus best evidence plus role family, nothing else. Practise it separately; it is not the long version delivered faster.

**The engineer's version.** At the technical phone screen, the same narrative can carry one more level of technical detail in the evidence sentence, because the listener can follow it and because it sets up the round. The structure does not change; the vocabulary does.

**The version that gets interrupted.** A good interviewer will cut in at the evidence sentence and start asking about it. This is a success, not a derailment. Do not fight your way back to the ending — answer the question, and land the role-family sentence when the thread comes back to you, or at the end of the call when they ask if there is anything else.

## Check yourself

::: check
A candidate's narrative reads: "I studied aerospace engineering, then I worked at a satellite company doing thermal analysis, then I moved into attitude control, and now I do simulation work." Is this a through-line? Apply the test from this lesson and say what would fix it.
:::

::: answer
It is a list, not a through-line, and the test shows it immediately: the sentences can be reordered without the paragraph becoming false, because nothing in it explains why the sequence went that way. A through-line needs a reason. The fix is to find the actual cause of the direction and state it — for example, that thermal analysis work kept running into the pointing budget, which is what pulled the candidate into attitude control, and that the simulation work exists because the attitude problems could not be settled analytically. Same four facts, now with a direction the listener can follow and remember.
:::

::: check
Why does the lesson insist on one piece of evidence rather than three, given that a candidate with three good projects is objectively stronger than one with a single project?
:::

::: answer
Because the narrative's job is not to establish the total size of your portfolio — the résumé does that — but to give the listener a place to start. Three artefacts in ninety seconds means none of them gets a checkable detail attached, so none is memorable and none invites a question. One artefact with a specific fact in it produces the follow-up you want and steers the next part of the conversation onto ground you have prepared. The other two projects do not disappear; they come up when the interviewer asks what else you have done, and they arrive with more impact after one has already landed.
:::

::: check
You have recorded your narrative and it runs a hundred and eight seconds. You are confident every sentence in it is worth saying. What do you do?
:::

::: answer
Cut roughly twenty-five to thirty words, measured against your own speaking rate, and do not attempt to fix the overrun by speaking faster — a rushed delivery costs more than the content you would have saved. The place to cut is almost never the evidence sentence or the final sentence; it is the opening and the qualifications. Typical savings: the frame sentence, which can usually lose half its length; any adjective about yourself; any clause beginning "which meant that"; and any second example offered in support of a point the first example already made.
:::

::: check
A candidate ends with: "I am looking for a GNC role where I can apply my controls background and keep growing." Grade this ending against the second test in this lesson and rewrite it.
:::

::: answer
It fails. Read alone, the sentence names no program area and no technical problem — "a GNC role" is the whole role family and "keep growing" is a feeling about the candidate's own trajectory rather than a statement about work. A passing rewrite names the family narrowly, attaches a flight domain or program area, and names the problem: for instance, "I am aiming at GNC on the spacecraft side — rendezvous and proximity operations specifically, because the problem I want to be working on is relative navigation with a non-cooperative target." That is actionable by a recruiter and interesting to an engineer, and it is barely longer.
:::

::: check
An interviewer interrupts your narrative twelve seconds in, at your first mention of a project, and asks a detailed question about it. Has the narrative failed?
:::

::: answer
No — it has succeeded early. The narrative exists to produce exactly this: a listener who reaches for something specific. Answer the question properly, at the depth asked. The one thing you still owe yourself is the closing sentence, since the role-family statement is the part that does the routing; land it when the thread returns to you, or when the interviewer asks whether there is anything else you want to cover. Trying to force your way back to the prepared script instead of answering the question is the only way this situation goes wrong.
:::

::: check
Explain why a candidate with a genuinely non-linear career is advised to state the unusual fact plainly in the opening sentence rather than working up to it.
:::

::: answer
Because an unexplained oddity in a background consumes the listener's attention for as long as it goes unexplained. If the degree does not match the field, or there is a visible gap, or the last job was in a different industry, the listener will be quietly working on that question instead of hearing the through-line — and they may resolve it with a worse assumption than the truth. Stating it in the first sentence, without apology, costs about six words and frees the remaining eighty seconds to do their actual job. It also means the through-line that follows reads as an explanation rather than as an evasion.
:::

## Summary

| Part | Length | Job | Failure mode |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Where you started | One sentence | Give the listener a frame; defuse any oddity | Turning it into a biography |
| Through-line | Two or three sentences | Explain the direction, not the sequence | A reorderable list |
| Strongest evidence | One or two sentences | Give one checkable artefact to interrogate | Three artefacts, none memorable |
| Role family | Final sentence | Make the routing decision for them | Ending on flexibility or passion |
| Whole thing | Roughly 200 words, 85–95 seconds | Be repeatable back by a non-specialist | Never timed, never recorded |
