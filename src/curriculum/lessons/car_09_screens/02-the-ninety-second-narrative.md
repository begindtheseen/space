Think about a movie trailer. In about two minutes it has to make you want to see a two-hour film. It cannot show everything, so it shows one great scene, and it ends on the title and the opening date — the part you act on. A trailer that ended on "this movie has a lot of different things in it" would sell no tickets.

Your **background narrative** is a trailer for you: the short spoken answer to *tell me about yourself*, about ninety seconds long. People sometimes call this kind of answer an **[[elevator pitch|elevator-pitch]]**.

You will give some version of it at the recruiter screen, again when the engineer on the technical phone screen says *so, tell me about your background*, again at the onsite to five or six people in a row, and in shorter form every time you meet someone at the company. It is the most-reused ninety seconds of the job search, and the most often unprepared.

It is also annoying to write. The material is not the problem. The format punishes everything engineers are trained to do: qualify, list, give context, cover the edge cases. Ninety seconds has no room for that, and trying to fit it in produces the classic failure: a narrative that is entirely true, entirely complete, and completely forgettable.

This lesson gives you the structure, the word budget, the rule for the ending, and a way to grade it yourself.

## Why ninety seconds, and what that is in words

No employer publishes the ninety-second rule. It comes from this module's own exercise, which asks you to land between eighty-five and ninety-five seconds. Its value does not come from research: a budget forces you to cut, and cutting is the whole exercise. Without a time limit the task gets much easier — which is why candidates who have never timed themselves ramble.

Turn the time budget into a word budget, because words are what you can edit. Do not take a **[[speaking rate|speaking-rate]]** from a book — measure your own. Read two hundred words of ordinary prose aloud at the pace you would really use on a call, not faster, and time it. Divide words by minutes and you have your rate in words per minute.

Ninety seconds is one and a half minutes. So the budget is your rate times 1.5. For someone who speaks at 130 words a minute:

$$
130 \times 1.5 = 195 \text{ words}
$$

At 150 words a minute:

$$
150 \times 1.5 = 225 \text{ words}
$$

So the target is roughly two hundred words — about a dozen sentences. That is much less than you will want.

Write to the word count, then record yourself and check the clock, because the two drift apart. A page of two hundred words runs long if it holds three numbers and an acronym, since you slow down to say those carefully. If the recording comes in at a hundred and five seconds, do not fix it by speaking faster. Fix it by cutting about twenty words: ten seconds over, at 130 words a minute, is $130 \times 10 / 60 \approx 22$ words.

::: key
The ninety-second narrative has four parts: where you started, the through-line that led to GNC, your single strongest piece of evidence, and the specific role family you want. It ends on a program and a technical problem, never on passion or flexibility. Target roughly two hundred words, measured against your own speaking rate, and land it between eighty-five and ninety-five seconds without rushing.
:::

## The four parts

Here are the [[four parts|four-parts]] in order, with a rough size for each.

### Where you started

One sentence. Its job is to give the listener a frame to hang everything else on, not to be interesting: a degree, a first job, a first project — whichever starts the line you are about to draw.

If your background is unusual, the unusual fact goes here, stated plainly and without defensiveness. Otherwise an unexplained gap or an unexpected degree takes up the listener's attention for the rest of the ninety seconds.

### The through-line

Two or three sentences, and the hardest to write. A **[[through-line|through-line]]** is the *reason* your path went the way it did, not the path itself.

"I did mechanical engineering, then some controls work, then simulation" is a list. Compare: "I came in through structures, but the problems I kept being handed were closed-loop ones. I ended up owning the control side of the payload pointing because nobody else wanted the stability analysis, and that is what I have done since." That is a through-line. It explains a direction instead of reciting a sequence.

Here is the test. If you could shuffle the sentences into a different order and the paragraph would still be true, it is a list, not a through-line. Rewrite it.

Be honest here. A through-line does not claim your career was straight — only that you can see the pattern in it now. "I spent two years in automotive and I do not pretend I planned it, but sensor fusion on a vehicle that has to work every time turned out to be the closest thing to flight software I could have done" beats an invented childhood dream.

### The single strongest piece of evidence

One thing — the one **artifact** (a thing you built or produced) you would most want an engineer to question you about. This is the hook the whole narrative is built around.

It should be concrete enough to check and small enough for one sentence: what it was, what you did, and one fact that shows depth. "A six-degree-of-freedom simulation" is not enough. "A six-degree-of-freedom simulation I wrote from scratch, including the **[[Monte Carlo dispersion campaign|monte-carlo]]** that found the wind case that broke our controller" is enough. The last clause is a fact that only someone who did the work would say.

Whatever you name here, you must survive three questions on it. That is lesson five: your narrative points at a line on your résumé, and résumé-depth questioning is what happens when the interviewer follows the pointer. Never hook onto something you cannot defend.

### The role family

The last sentence, and the one candidates leave off. It names what you want, specifically enough that a recruiter can route it. That means a **[[role family|role-families]]** from this track's second module, a program or vehicle area if you know it, and the technical problem you want to be near.

*"I am looking at GNC roles on the vehicle side — ascent and entry guidance specifically, because the part I want to be doing is trajectory design against real dispersions."*

Compare the two endings candidates fall back on. "I am open to anything in GNC" is meant to sound easy to work with. It sounds like not knowing what you want, and hands the routing decision to someone who knows less about you than you do. "I have always been passionate about space" cannot be checked, is true of nearly every applicant, and sets you apart from nobody.

::: warning Ending on flexibility is not the safe option it feels like
Saying you are open to anything feels generous and low-risk. Its actual effect is that the person routing your application must guess, in a hurry, which requisition to check you against — and a guess from an answer that named nothing will not reliably land on the role you would be strongest for. Being specific does not close doors at this stage. It opens the right one.
:::

## The test for whether it works

You do not need a career coach to grade this. Play your recording to someone — ideally someone who does not work in aerospace — and ask them to tell you three things back:

1. What discipline you work in.
2. One thing you built or did.
3. Which kind of role you are looking for.

All three from one listen: it works. One or two: you know which part to rewrite. None: the narrative is a list.

Then run the second test on yourself. Read the last sentence alone. Does it name a program area and a technical problem? If it names a feeling, an attitude or a willingness, the ending has failed, however good the first eighty seconds were — because the ending is the part the listener acts on.

::: key
Two tests. First: a non-specialist who hears it once should be able to tell you back your discipline, one thing you did, and the role family you want. Second: the final sentence, read alone, must name a program area and a technical problem rather than a feeling or a flexibility.
:::

## Worked narratives

::: example The same candidate, twice
**Interviewer:** Thanks for making the time. To start — tell me a bit about your background.

**Weak version (about a hundred and fifty words, and it says almost nothing):**

"Sure. So I graduated in mechanical engineering, and I have always been really interested in aerospace — I think space is the most exciting thing happening right now. After graduating I joined a company doing precision motion systems, where I worked on a few different projects. I did some mechanical design, some testing, and towards the end I got involved with the control software, which I really enjoyed. I have also done a lot of self-study — I have worked through orbital mechanics, control theory, estimation, a lot of Python, and I have been learning C++. I built some personal projects too, a simulation and a few smaller things. I am a fast learner and I enjoy working on hard problems with good people. I am really excited about the possibility of working somewhere like this, and I am open to whatever GNC role would be the best fit for the team."

**Strong version (a little under two hundred words, same candidate, same facts):**

"I am a controls engineer, and I came into it sideways. I did mechanical engineering and joined a precision motion company expecting to do hardware, but the interesting problems there all turned out to be closed-loop — the mechanism was fine, the loop was what limited us. I ended up owning the control design for a two-axis pointing stage, which meant identifying the plant from measured [[frequency response|frequency-response]] data, designing the compensator, and dealing with a structural resonance at forty hertz that we could not move.

The thing I would most want you to ask me about is a six-degree-of-freedom launch vehicle simulation I wrote from scratch, in Python with a C++ inner loop. It has a full Monte Carlo dispersion campaign, and the reason it was worth building is that it found a wind-shear case where my [[gain schedule|gain-schedule]] lost margin — I would never have found that analytically.

What I am looking for is GNC on the vehicle side. Ascent guidance and control specifically, because the problem I want to be working on is trajectory and controller design against real dispersions rather than nominal cases."

**What makes the difference.** The weak one is a list of activities, all true, none ranked by importance, ending on flexibility exactly where a routing decision should have been. It holds adjectives about the candidate ("really interested", "fast learner", "really excited") and no fact that only this candidate could have said.

The strong one names the discipline in its first five words. It gives a through-line with a reason in it: the loop was the limit, not the mechanism. It names one artifact with a checkable detail. It ends on a role family plus a technical problem.

The first sentence of the second paragraph openly invites questions. That is no risk if you did the work, and a very cheap way to steer the next twenty minutes onto ground you have prepared. Sanity check with the first test: discipline (controls), one thing built (the launch simulation), role wanted (ascent guidance and control). All three come back from one listen.
:::

::: example Fixing an ending without rewriting the narrative
A candidate has a strong first eighty seconds — clear discipline, a real through-line, a good artifact — and closes with:

"So yeah, that is me. I am really open to where I could be most useful. Of course I would love to be on something like Starship, but honestly I would be happy anywhere in GNC, I want to learn from the best people I can."

**Diagnosis:** four problems in four lines.

1. "Open to where I could be most useful" hands the routing decision away.
2. "I would love to be on Starship" names a program but attaches no technical problem. It sounds like wanting to be near something famous, not wanting to solve something.
3. "Happy anywhere in GNC" then takes back the only specific thing that was said.
4. "Learn from the best people" makes the closing about what the candidate would receive, not what they would do.

**Repair, keeping everything else identical:**

"What I am aiming at is GNC on a vehicle with an atmospheric flight phase — entry and landing guidance specifically. The part of that I have actually worked on is the dispersion side, and the part I have not is the real-time implementation, which is the gap I would most want to close in a first year."

**What changed.** The ending now names a role family, a flight regime and a technical problem. It claims one thing the candidate has done and one they have not, which is more believable than nonstop confidence, and it turns "learning" into one specific gap. It is also shorter than what it replaced. Endings almost always are, once the hedging comes out.
:::

## Variants you should have ready

**The twenty-second version.** Sometimes you will hear *quickly, what is your background?* Have a two-sentence version: discipline, best evidence, role family, nothing else. Practice it separately; it is not the long version said faster.

**The engineer's version.** At the technical phone screen, the evidence sentence can carry one more level of technical detail, because the listener can follow it and it sets up the round. The structure stays; the vocabulary changes.

**The version that gets interrupted.** A good interviewer will cut in at the evidence sentence. That is a success, not a derailment. Answer the question, and land the role-family sentence when the thread comes back to you, or when they ask at the end if there is anything else.

## Check yourself

::: check
A candidate's narrative reads: "I studied aerospace engineering, then I worked at a satellite company doing thermal analysis, then I moved into attitude control, and now I do simulation work." Is this a through-line? Apply the test from this lesson and say what would fix it.
:::

::: answer
It is a list: you can reorder the sentences and the paragraph stays true, because nothing explains why the path went that way. The fix is to find the real cause of the direction and say it. For example: the thermal analysis work kept running into the pointing budget, which pulled the candidate into attitude control; and the simulation work exists because the attitude problems could not be settled with pencil-and-paper analysis. Same four facts, now with a direction the listener can follow and remember.
:::

::: check
Why does the lesson insist on one piece of evidence rather than three, given that a candidate with three good projects is stronger than one with a single project?
:::

::: answer
Because the narrative's job is not to show the size of your portfolio — the résumé does that — but to give the listener a place to start. Three artifacts in ninety seconds means none gets a checkable detail, so none is memorable and none invites a question. One artifact with a specific fact produces the follow-up you want and steers the conversation onto ground you have prepared. The other two projects come up when the interviewer asks what else you have done, and land harder once the first one has.
:::

::: check
You have recorded your narrative and it runs a hundred and eight seconds. You are confident every sentence in it is worth saying. What do you do?
:::

::: answer
Cut roughly twenty-five to thirty words, measured against your own speaking rate. At 130 words a minute, getting from 108 down to 95 seconds means losing 13 seconds, and $130 \times 13 / 60 \approx 28$ words. Do not speak faster: a rushed delivery costs more than the words you would save. Cut the opening and the qualifications, almost never the evidence or final sentence. Typical savings: half the frame sentence; any adjective about yourself; any clause beginning "which meant that"; any second example backing up a point the first already made.
:::

::: check
A candidate ends with: "I am looking for a GNC role where I can apply my controls background and keep growing." Grade this ending against the second test in this lesson and rewrite it.
:::

::: answer
It fails. Read alone, it names no program area and no technical problem: "a GNC role" is the entire field, and "keep growing" is a feeling about the candidate's own path. A passing rewrite names the family narrowly, attaches a flight domain, and names the problem: "I am aiming at GNC on the spacecraft side — rendezvous and proximity operations specifically, because the problem I want to be working on is relative navigation with a non-cooperative target." A recruiter can act on that, an engineer finds it interesting, and it is barely longer.
:::

::: check
An interviewer interrupts your narrative twelve seconds in, at your first mention of a project, and asks a detailed question about it. Has the narrative failed?
:::

::: answer
No — it has succeeded early. The narrative exists to produce a listener who reaches for something specific. Answer properly, at the depth asked. You still owe yourself the closing sentence, since the role-family statement does the routing: land it when the thread comes back to you, or when the interviewer asks whether there is anything else. The only way this goes wrong is forcing your way back to the script instead of answering.
:::

::: check
Explain why a candidate with a genuinely winding career is advised to state the unusual fact plainly in the opening sentence rather than working up to it.
:::

::: answer
Because an unexplained oddity holds the listener's attention for as long as it stays unexplained. A degree that does not match, a visible gap, a last job in another industry: the listener puzzles over it instead of hearing the through-line, and may settle it with a worse guess than the truth. Stating it first, without apology, costs about six words and frees the other eighty seconds to do their job. It also makes the through-line read as an explanation, not an evasion.
:::

## Summary

| Part | Length | Job | Failure mode |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Where you started | One sentence | Give the listener a frame; defuse any oddity | Turning it into a life story |
| Through-line | Two or three sentences | Explain the direction, not the sequence | A list you could reorder |
| Strongest evidence | One or two sentences | Give one checkable artifact to question | Three artifacts, none memorable |
| Role family | Final sentence | Make the routing decision for them | Ending on flexibility or passion |
| Whole thing | Roughly 200 words, 85–95 seconds | Be repeatable back by a non-specialist | Never timed, never recorded |

::: context elevator-pitch Why an elevator
The name comes from a thought experiment: you step into an elevator with someone important, and you have until the doors open to tell them who you are and what you want. That is somewhere between thirty seconds and two minutes. The point is not the elevator. It is that a short, fixed window forces you to decide what matters most — which is exactly what the ninety-second budget does for your background.
:::

::: context through-line A word borrowed from the theater
**Through-line** comes from acting. The Russian director Konstantin Stanislavski taught actors to find the "through-line of action" — the unbroken line that carries a character through every scene toward one main aim, so that each moment makes sense as a step along it. Your career narrative borrows the idea: the jobs and projects are the scenes, and the through-line is what explains why each one led to the next.
:::

::: context monte-carlo Thousands of flights with the dice rolled
A **Monte Carlo** run is named after the casino in Monaco, because it relies on chance. You run the same simulation thousands of times, and each time you nudge the inputs at random within realistic limits: a bit more wind, a slightly heavier stage, a sensor that reads a little high. The spread of those nudges is the **dispersion**. A **campaign** is the whole batch. It finds the rare bad combination that a single "nominal" run never meets — like the wind case in this example.
:::

::: context role-families The map from module two
A **role family** is a group of related jobs that share skills — for example guidance and trajectory, navigation and state estimation, attitude control for satellites, or the software and simulation work around them. This track's second module drew that map. Naming one in your last sentence tells the recruiter which part of the map to route you to.
:::

::: context frequency-response Shaking the system to learn it
**Frequency response** is what you get by wiggling a system at many speeds and measuring how strongly, and how late, it answers each one. **Hertz** (Hz) means cycles per second, so a resonance at 40 Hz is a speed of wiggling — forty times a second — where the structure responds far more than elsewhere, like a wine glass at one pitch. You will learn to read these plots in the frequency-domain lessons later in the course.
:::

::: context gain-schedule Different settings for different moments
A controller has **gains**: numbers that set how hard it reacts to an error. A rocket climbing through thick air, then thin air, needs different gains at different moments, so engineers store a table of them keyed to speed or altitude. That table is a **gain schedule**. **Margin** is how far the controller is from going unstable; "lost margin" means the wind case pushed it uncomfortably close.
:::
