The second call is a different animal from the first. The person on the other end is an engineer on the team or the hiring manager, the material is your actual work and the fundamentals underneath it, and this curriculum's source material reports the standard length as thirty minutes, with some reports of sixty to ninety. There is no whiteboard, no shared screen you can rely on, and no visual aid of any kind unless you arrange one.

That last constraint is the defining one, and it is worth sitting with before we get to content. Everything you know how to explain, you learned to explain with a surface: a board, a notebook, a plot on a screen. Strip that away and much of what made those explanations work does not survive the move to audio — not because the speaker has stopped knowing the answer, but because the explanation depended on a gesture at a diagram the listener cannot see. The next few lessons are about closing that gap. This one is about the frame it happens in: who runs the call, what it is for, how the thirty minutes actually get spent, and what you can arrange in advance.

## Who runs it, and what they are positioned to assess

The source reports two possibilities: an engineer on the team, or the hiring manager. It is worth asking the recruiter which, because the two are positioned to assess different things and the difference changes what you should prepare.

An **engineer on the team** is, in effect, answering one question: *would I want to work on my problem with this person?* That makes depth on your own work and fluency on fundamentals the whole of the material, and it makes your questions at the end genuinely informative — an engineer will tell you what the work is actually like, because they are living in it. It also means the questions will tend to come from what that engineer personally works on, which is why knowing which program and team the requisition sits in is useful preparation rather than trivia.

A **hiring manager** is answering a wider question: *does this person fill a gap on this team, at what level, and can they be trusted with a piece of it?* The technical content may be identical, but the same answer gets read against scope and ownership as well as correctness. The hiring manager is also the person who can tell you what the team's actual staffing problem is, which is the single most useful thing you can learn at this stage.

You do not need two different preparations. You need one preparation and the awareness of which frame your answers are landing in.

::: key
The technical phone screen is reported at thirty minutes, sometimes sixty to ninety, run by an engineer on the team or the hiring manager. It covers résumé depth plus fundamental engineering questions tied to the role. There is no whiteboard, so structure has to come from your voice.
:::

## Thirty minutes, spent

Here is a planning model. It is arithmetic, not a report: nobody has told this curriculum how any particular interviewer allocates a call, and interviewers differ enormously. But a budget you constructed yourself is still far better than no sense of time at all, and the conclusion it produces is robust to the details.

Assume thirty minutes. Two go to greetings and the interviewer explaining the format. Two more go to your background narrative, in its engineer-facing form. Five go to your questions at the end, because a good interviewer will leave room for them and you should assume they will. That leaves twenty-one minutes for the substance. Thirty minus two minus two minus five is twenty-one.

Now divide. At four minutes per answer that is five questions; at five minutes per answer it is four. So the technical core of a thirty-minute screen is on the order of four or five substantive questions, and **each of your answers has a budget of roughly four to five minutes.**

That number is the single most useful thing in this lesson, because it converts an abstract instruction — *be concise* — into a constraint you can rehearse against. This module's own verbal-derivation exercise sets the same bar: every answer in under five minutes. An answer that runs eleven minutes has not merely gone on too long; it has consumed the airtime of two other questions, which means the interviewer leaves the call with evidence about half as many things as they planned to probe. You do not get graded on the questions that were not asked, but you do not get credit for them either.

If the call turns out to be sixty or ninety minutes, the budget loosens and you will feel it — but plan for the shorter one, because a candidate prepared for thirty minutes handles ninety comfortably and the reverse is not true.

::: warning Long answers are not read as thoroughness
The instinct, under pressure, is that more detail demonstrates more knowledge. On a timed call it demonstrates something else: that you cannot tell which parts of your own answer matter. Selecting the four things worth saying out of the twenty you know is itself the skill being examined — it is the same skill as writing a readable review package or a test report someone else will act on.
:::

## What you can arrange in advance

Very little about this call is under your control, so take the parts that are.

**Find out the format.** Ask the recruiter: how long is it, who is it with, is it a phone call or a video call, and is there a shared document or editor. These are ordinary scheduling questions and you are entitled to the answers. Knowing whether you will have a shared editor changes your preparation substantially — with one, you can write an equation; without one, you must say it.

**Set up the room.** Somewhere quiet, with a connection you have tested. A headset, so your hands are free and your voice is steady. Water. A door that closes.

**Put four things on the desk.** Your résumé, printed, so you can see the exact line the interviewer is reading from. The job posting. A page of your own questions. And a blank page.

The blank page is the important one. You should write while you think, even though nobody can see it — not to show your work, but because the act of writing a variable down keeps you from losing track of it mid-sentence. The failure mode on a voice-only technical question is not being unable to do the derivation; it is being four steps in and having forgotten whether you defined the error as measured minus commanded or the other way round.

**Do not put a script on the desk.** A read answer is audible, every time. Notes are fine — a few words per topic, to trigger a structure you have practised. Sentences are not.

::: key
Ask the recruiter four things before the call: how long, with whom, what medium, and whether there is a shared editor. Then set up a quiet room with a headset, and put your printed résumé, the posting, your questions and a blank page on the desk. Write while you think even though no one can see it.
:::

## One legitimate move at the top of the call

If you are unsure what tools you have, ask in the first minute. It takes eight seconds and it is not a weakness:

> "Before we start — would it help if I typed anything into a shared document when we get to the technical part, or would you rather I just talk it through?"

Either answer is useful. If there is a shared editor, you have just gained the ability to write an equation and to leave a visible artefact of your reasoning. If there is not, you have confirmed the constraint rather than discovering it mid-derivation, and you can set up your answers accordingly.

What this question is not is a request for accommodation. Asked once, at the start, in a sentence, it reads as someone arranging their workspace. Asked in the middle of a question you are struggling with, it reads as a stall.

## The two halves of the material

Every technical screen in this module's source material is described the same way: résumé depth plus fundamental engineering questions tied to the role. Treat them as two separate preparations, because they fail in different ways.

**Résumé depth** fails when you wrote something aspirationally. The remedy is the interrogation exercise in lesson five, and it is mechanical: every line, three questions deep, or the line comes off.

**Fundamentals** fail when you know the material in written form but have never produced it out loud. The remedy is lessons six through ten, and it is also mechanical: say the derivations, out loud, on a timer, with no surface to write on.

Neither remedy involves learning new material. This is the characteristic feature of phone-screen preparation for someone who has worked through a technical curriculum: the gap is almost never knowledge, it is retrieval under time pressure in a medium you have never practised in.

::: example The engineer's opening, two ways
**Interviewer:** Hi — so I am on the GNC team here, I work mostly on the ascent side. Maybe start by giving me a quick tour of your background?

**Weak answer:** "Sure, thanks for taking the time. So I graduated in 2019 with a mechanical engineering degree — actually I started in physics and switched after my second year, which was mostly because of a dynamics course I took. Then I worked at a company doing precision motion systems for about two years, where I was on a team of about eight, doing a mix of mechanical design and testing initially, and then I got more into the control side. After that I spent about a year doing contract work, some of which was aerospace-adjacent, and in the meantime I have been working through a lot of the GNC material on my own — orbital mechanics, control theory, estimation. And now I am looking to move into the industry properly."

**Strong answer:** "I am a controls engineer — came in through mechanical, but the work I have owned for the last two years is closed-loop. Most relevant to you: I designed the control for a two-axis pointing stage where the limit was a structural mode at forty hertz I could not move, so the whole design was about notching it and keeping enough margin at crossover. Alongside that I have built a six-degree-of-freedom launch vehicle sim with a Monte Carlo dispersion campaign, which is where my ascent interest comes from. Happy to go deep on either."

**What makes the difference:** the weak version is chronological and evenly weighted, which means the interviewer — who has twenty-one minutes of substance to get through — has to do the work of finding the relevant part themselves. It also spends its best seconds on a degree switch from years ago, mentions a team size that carries no information, and uses "aerospace-adjacent" and "a lot of the GNC material", both of which are hedges that invite a harder probe than a specific claim would.

The strong version is ordered by relevance rather than by date, names the discipline immediately, and picks the one project that connects to what this interviewer just said they work on. The forty-hertz detail is doing a lot of work: it is a specific, checkable fact that signals the candidate actually did the analysis. And the closing sentence hands the interviewer a choice of two prepared threads, which is a quiet way of steering the next twenty minutes without appearing to.
:::

::: example The same answer at eleven minutes and at four
**Interviewer:** You mentioned the pointing stage. How did you identify the plant?

**The eleven-minute answer** opens with the history of the project, explains why the previous approach was inadequate, describes the test setup including the fixture, recounts a problem with the shaker, explains what a frequency response function is, describes the excitation signal and why a chirp was chosen over white noise, mentions two alternatives that were considered and rejected, gets to the measured response at around minute seven, describes the fitting procedure, and finishes with the resulting model. Everything in it is true and competent. By the time it ends, two of the interviewer's planned questions no longer exist.

**The four-minute answer:**

"Swept-sine excitation through the drive amplifier, measuring the encoder output — so I got the transfer function from commanded torque to measured angle directly. I ran it from about one hertz to two hundred, log-spaced, with enough averaging to get coherence above about 0.9 over the band I cared about. Below coherence 0.9 I did not trust the data and I say so in the report.

What came out was a rigid-body double integrator with a lightly damped mode at forty hertz. I fitted that as a second-order pair rather than doing a full modal fit, because I only needed it accurate near crossover.

The thing I would flag is that the mode moved by a couple of hertz between the two units we tested, so I designed the notch wide rather than deep. If you want, I can go into the margin argument for that."

**What makes the difference:** the second answer is the same work. It starts at the answer rather than at the beginning of the story, gives three specific facts — the excitation, the coherence threshold, the model order — that only someone who did it would produce, names one judgment call and the reason for it, and closes by offering the next level of depth instead of taking it unasked.

That last move is the important one. Offering depth rather than delivering it unprompted keeps the interviewer in control of their own time, and it almost always produces a follow-up, which is exactly what you want: a follow-up means the interviewer is spending their scarce minutes on ground you chose.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
Construct the time budget for a thirty-minute screen and state the conclusion it produces about individual answers. Be explicit about which parts are assumption and which are arithmetic.
:::

::: answer
The assumptions are the allocations: roughly two minutes for greetings and format, two for your background narrative, and five for your questions at the end. Those are planning choices, not reported facts about any employer. The arithmetic is then fixed: thirty minus two minus two minus five leaves twenty-one minutes of substance, which at four to five minutes per answer is four or five substantive questions. The conclusion is that each answer has a budget of roughly four to five minutes, which is the same bar this module's verbal-derivation exercise sets.
:::

::: check
Why does this lesson tell you to write on a blank page during a voice-only call, given that the interviewer cannot see anything you write?
:::

::: answer
Because the writing is for you, not for them. The characteristic failure on a voice-only technical question is not an inability to do the derivation but losing track of your own definitions partway through — forgetting whether the error was defined as measurement minus command, which sign convention you chose, or what you called the inertia. Writing each variable down as you name it keeps those fixed while your attention is on the argument and on speaking clearly. It costs nothing and it prevents the most common way a good answer goes wrong.
:::

::: check
An interviewer introduces themselves as the hiring manager rather than an engineer on the team. What, if anything, should change about your technical answers, and what should change about your questions?
:::

::: answer
The technical answers should not change: correctness and fluency are assessed the same way, and you should not attempt to pitch differently to a manager. What is worth knowing is that the same answer will also be read against scope and ownership — what you personally decided, as against what the team did — so being precise about your own role in the work matters more than usual. Your questions should change, because the hiring manager can answer things an engineer often cannot: what the team's actual staffing gap is, how the role is scoped, and what the first six months would look like.
:::

::: check
A candidate asks, eighteen minutes into a difficult question, whether they could use a shared document to write something down. Why does this land differently from asking the same thing in the first minute?
:::

::: answer
Because the timing changes what the request appears to be about. Asked at the top of the call, it is someone arranging their workspace before starting, and it is answered in one sentence by either side. Asked in the middle of a question that is going badly, it arrives as a response to difficulty, which makes it read as a stall regardless of intent — and it also costs a minute of an answer that is already short of time. The fix is to have resolved the question before the call, by asking the recruiter what medium the interview uses.
:::

::: check
Explain why preparation for this screen, for someone who has worked through a technical curriculum, is usually not about learning new material.
:::

::: answer
Because the two halves of the screen fail for reasons that are not knowledge gaps. Résumé depth fails when a line was written aspirationally rather than accurately, and the remedy is editing the résumé and rehearsing each surviving line three questions deep. Fundamentals fail when material that is solid in written form has never been produced out loud, under time pressure, with no surface to write on — a medium most engineers have never practised in. Both remedies are rehearsal, not study, which is why the exercises in this module are all recordings and timers rather than reading.
:::
