Think about explaining how to get to your house. Standing next to a friend with a map, it is easy: you point. "Here, then left, then this street." Now do it over the phone. "This street" means nothing. You have to say the names, the turns, the order, and how the listener will know they went wrong. The knowledge is the same. The way you share it has to change completely.

That is the second call in a hiring process. After the recruiter screen comes the **technical phone screen** — a call where someone who does the actual work checks whether you can do it too. The person on the other end is an engineer on the team or the **[[hiring manager|hiring-manager]]**, the boss who will lead the team you would join. The material is your own past work and the engineering basics underneath it. This curriculum's source material reports the usual length as thirty minutes, with some reports of sixty to ninety.

And there is no whiteboard — no shared screen you can count on, no drawing, no pointing, unless you arrange something in advance. That missing surface defines this call. Every explanation you have ever given leaned partly on a board, a notebook or a plot, and over audio that pointing is lost — not because you stopped knowing the answer, but because the listener cannot see the picture. The next few lessons close that gap. This one sets the frame: who runs the call, what it is for, how the thirty minutes get spent, and what you can set up ahead of time.

## Who runs it, and what they are judging

The source reports two possibilities: an engineer on the team, or the hiring manager. It is worth asking the recruiter which one, because they are placed to judge different things.

An **engineer on the team** is really answering one question: *would I want to work on my problem with this person?* So depth on your own work and ease with the fundamentals are the whole of the material. Your questions at the end are worth a lot here, because an engineer lives in the work and will tell you what it is really like. Their questions will also tend to come from what they personally work on. That is why knowing which program and team the job opening — the **[[requisition|requisition]]** — sits in is useful preparation, not trivia.

A **hiring manager** is answering a wider question: *does this person fill a gap on this team, at what level, and can they be trusted with a piece of it?* The technical questions may be exactly the same. But your answer gets read against **scope** (how big a piece you handled) and **ownership** (what you personally decided), as well as correctness. The hiring manager is also the one person who can tell you what the team's real staffing problem is — the single most useful thing you can learn at this stage.

One preparation covers both, as long as you know which frame your answers land in.

::: key
The technical phone screen is reported at thirty minutes, sometimes sixty to ninety, run by an engineer on the team or the hiring manager. It covers resume depth plus fundamental engineering questions tied to the role. There is no whiteboard, so structure has to come from your voice.
:::

## Thirty minutes, spent

Think of a thirty-minute call like a thirty-dollar allowance: if you do not plan where it goes, it is gone before you buy the thing you wanted. Here is a planning model. It is arithmetic, not a report. Nobody has told this curriculum how any particular interviewer splits a call, and interviewers differ enormously. But a **[[time budget|time-budget]]** you built yourself beats having no sense of time at all, and the conclusion does not depend much on the details.

::: example Building the thirty-minute budget
Assume the call is $30$ minutes. Now make three planning choices:

- $2$ minutes for greetings and the interviewer explaining the format;
- $2$ minutes for your background story, in its engineer-facing form;
- $5$ minutes for your questions at the end, because a good interviewer leaves room for them and you should assume they will.

**Step 1 — subtract the fixed pieces.** Take those out of the thirty:

$$
30 - 2 - 2 - 5 = 21 \text{ minutes of substance.}
$$

**Step 2 — divide by an answer length.** If each answer takes $4$ minutes, $21 \div 4 = 5.25$, so about five questions fit. If each takes $5$ minutes, $21 \div 5 = 4.2$, so about four fit.

**Result:** the technical core of a thirty-minute screen is about four or five real questions, and **each of your answers has a budget of roughly four to five minutes.**

**Sanity check:** five answers of four minutes is $20$ minutes, and four of five minutes is $20$ minutes too. Both fit inside $21$ with a minute to spare, so the budget is consistent.
:::

That four-to-five-minute number turns a vague instruction — *be concise* — into a limit you can practice against with a timer. This module's own verbal-derivation exercise sets the same bar: every answer in under five minutes.

An eleven-minute answer eats the airtime of about two other questions. The interviewer leaves the call with evidence on only about half the things they planned to probe. You are not marked down for questions that were never asked — but you get no credit for them either.

If the call turns out to be sixty or ninety minutes, the budget loosens. Plan for the shorter call anyway. Someone prepared for thirty minutes handles ninety comfortably. The reverse is not true.

::: warning Long answers are not read as thoroughness
Under pressure, it feels like more detail proves more knowledge. On a timed call it proves something else: that you cannot tell which parts of your own answer matter. Picking the four things worth saying out of the twenty you know is itself the skill being tested. It is the same skill as writing a readable **[[review package|review-package]]** or a test report someone else will act on.
:::

## What you can set up in advance

Little about this call is under your control, so take the parts that are.

**Find out the format.** Ask the recruiter four things: how long is it, who is it with, is it a phone call or a video call, and is there a **[[shared editor|shared-editor]]** — a document you can both type into during the call. These are ordinary scheduling questions. The last one changes your preparation the most. With a shared editor, you can write an equation. Without one, you must say it.

**Set up the room.** Somewhere quiet, with a tested connection. A headset, so your hands are free. Water. A door that closes.

**Put four things on the desk.** Your resume, printed, so you can see the exact line the interviewer is reading from. The job posting. A page of your own questions. And a blank page.

The blank page matters most. You should write while you think, even though nobody can see it. Writing a variable down stops you from losing track of it halfway through a sentence. The usual way a voice-only technical answer fails is not that you cannot do the math. It is being four steps in and forgetting whether you defined the error as *measured minus commanded* or the other way around. The page holds that for you.

**Do not put a script on the desk.** A read-out answer always sounds read out. A few words per topic, to trigger a practiced structure, are fine. Full sentences are not.

::: key
Ask the recruiter four things before the call: how long, with whom, what medium, and whether there is a shared editor. Then set up a quiet room with a headset, and put your printed resume, the posting, your questions and a blank page on the desk. Write while you think even though no one can see it.
:::

## One fair move at the top of the call

If you are still unsure what tools you have, ask in the first minute. It takes about eight seconds, and it is not a sign of weakness:

> "Before we start — would it help if I typed anything into a shared document when we get to the technical part, or would you rather I talk it through?"

Either answer helps. If there is a shared editor, you have gained a place to write an equation. If there is not, you have confirmed the limit up front instead of discovering it in the middle of a derivation.

Asked once, at the start, in one sentence, it sounds like someone arranging their desk before work. Asked in the middle of a question you are struggling with, it sounds like a stall.

## The two halves of the material

Every technical screen in this module's source material is described the same way: resume depth plus fundamental engineering questions tied to the role. Prepare for them separately, because they fail in different ways.

**Resume depth** fails when you wrote something **aspirationally** — describing what you hoped you did, or were near, instead of what you did. The fix is the three-questions-deep drill in lesson five, and it is mechanical: every line, three questions deep, or the line comes off.

**Fundamentals** fail when you know the material on paper but have never said it out loud. The fix is lessons six through ten, and it is also mechanical: say the derivations out loud, on a timer, with nothing to write on.

Neither fix involves learning new material. For someone who has worked through a course like this one, the gap is almost never knowledge. It is pulling that knowledge out fast, under time pressure, in a medium you have never practiced in.

::: example The engineer's opening, two ways
**Interviewer:** Hi — so I am on the GNC team here, I work mostly on the ascent side. Maybe start by giving me a quick tour of your background?

**Weak answer:** "Sure, thanks for taking the time. So I graduated in 2019 with a mechanical engineering degree — actually I started in physics and switched after my second year, which was mostly because of a dynamics course I took. Then I worked at a company doing precision motion systems for about two years, where I was on a team of about eight, doing a mix of mechanical design and testing initially, and then I got more into the control side. After that I spent about a year doing contract work, some of which was aerospace-adjacent, and in the meantime I have been working through a lot of the GNC material on my own — orbital mechanics, control theory, estimation. And now I am looking to move into the industry properly."

**Strong answer:** "I am a controls engineer — came in through mechanical, but the work I have owned for the last two years is closed-loop. Most relevant to you: I designed the control for a two-axis pointing stage where the limit was a [[structural mode at forty hertz|structural-mode]] I could not move, so the whole design was about notching it and keeping enough margin at crossover. Alongside that I have built a six-degree-of-freedom launch vehicle sim with a [[Monte Carlo dispersion campaign|monte-carlo]], which is where my ascent interest comes from. Happy to go deep on either."

**What makes the difference.** The weak version goes in date order and gives every year equal weight. That means the interviewer — who has about twenty-one minutes of substance to get through — has to dig out the relevant part themselves. It spends its best seconds on an old degree switch, mentions a team size that tells the listener nothing, and uses "aerospace-adjacent" and "a lot of the GNC material". Both are **hedges** — soft, blurry phrases — and a blurry claim invites a harder probe than a sharp one.

The strong version is ordered by relevance, not by date. It names the discipline in the first sentence and picks the one project that connects to what this interviewer said they work on. The forty-hertz detail is a specific, checkable fact that signals the candidate really did the analysis. The last sentence offers two prepared threads, which quietly steers the next twenty minutes.

**Sanity check:** read the strong answer aloud. It takes about thirty seconds — well inside the two minutes the budget gave the background.
:::

::: example The same answer at eleven minutes and at four
**Interviewer:** You mentioned the pointing stage. How did you identify the plant?

(To **identify the plant** means to measure how the machine being controlled — the "plant" — responds to a push, and turn that into a math model.)

**The eleven-minute answer** opens with the history of the project. It explains why the old approach was not good enough, describes the test setup including the fixture, tells the story of a problem with the shaker, explains what a frequency response function is, describes the test signal and why a chirp was chosen over white noise, mentions two alternatives that were rejected, reaches the measured response around minute seven, describes the fitting, and finishes with the model. Everything in it is true and competent. By the time it ends, two of the interviewer's planned questions no longer exist.

**The four-minute answer:**

"[[Swept-sine|swept-sine]] excitation through the drive amplifier, measuring the encoder output — so I got the transfer function from commanded torque to measured angle directly. I ran it from about one hertz to two hundred, log-spaced, with enough averaging to get [[coherence|coherence]] above about 0.9 over the band I cared about. Below coherence 0.9 I did not trust the data, and I say so in the report.

What came out was a rigid-body [[double integrator|double-integrator]] with a lightly damped mode at forty hertz. I fitted that as a second-order pair rather than doing a full modal fit, because I only needed it accurate near crossover.

The thing I would flag is that the mode moved by a couple of hertz between the two units we tested, so I designed the notch wide rather than deep. If you want, I can go into the margin argument for that."

**What makes the difference.** The second answer describes the same work. But it starts at the answer, not at the beginning of the story. It gives three specific facts — the test signal, the coherence threshold, the model order — that only someone who did the work would produce. It names one judgment call and the reason for it. And it closes by *offering* the next level of depth instead of launching into it unasked.

That last move matters most. Offering depth keeps the interviewer in control of their own time, and it almost always produces a follow-up question. That is what you want: a follow-up means the interviewer is spending their scarce minutes on ground you chose.

**Sanity check:** four minutes is inside the four-to-five-minute budget from the first example. Eleven minutes is more than double it.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
Build the time budget for a thirty-minute screen and state what it tells you about each answer. Say which parts are assumptions and which are arithmetic.
:::

::: answer
The assumptions are the allocations: about two minutes for greetings and format, two for your background narrative, and five for your questions at the end. Those are planning choices, not reported facts about any employer.

The arithmetic then follows: $30 - 2 - 2 - 5 = 21$ minutes of substance. At four to five minutes per answer, $21 \div 4 \approx 5$ and $21 \div 5 \approx 4$, so four or five real questions.

The conclusion: each answer has a budget of roughly four to five minutes — the same bar this module's verbal-derivation exercise sets.
:::

::: check
Why does this lesson tell you to write on a blank page during a voice-only call, when the interviewer cannot see anything you write?
:::

::: answer
Because the writing is for you, not for them. The usual failure on a voice-only technical question is not being unable to do the derivation. It is losing track of your own definitions partway through — forgetting whether the error was measurement minus command, which sign convention you chose, or what letter you gave the inertia. Writing each variable down as you name it holds those fixed while your attention is on the argument and on speaking clearly. It costs nothing and prevents the most common way a good answer goes wrong.
:::

::: check
The interviewer introduces themselves as the hiring manager, not an engineer on the team. What should change about your technical answers, and what should change about your questions?
:::

::: answer
The technical answers should not change. Correctness and fluency are judged the same way, and you should not try to pitch differently to a manager. What is worth knowing is that the same answer will also be read against scope and ownership — what you personally decided, versus what the team did — so being precise about your own role matters more than usual.

Your questions should change, because a hiring manager can answer things an engineer often cannot: what the team's real staffing gap is, how the role is scoped, and what the first six months would look like.
:::

::: check
Eighteen minutes into a difficult question, a candidate asks whether they could use a shared document to write something down. Why does this land differently from asking the same thing in the first minute?
:::

::: answer
Because the timing changes what the request seems to be about. At the top of the call, it is someone arranging their workspace before starting, and either side answers it in one sentence. In the middle of a question that is going badly, it arrives as a reaction to difficulty, so it reads as a stall whatever the intent. It also costs a minute of an answer that is already short of time. The fix is to settle it before the call, by asking the recruiter what medium the interview uses.
:::

::: check
Explain why preparing for this screen, for someone who has worked through a technical course, is usually not about learning new material.
:::

::: answer
Because both halves of the screen fail for reasons that are not knowledge gaps. Resume depth fails when a line was written aspirationally rather than accurately; the fix is editing the resume and rehearsing each surviving line three questions deep. Fundamentals fail when material that is solid on paper has never been said out loud, under time pressure, with nothing to write on — a medium most engineers have never practiced in. Both fixes are rehearsal, not study. That is why this module's exercises are recordings and timers rather than reading.
:::

::: context review-package The same skill at work
In aerospace, big design decisions go through **design reviews**: meetings where other engineers examine the work before it moves on. The written material sent ahead of time is the review package. Reviewers are busy, and a package that buries the one risky decision under forty pages of routine detail is a bad package, even if every page is right. Choosing what to say first is a skill you will use every week of an engineering job, which is why interviewers watch for it on a call.
:::

::: context shared-editor What a shared editor looks like
A shared editor is any document both people can type into at the same time and see live — a shared online document, or a coding pad that some companies use for interviews. On a call with one, you can type $\theta$ or "e = measured − commanded" and both of you can see it. Without one, every symbol exists only in the air, so you must name it clearly and repeat it when you use it again. Asking which kind of call it is costs nothing.
:::

::: context structural-mode Why a structure has a note it likes
Tap a wine glass and it rings at one pitch. Every solid structure has a few frequencies it likes to shake at, called **structural modes**. A mode at forty hertz means the stage flexes back and forth forty times a second if something pushes it at that rate. If the controller pushes near that frequency, the structure rings instead of moving where it was told. A **notch filter** tells the controller to ignore signals right around that frequency. Rockets meet this too: their long thin bodies bend, and the flight controller must not excite those bending modes.
:::

::: context monte-carlo Running the flight thousands of times
A **Monte Carlo** campaign runs the same simulation thousands of times, each time with the uncertain things — winds, engine thrust, sensor errors — nudged a little at random. The spread of results, called the **dispersion**, shows how the design behaves in bad luck as well as good. The name comes from the famous casino in Monaco, because the method runs on random numbers like a game of chance. You build one later in the verification part of the course.
:::

::: context swept-sine Asking a machine one note at a time
To learn how a machine responds, engineers shake it with a smooth wave whose frequency slowly rises — low notes first, then higher ones. That is a **swept sine**, and a fast sweep is often called a **chirp**, because as sound it goes up like a bird call. At each frequency you record how big the response is and how late it arrives.

::: context coherence How much to trust each point
**Coherence** is a number from 0 to 1 that says how much of the measured output is explained by the input you applied, frequency by frequency. Near 1, the response is clean and the data can be trusted. Lower, and noise or something else is mixed in. Setting a threshold — here about 0.9 — and saying out loud that you ignored data below it is exactly the kind of detail that shows an interviewer you ran the test yourself.
:::

::: context double-integrator The simplest thing you can control
A **double integrator** is an object whose acceleration is set directly by the push you give it — like a hockey puck on perfect ice. Push, and its speed changes; its speed changes where it is. Two steps ("integrations") from push to position give it the name. A pointing stage, a spacecraft turning in space, and a rocket's attitude all look like this at first glance. Lesson eight of this module designs a controller for one, out loud.
:::
