Picture a yard sale. You are selling your old bike, and a neighbor asks, "How much do you want for it?" If you say "oh, ten dollars, I guess," that is now the price. Nobody will say, "No, please, let me pay you forty." The first number you say out loud becomes the ceiling. That effect has a name: **[[anchoring|anchoring]]** — the first number named in a negotiation tends to pull everything after it toward itself.

The recruiter screen has its own version of that neighbor. Somewhere in the second half of the call, often tossed in like an afterthought, comes the question: *what are your compensation expectations?* It arrives before anyone has seen your work. (**[[Compensation|compensation]]** means everything you are paid — mainly salary, sometimes more.) It is the only question in the screen where a careless sentence costs you money for years, and the one most candidates have never written an answer to.

This lesson gives you the three answers this module's exercise asks for — a redirect to the posted band, a researched range, and a version for when you are pressed for a single number — and the reason for each one's shape, because a script you do not understand falls apart the moment the conversation drifts.

It will not tell you what to ask for. The number depends on the level, the location, the year and the requisition, and any figure printed here would be wrong by the time you read it. You get the structure of an answer and the method for finding the number yourself.

## Why the question is asked this early

It is a compatibility filter, asked early for the same reason as the eligibility question: cheap to ask, expensive to discover late.

The mechanism comes from this track's module on levels. Pay for an engineering role is generally attached to a **level** as a **[[band|band-per-level]]** — a range with a bottom and a top — instead of being worked out from scratch for each person. So a requisition comes with a range already fixed before you ever apply. If your expectation sits far above its top, no amount of interviewing changes the outcome, and both sides would rather learn that at minute fifteen than after a full day of onsite interviews. That is the whole of the recruiter's interest.

Your interest pulls the other way. Whatever you say becomes a note in the file that travels with you, and the risks are lopsided:

- A number that is **too high** can, at worst, end a conversation you could not have won anyway.
- A number that is **too low** quietly becomes a ceiling, because an employer has little reason to build an offer well above what the candidate said they wanted.

There is no mechanism by which volunteering a low number gets you more. That is not a claim about anybody's motives. It is how offers get built.

::: key
Redirect to the posted band first: the posting lists a range, and you are targeting the upper part of it given your experience. If pressed, give a researched range anchored at the top of the level you are targeting. Never volunteer a number below the published band, and never apologize for the number you do give.
:::

## Before the call: three things to find out

**1. Whether the posting carries a band.** A number of US states and cities now require employers to publish a pay range in the job posting, under what are called **[[pay transparency laws|pay-transparency]]**. Which places, and what they require, changes over time, so do not rely on a list — open the posting for the role's location and look. If a band is there, it is public and it is the employer's own number. Quoting it back costs nothing and anchors nothing against you.

**2. Which level you are being considered for.** This matters more than the number, because the band belongs to the level. An answer that names a level — *the band for the Level II posting* — is precise in a way a bare figure is not, and it quietly supports the level you want to be placed at. If you do not know which requisition you are being considered against, ask before the compensation question arrives — one reason lesson one put it on your list.

**3. A researched range of your own.** You need this when no band is posted. **[[Salary aggregator sites|aggregators]]** exist and are worth reading, but treat what they show as a wide range, not a number. Their data is self-reported, often old, often mixes levels, and almost always mixes locations. Cross-check against posted bands for similar roles at similar companies in the same **metropolitan area** — the city and its surrounding towns, where pay tends to be similar. The result is not a single figure but a range you can defend, plus one sentence on where it came from.

::: warning Do not build your answer out of your current salary
What you earn now is a fact about your last employer, not about this role, and a band does not move because a candidate is underpaid today. Several US states and cities forbid employers to ask about **[[salary history|salary-history]]** at all. Which places, and exactly what is forbidden, varies and changes, so check for this role's location. Law or no law, you do not owe the number. The answer to *what are you making now?* is the same redirect as the answer to *what are you expecting?*
:::

## The three answers

### Answer one: the redirect

Use it whenever a band is posted, which is now common for US roles in places that require it. It is short, friendly, and ends the topic.

> "I saw the posting lists a range for this level. Given my experience I am targeting the upper part of that, and I would rather see where the role lands after the technical conversations before getting more precise than that."

Three things are doing the work here:

1. It cites the employer's own published number, so there is nothing for the recruiter to push back on.
2. It states a direction inside the band instead of a point. *The upper part* is real information, but it fixes nothing.
3. It gives a reason to wait that helps the recruiter too: the level is not settled yet, and both of you know it.

### Answer two: the researched range

Use it when there is no posted band, or when the recruiter asks again after the redirect. Give a range, not a number, with the bottom of your range at or above the midpoint of what your research says this level pays in this location.

> "I have looked at comparable GNC roles at this level in this area, and the range I am working with is roughly the upper half of what those postings list. That is where I would expect to land for a Level II with my background — and if the band for this requisition is different, I would rather you tell me now than at the end."

The invitation at the end is the fastest route to the one fact you want — their number — and recruiters often take it.

Put the **[[bottom of your stated range|range-bottom]]** at a figure you would be genuinely pleased with, not one you would grudgingly accept. The bottom is the only part of a range the other side hears as a promise. A range whose bottom would make you unhappy is a number you have already given away.

### Answer three: pressed for a single number

Some recruiters come back a third time for one figure. It is not hostile — often there is a form to fill in — but do not let the third ask undo the first two.

> "If you need a single figure for the file, put down the top of the posted band for this level. I would rather be precise about it after I know more about the scope, and I am not going to give you a number I would have to revise upward later."

If there is no posted band, the single figure is the top of your researched range, stated without hedging, and followed by nothing. The **[[silence after the number|silence]]** is part of the answer. Filling it with reasons is how candidates talk themselves down.

::: key
Three answers, in order of preference: redirect to the posted band; a researched range whose bottom is a number you would be genuinely happy with; and, if pressed for one figure, the top of the band or of your range, stated plainly and followed by silence. None of the three contains an apology, a justification, or a number below the posted band.
:::

## Worked exchanges

In each example, read the weak answer and count the separate mistakes before reading the explanation.

::: example The question at minute fourteen
**Recruiter:** Before we wrap up — do you have a sense of your compensation expectations?

**Weak answer:** "Oh — I mean, I have not really thought about it in detail. I am currently on a fairly modest salary, to be honest, so I think almost anything here would be a step up. I would say somewhere around the low end of what I have seen for these roles? I am much more interested in the work than the number, so I am pretty flexible on that."

**Strong answer:** "The posting lists a band for this level, and given my background I am targeting the upper part of it. I would rather get more precise after the technical rounds, once we both know which level this is landing at. Is the posted range the one budgeted for this requisition?"

**What makes the difference.** The weak answer does four damaging things in five sentences, and every one is a sentence people say to be polite:

1. It admits to not having prepared.
2. It brings up current salary, which has nothing to do with this role's band — and which some places do not even let the employer ask about.
3. It volunteers the low end, which is now a ceiling written in the file.
4. It calls itself flexible, which invites the offer to sink even below that ceiling.

"I am more interested in the work than the number" is true of almost everybody. It is heard as a discount offer, not a statement of values.

The strong answer is three sentences with no invented figure. It cites the employer's own range, states a direction, gives a reason to wait that helps the recruiter, and turns the question around to get the one fact worth having. Sanity check on length: it takes about fifteen seconds to say, roughly the airtime this topic deserves on a screening call.
:::

::: example Pressed a second and a third time
**Recruiter:** I understand, but for the file I do need a number — even a rough one.

**Weak answer:** "Sure, sorry — um, I guess somewhere around the middle of the band? Maybe a bit below, depending on the level. I do not want to price myself out or anything, and of course if the level ends up being lower then that would change things, so — yeah, somewhere in that region. Does that seem reasonable?"

**Strong answer:** "Put down the top of the posted band for this level. If the scope turns out to be different from what I am assuming, we can revisit it — but that is the honest answer for the level I am applying to."

*(then stop talking)*

**What makes the difference.** Count the steps down in the weak answer. It apologizes. It names the middle. It moves itself lower mid-sentence. It offers a situation in which it would go lower still. And it ends by asking the other side to approve it. The giveaways add up: the recruiter writes down a figure below the midpoint, with a stated willingness to go lower. Nobody applied pressure. The candidate did it alone, in the space after the number — where it almost always happens.

The strong answer gives a real, usable figure, so the recruiter can move on. It makes the basis clear — this level — and keeps the right to revisit if the level changes. Then it stops. The ten or so seconds of quiet after a number is the riskiest moment in this conversation, and the only skill needed is not filling it.
:::

::: example No posted band, and a floor that does not match
**Recruiter:** This one is not posted with a range. What are you looking for?

**Candidate:** "I have looked at comparable GNC roles at this level in this area, and I am working in the upper half of what those postings list — I can tell you the specific figure if it is useful. What is the band budgeted for this requisition?"

**Recruiter:** *(names a range whose top is below the candidate's floor — the lowest figure the candidate could accept)*

**Weak response:** "Okay — no, that could work. I am sure we could make something work. Let us keep going and see where it lands."

**Strong response:** "Thanks for telling me straight away, that is useful. That top is below where I can go for this level, so I want to flag it now rather than at the end. Is there room at a higher level for someone with my background, or is this requisition fixed at this one? If it is fixed, I would rather we both find that out now."

**What makes the difference.** The weak response is the most expensive politeness in the process. It keeps alive a process that cannot end in an accepted offer, costs both sides several rounds of interviews, and ends in a declined offer or a resented acceptance. It also wrecks the candidate's credibility on the number: they said the range could work and will have to take that back.

The strong response does three separate things:

1. It thanks the recruiter for the information, which keeps the relationship friendly.
2. It states the mismatch as a fact, not a complaint.
3. It asks the question that might actually fix it — whether a different level is open. The band follows the level, so a level mismatch is far easier to solve than a band mismatch.

It closes by naming the cost of carrying on, which is a cost the recruiter feels too.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
Explain, without appealing to anyone's motives, why volunteering a number below the posted band cannot help you.
:::

::: answer
Because of how offers are built. Pay is attached to a level as a band that already exists, and the offer is put together inside that band. A stated expectation goes into the file and works as a ceiling: an employer has little reason to build an offer well above the figure the candidate said they wanted. So the stated number can push the result down, but it has no way to push it up. That lopsidedness holds whether or not anyone involved is trying to drive a hard bargain.
:::

::: check
Why is the redirect answer stronger than refusing outright to discuss compensation until an offer exists?
:::

::: answer
Because the recruiter has a real need: checking whether your expectations fit a band fixed before you applied, since a late mismatch wastes everyone's time. A flat refusal blocks that check, sounds obstructive, and does not even protect you — the topic comes back later under worse conditions. The redirect answers the real question — are we in the same range? — using the employer's own published number. The recruiter gets what they need, and you commit to nothing.
:::

::: check
A candidate gives the top of the posted band, and the recruiter says nothing for several seconds. What is the correct next move, and what is the common error?
:::

::: answer
The correct next move is to say nothing. The silence is either the recruiter writing the number down or an ordinary pause, and it carries no information. The common error is to fill it — by justifying the number, offering a lower one, or asking whether it seems reasonable. Each of those reopens a question that was closed, and all of them sound unsure about a figure you called honest. Almost every self-inflicted giveaway in a pay conversation happens in the ten seconds after the number, not during the question.
:::

::: check
The posting carries no band and the recruiter asks for your expectations before you have named a level. What should you establish first, and why does it change the answer?
:::

::: answer
Establish which requisition and level you are being considered against. The band belongs to a level, so an expectation given without one is either meaningless or silently accepts whatever level the recruiter had in mind — which may be lower than the one your experience supports. Naming the level first makes your figure precise, supports the level you want, and gives a clean reason to revise if the level changes: the number was always a number for that level.
:::

::: check
Why does this lesson tell you to put the bottom of your researched range at a figure you would be genuinely pleased with, rather than at your true minimum?
:::

::: answer
Because the bottom of a range is the only part the other side treats as a promise: it is the number you said you would accept, and an offer built there fits exactly what you asked for. Stating your true minimum as the bottom turns your walk-away point into your expected result. A bottom you would be happy with keeps the whole range acceptable without resentment. It costs nothing, because a range whose bottom is above the band will be met with the band, not with silence.
:::

::: check
A recruiter asks what you are currently earning. Give the answer, and say what makes it safe to decline.
:::

::: answer
Redirect, with something like: "I would rather anchor on this role than my last one — the posting lists a band for this level and I am targeting the upper part of it." It is safe because your current pay is a fact about a different employer with no bearing on a band set before you applied, so declining costs the recruiter nothing they need. Also, a number of US states and cities forbid employers to ask about salary history; which ones, and exactly what is forbidden, changes over time, so check for the place the role sits in. Either way, the redirect is the same sentence.
:::

## Summary

| Situation | Answer | The rule it protects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Band is posted | Cite it; say you are targeting the upper part | Never invent a figure when the employer has published one |
| No band posted | A researched range, bottom at a figure you would be happy with | A range's bottom is heard as a commitment |
| Pressed for one number | Top of the band, or top of your range, then stop | Nothing after the number improves it |
| Asked for salary history | Redirect to the band for this level | Your last employer's number is not this role's band |
| Their band is below your floor | Say so immediately; ask whether a higher level exists | The band follows the level, so a level question is solvable |

::: context compensation More than a salary
**Compensation** is everything an employer pays you for your work. The biggest piece is **base salary**, the fixed yearly amount. Many engineering offers add others: a **bonus** (a one-time or yearly payment), and at many aerospace companies **equity** — a small share of ownership in the company, often as stock that is handed to you over several years. When a recruiter asks your "compensation expectations", they usually mean base salary first, but it is worth asking which they mean.
:::

::: context pay-transparency Laws that put the range on the posting
**Pay transparency laws** require an employer to show a good-faith pay range in a job posting. Colorado was among the first US states, with a requirement that took effect in 2021; New York City, California and Washington followed with their own rules. The details — which employers, which roles, what counts as a range — differ from place to place and keep changing, which is why the lesson says to look at the actual posting for the role's location.
:::

::: context aggregators Where crowd-sourced pay data comes from
Sites such as Glassdoor and Levels.fyi collect pay figures that are mostly reported by employees themselves. That makes them useful — they cover companies that publish nothing — and also rough. A figure may be years old, may come from a different level or city, and may include bonus or stock in one entry and not the next. Treat them as a sketch of the neighborhood, then sharpen it with real posted bands.
:::

::: context salary-history Why some places ban the question
If every new employer bases its offer on your last salary, a gap in pay can follow a person from job to job for a whole career. Lawmakers who wanted to stop that began banning the question; Massachusetts was one of the first states to do so, with a law passed in 2016. The rules vary widely, but the lesson's answer works everywhere: talk about this role's band, not your last employer's number.
:::

::: context silence Why the pause feels so loud
On a phone or video call you cannot see the other person nodding or writing, so a few seconds of quiet feel much longer than they are. Most people rush to fill that gap, and on a pay question the easiest thing to fill it with is a lower number. Knowing the pause is coming — and that it usually means someone is typing your figure into a form — makes it far easier to sit through.
:::
