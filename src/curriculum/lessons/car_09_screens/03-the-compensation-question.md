Somewhere in the second half of the recruiter screen, often introduced as an afterthought, comes the question: *what are your compensation expectations?* It arrives before you have spoken to a single engineer, before anyone has seen your work, and before you know what the role actually is. It is the only question in the whole screen where a careless sentence costs you money for years, and it is the one most candidates have not written an answer to.

This lesson gives you three answers, which is what this module's own exercise asks for: a redirect to the posted band, a researched range, and a version for when you are pressed for a single number. It also gives you the reason each one is shaped the way it is, because scripts you do not understand collapse the moment the conversation goes slightly off-script.

One thing this lesson will not do is tell you what to ask for. No curriculum can: the number depends on the level, the location, the year and the specific requisition, and any figure printed here would be wrong by the time you read it and worse than useless if you repeated it on a call. What this lesson gives you is the structure of an answer and the method for filling in the number yourself.

## Why the question is asked this early

It is a compatibility filter, and it is asked early for the same reason the eligibility question is: it is cheap to ask and expensive to discover late.

The mechanism was established in this track's module on levels. Compensation for an engineering role is generally attached to a level as a band — a range — rather than assembled from scratch for each candidate. A requisition therefore comes with a range already fixed before you ever apply. If your expectation sits far above the top of that range, no amount of interviewing changes the outcome, and both sides would rather find out in minute fifteen than after an onsite. That is the whole of the recruiter's interest in the question.

Your interest is different and pulls in the opposite direction. Whatever you say becomes a data point in the recruiter's notes that travels with your file. The asymmetry is the thing to understand: a number that is too high can, at worst, end a conversation you could not have won anyway; a number that is too low quietly becomes a ceiling, because no employer constructs an offer materially above what the candidate said they wanted. There is no mechanism by which volunteering a low number gets you more. That is not a claim about anybody's motives — it is arithmetic about how offers get built.

::: key
Redirect to the posted band first: the posting lists a range, and you are targeting the upper part of it given your experience. If pressed, give a researched range anchored at the top of the level you are targeting. Never volunteer a number below the published band, and never apologise for the number you do give.
:::

## Before the call: three things to find out

**Whether the posting carries a band.** A number of US states and cities now require employers to publish a pay range in the job posting. Which jurisdictions, and exactly what they require, changes over time, so do not rely on a list in a curriculum — open the posting for the location the role actually sits in and look. If a band is there, it is public information, it is the employer's own number, and quoting it back costs you nothing and anchors nothing against you.

**Which level you are being considered for.** This matters more than the number, because the band is attached to the level. A compensation answer that names a level — *the band for the Level II posting* — is precise in a way that a bare figure is not, and it quietly reinforces the leveling case you want made. If you do not know which requisition you are being considered against, that is a question to ask before the compensation question arrives, which is one reason lesson one put it on the list.

**A researched range of your own.** You need this for the case where no band is posted. Published aggregators exist and are worth reading, but treat their output as a wide range rather than a number: the data is self-reported, often stale, frequently mixes levels, and almost always mixes locations. Cross-check against any posted bands you can find for comparable roles at comparable companies in the same metropolitan area. The output of this research is not a figure, it is a range you can defend, plus a one-sentence account of where it came from.

::: warning Do not build your answer out of your current salary
Your existing compensation is a fact about your last employer, not about the value of this role, and a role's band does not move because a candidate happens to be underpaid today. Several US states and cities prohibit employers from asking for salary history at all — which jurisdictions and what exactly is prohibited varies and changes, so check for the location this role sits in rather than trusting a list. Independent of the law, you do not owe the number, and the answer to *what are you making now?* is the same redirect as the answer to *what are you expecting?*
:::

## The three answers

### Answer one: the redirect

Use it whenever a band is posted, which is most of the time now for US roles in the states that require it. It is short, it is friendly, and it ends the topic.

> "I saw the posting lists a range for this level. Given my experience I am targeting the upper part of that, and I would rather see where the role lands after the technical conversations before getting more precise than that."

Three things are doing work. It cites the employer's own published number, so there is nothing for the recruiter to push back on. It states a direction within the band rather than a point, which is a real answer — *the upper part* is information — without fixing anything. And it names a reason for deferring that serves the recruiter too: the level is not settled yet, and both of you know it.

### Answer two: the researched range

Use it when there is no posted band, or when the recruiter asks again after the redirect. A range, not a number, with the bottom of your range at or above the midpoint of what your research says the level pays in that location.

> "I have looked at comparable GNC roles at this level in this area, and the range I am working with is roughly the upper half of what those postings list. That is where I would expect to land for a Level II with my background — and if the band for this requisition is different, I would rather you tell me now than at the end."

The invitation at the end is not a courtesy. It is the fastest route to the one piece of information you actually want, which is their number, and recruiters frequently take it.

Put the bottom of your stated range where you would genuinely be pleased, not where you would grudgingly accept, because a range's bottom is the only part of it the other side hears as a commitment. A range whose bottom you would be unhappy with is a number you have already conceded.

### Answer three: pressed for a single number

Some recruiters will come back a third time and ask for one figure. Do not treat this as hostile — often it is a form they have to fill in. But do not let the third ask undo the first two.

> "If you need a single figure for the file, put down the top of the posted band for this level. I would rather be precise about it after I know more about the scope, and I am not going to give you a number I would have to revise upward later."

If there is no posted band, the single figure is the top of your researched range, stated without hedging, followed by nothing. The silence after the number is part of the answer; filling it with justification is how candidates talk themselves down.

::: key
Three answers, in order of preference: redirect to the posted band; a researched range whose bottom is a number you would be genuinely happy with; and, if pressed for one figure, the top of the band or of your range, stated plainly and followed by silence. None of the three contains an apology, a justification, or a number below the posted band.
:::

## Worked exchanges

::: example The question at minute fourteen
**Recruiter:** Before we wrap up — do you have a sense of your compensation expectations?

**Weak answer:** "Oh — I mean, I have not really thought about it in detail. I am currently on a fairly modest salary, to be honest, so I think almost anything here would be a step up. I would say somewhere around the low end of what I have seen for these roles? I am much more interested in the work than the number, so I am pretty flexible on that."

**Strong answer:** "The posting lists a band for this level, and given my background I am targeting the upper part of it. I would rather get more precise after the technical rounds, once we both know which level this is landing at. Is the posted range the one budgeted for this requisition?"

**What makes the difference:** the weak answer does four damaging things in five sentences, and every one of them is a sentence candidates say out of politeness. It admits to not having prepared. It introduces current salary, which is irrelevant to this role's band and which several jurisdictions do not even permit the employer to ask for. It volunteers the low end, which is a ceiling now written in the file. And it closes by describing itself as flexible, which converts the ceiling into a floor-to-ceiling collapse. Note that "I am more interested in the work than the number" is true of almost everybody and buys nothing: it is heard as a discount offer, not as a statement of values.

The strong answer is three sentences, contains no figure the candidate invented, cites the employer's own published range, states a direction, gives a reason to defer that helps the recruiter, and ends by turning the question around to extract the one fact worth having. It also takes about fifteen seconds, which is roughly the amount of airtime this topic deserves at a screening call.
:::

::: example Pressed a second and a third time
**Recruiter:** I understand, but for the file I do need a number — even a rough one.

**Weak answer:** "Sure, sorry — um, I guess somewhere around the middle of the band? Maybe a bit below, depending on the level. I do not want to price myself out or anything, and obviously if the level ends up being lower then that would change things, so — yeah, somewhere in that region. Does that seem reasonable?"

**Strong answer:** "Put down the top of the posted band for this level. If the scope turns out to be different from what I am assuming, we can revisit it — but that is the honest answer for the level I am applying to."

*(then stop talking)*

**What makes the difference:** the weak answer apologises, names the middle, revises itself downward mid-sentence, volunteers a scenario in which it would be lower, and finishes by asking the other side to validate it. Each clause is a small concession, and they compound: the recruiter now records a figure below the midpoint with a stated willingness to go lower. Notice that no pressure was applied to produce this. The candidate did it to themselves in the space after the number, which is where it almost always happens.

The strong answer gives a real, usable figure — the recruiter's problem is solved, and they will move on — while making the basis explicit and reserving the right to revisit if the level changes. Then it stops. The instruction to stop talking is not a rhetorical flourish; the twelve seconds of quiet after a number is the single highest-risk moment in this entire conversation, and the only skill required is not filling it.
:::

::: example No posted band, and a floor that does not match
**Recruiter:** This one is not posted with a range. What are you looking for?

**Candidate:** "I have looked at comparable GNC roles at this level in this area, and I am working in the upper half of what those postings list — I can tell you the specific figure if it is useful. What is the band budgeted for this requisition?"

**Recruiter:** *(names a range whose top is below the candidate's floor)*

**Weak response:** "Okay — no, that could work. I am sure we could make something work. Let us keep going and see where it lands."

**Strong response:** "Thanks for telling me straight away, that is useful. That top is below where I can go for this level, so I want to flag it now rather than at the end. Is there room at a higher level for someone with my background, or is this requisition fixed at this one? If it is fixed, I would rather we both find that out now."

**What makes the difference:** the weak response is the most expensive kind of politeness in the whole process. It keeps a process alive that cannot end in an accepted offer, costs both sides several rounds of interviewing, and ends with either a declined offer or a candidate accepting something they resent. It also destroys the candidate's credibility on the number, because they have now said the range could work and will have to un-say it.

The strong response does three separate things: it thanks the recruiter for the information, which keeps the relationship intact; it states the mismatch as a fact rather than a complaint; and it asks the question that might actually solve it — whether a different level is available — because the band follows the level, and a level mismatch is a far more tractable problem than a band mismatch. It closes by making the cost of continuing explicit, which is an argument the recruiter also feels.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
Explain, without appealing to anyone's motives, why volunteering a number below the posted band cannot help you.
:::

::: answer
Because of how offers are constructed. Compensation is attached to a level as a pre-existing band, and the offer is assembled inside that band. A stated expectation enters the file and functions as a ceiling: no employer builds an offer materially above the figure the candidate said they wanted, since there is no reason to. So the stated number can move the outcome downward but has no mechanism for moving it upward. That asymmetry holds regardless of whether anyone involved is trying to drive a hard bargain.
:::

::: check
Why is the redirect answer stronger than simply refusing to discuss compensation until an offer exists?
:::

::: answer
Because the recruiter has a legitimate need at this stage: they are checking compatibility between your expectations and a band that was fixed before you applied, and finding an incompatibility late wastes everyone's time. A flat refusal blocks that check, reads as obstructive at a screening call, and does not even protect you, since the topic simply returns later under worse conditions. The redirect answers the underlying question — are we in the same range? — using the employer's own published number, which gives the recruiter what they need while committing you to nothing.
:::

::: check
A candidate gives the top of the posted band, and the recruiter says nothing for several seconds. What is the correct next move, and what is the common error?
:::

::: answer
The correct next move is to say nothing. The silence is either the recruiter writing the number down or an ordinary pause, and it carries no information. The common error is to fill it — typically by justifying the number, offering a lower alternative, or asking whether it seems reasonable. Every one of those reopens a question that had just been closed, and all of them read as a lack of conviction about a figure you have just described as honest. Almost every self-inflicted concession in a compensation conversation happens in the ten seconds after the number rather than during the question.
:::

::: check
The posting carries no band and the recruiter asks for your expectations before you have named a level. What should you establish first, and why does it change the answer?
:::

::: answer
Establish which requisition and level you are being considered against. The band is attached to a level, so an expectation quoted without one is either meaningless or silently agrees to whichever level the recruiter had in mind — which may be lower than the one your case supports. Naming the level first makes your figure precise, supports the leveling argument you want made anyway, and gives you a clean basis to revise if the level changes later: the number was always stated as a number for that level.
:::

::: check
Why does this lesson tell you to put the bottom of your researched range at a figure you would be genuinely pleased with, rather than at your true minimum?
:::

::: answer
Because a range's bottom is the only part of it that the other side treats as a commitment: once stated, it is the number you have said you would accept, and an offer built at that figure is entirely consistent with what you asked for. Stating your true minimum as the bottom therefore converts your walk-away point into your expected outcome. Setting the bottom at a figure you would be happy with keeps the whole stated range one you can accept without resentment, and it costs nothing, because a range whose bottom is above the band will be met with the band rather than with silence.
:::

::: check
A recruiter asks what you are currently earning. Give the answer, and say what makes it safe to decline.
:::

::: answer
Redirect: something like "I would rather anchor on this role rather than my last one — the posting lists a band for this level and I am targeting the upper part of it." What makes it safe is that your current pay is a fact about a different employer and has no bearing on a band that was set before you applied, so declining costs the recruiter nothing they actually need. It is also worth knowing that a number of US states and cities prohibit employers from asking for salary history; which ones and exactly what is prohibited changes over time, so check for the jurisdiction the role sits in. Either way, the redirect is the same sentence.
:::

## Summary

| Situation | Answer | The rule it protects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Band is posted | Cite it; say you are targeting the upper part | Never invent a figure when the employer has published one |
| No band posted | A researched range, bottom at a figure you would be happy with | A range's bottom is heard as a commitment |
| Pressed for one number | Top of the band, or top of your range, then stop | Nothing after the number improves it |
| Asked for salary history | Redirect to the band for this level | Your last employer's number is not this role's band |
| Their band is below your floor | Say so immediately; ask whether a higher level exists | The band follows the level, so a level question is tractable |
