The previous module drew the whole pipeline: roughly five to eight weeks, up to about nine touchpoints, from an application submitted online through to a decision. This module takes the first two of those touchpoints — the recruiter screen and the technical phone screen — and works them in the detail the map could not. The reason to spend a whole module on two conversations is that they gate everything else — no later stage happens unless both are passed — and that almost everything which decides them is preparation rather than talent.

This first lesson is about the recruiter screen: a call of roughly twenty to thirty minutes, conducted by someone whose job is not to evaluate your control theory. Understanding what that person is and is not assessing is most of the work. It is easy to treat the recruiter screen as either a formality to be got through or a technical interview in disguise, and both readings produce bad calls — the first because it is not a formality, the second because answering a logistics question with a derivation makes you look like someone who cannot read a room.

A note about what this module can and cannot tell you, since it applies to every lesson here. The structure of these two calls, the topics reported in them, and the durations above come from this curriculum's own source material, which is what you have. What no curriculum can tell you is how a specific recruiter at a specific company on a specific Tuesday will run a specific call. Where this module states something, it is because the source states it or because the mechanism is plain. Where it does not know, it will say so, and it will tell you how to find out — which, for most of these questions, means asking the recruiter you actually have.

## What the recruiter is for

A recruiter's job in a screening call is routing and filtering, not technical evaluation. Routing means deciding which requisition, which team, and which level your application belongs against — a company running several open GNC roles at once may have one that fits you well and three that do not, and a fifteen-minute conversation is much faster than reading your résumé five times. Filtering means checking the small number of binary facts that can end the process regardless of how good an engineer you are: eligibility, location, availability, and whether your compensation expectations and the budgeted band are in the same universe.

Neither of those jobs requires the recruiter to understand what a Kalman filter does. This is not a deficiency; it is a division of labour. The engineer who runs the next call is the one who assesses that, and they will do it properly. Expecting technical depth from the recruiter screen and being disappointed to find none is a misreading of the stage.

What follows from this is the single most useful behavioural rule for the call: **answer the question that was asked, at the level of detail it was asked at.** If a recruiter asks what you have been working on, the answer is two or three sentences a non-specialist can follow, ending somewhere a specialist could pull on. It is not four minutes on covariance propagation.

::: key
The recruiter screen is roughly twenty to thirty minutes, and it covers background, motivation, confirmation of export-control eligibility as a US person, location and relocation, timeline, and often compensation expectations. It is a screen rather than a conversation: its purpose is routing and filtering, not technical evaluation.
:::

## The six areas, and what each one is really asking

The six areas are not six questions asked in a fixed order — different recruiters run the call differently, and some will cover an area in a single sentence and skip another entirely. Treat the list as the set of things that need to be resolved before the call ends, and notice which ones have not come up, because you may need to raise them yourself.

### Background

Usually first, usually phrased as an open invitation: *tell me about yourself*, *walk me through your background*, *what have you been working on?* This is the question the entire next lesson is about, so it gets no more space here than one observation: it is not small talk, it is the routing question. What you say here determines which team the recruiter pictures you on and which role family they check you against. An answer that ends nowhere in particular forces the recruiter to guess.

### Motivation

*Why us? Why this role?* The reason this is asked at a screening stage rather than later is that a candidate applying to forty companies with the same cover letter is a different prospect from one who has a specific reason to be on this call, and the difference shows up fastest in a spoken answer. What a strong answer contains is a fact about the company or the team that is not true of every employer in the industry, connected to something you have actually done. What a weak answer contains is an enthusiasm that would be equally true if the company's name were swapped out.

### Export-control eligibility

This is the one that can end the call in the first five minutes, and this curriculum's first module covered it in full: the statutory basis, who counts as a US person, why it is confirmed at the recruiter screen rather than later, and what an honest answer sounds like from either side of the line. Nothing about it has changed here. The only thing worth adding is the tonal point: it is a routine logistics question, it is asked of everyone, and the right register for answering it is the same register you would use to confirm a start date. A long, defensive or apologetic answer to a yes-or-no question creates an impression that a one-word answer does not.

### Location and relocation

Where you are, where the role is, whether you would move, and when. This is a filter, not a negotiation. Answer it precisely, and volunteer the constraint if you have one — *I can relocate, and the earliest I could start is the first week of March* is more useful to a recruiter than *I am pretty flexible*, which tells them nothing they can plan around.

### Timeline

Yours and theirs. Yours: when you could start, and whether you are in process elsewhere with a deadline attached. Theirs: what the stages are from here and roughly when they happen. Both halves are useful to you, and the second half is the one candidates forget to ask for.

### Compensation expectations

Often, though not always, at the screening stage. This is the subject of lesson three, and it has exactly one rule worth stating this early: do not volunteer a number below the posted band. Everything else is technique.

::: warning The recruiter screen is a screen, not a conversation
Because it is friendly and low-pressure, it is easy to treat it as a chat where nothing is being evaluated. Things are being evaluated, though not the things you prepared for. Vagueness on logistics, an answer that does not land anywhere, or a number volunteered carelessly all have consequences, and they have them at the cheapest stage of the process to lose.
:::

## What you should be getting out of it

The screen runs in both directions, and the information available to you on this call is not available anywhere else in the process at the same quality. A recruiter can tell you, in a way an engineer interviewer generally will not:

- **Which requisition this is.** Which program, which team, which level. You may have applied to a posting and be being considered against a different one.
- **The stage sequence from here.** What the next call is, who runs it, what it covers.
- **The timeline.** When the next stage would happen, and when you should follow up if you have not heard.
- **The band**, where a posting carries one and you want it confirmed.

Ask for all four. None of them is a favour; a recruiter's job is to keep candidates moving through a process, and a candidate who knows what happens next is easier to move. Write the answers down during the call, because the timeline answer in particular is the thing you will want in three weeks when you are deciding whether to send a follow-up note.

::: key
Ask the recruiter, on the screening call, for four things: which program and team the requisition sits in, the stage sequence from here, the expected timeline, and when to check back if you have not heard. Write the answers down. This curriculum cannot tell you the per-stage timeline at any employer, and the person who can is on the phone with you.
:::

## Two worked calls

::: example The routing question, answered two ways
**Recruiter:** So, tell me a bit about yourself and what you have been working on.

**Weak answer:** "Sure — so I have always been fascinated by space, ever since I was a kid. I did my undergrad in mechanical engineering, and I have done a lot of different things: some CAD work, a bit of thermal, some controls. I am pretty flexible about what I work on, honestly. I just really want to be part of something like this."

**Strong answer:** "I am a controls engineer. Most of my work has been attitude control and estimation — I spent the last two years on a pointing control loop for an optical payload, and I have built and flown a full six-degree-of-freedom simulation for it. The part I am strongest at is closing loops on a rigid body with real sensors and real noise. I am looking at GNC roles on the vehicle side specifically."

**What makes the difference:** the weak answer gives the recruiter nothing to route on. "Pretty flexible" and "a lot of different things" mean the recruiter must now guess which of four open requisitions to check you against, and a guess made under time pressure defaults to the safest one, which is rarely the best one for you. It also ends on a feeling, which is unfalsifiable and which every other candidate on the list also has.

The strong answer names a discipline in the first four words, gives one concrete piece of evidence with a duration attached, states what the candidate is actually good at in a sentence a non-specialist can follow, and closes by naming the role family. The recruiter now has a routing decision they can make in one step, and the engineer on the next call has a thread to pull: *tell me about that pointing loop.* That is the answer working.
:::

::: example The logistics half of the call, and what it costs to be vague
**Recruiter:** And are you currently located in the Los Angeles area, or would this be a relocation?

**Weak answer:** "I am in Boston right now, but I am definitely open to relocating — I mean, for the right role, absolutely. Timing-wise I am sort of flexible, I would have to see, but I do not think it would be a problem."

**Strong answer:** "Relocation. I am in Boston, I would move for this, and I could start four weeks after an offer. One thing worth flagging: I have a final round with another company the week of the ninth, so if there is a way to keep this roughly in step, that helps me."

**What makes the difference:** the weak answer is not dishonest, but it is unusable. A recruiter writing notes cannot put "sort of flexible" into a scheduling conversation with a hiring manager, so the ambiguity either gets resolved by a second call, which costs time, or gets resolved by an assumption, which may be wrong. The strong answer gives a decision, a number, and a constraint, all three of which are things a recruiter can act on. The competing-process disclosure is the part candidates leave out for fear of seeming difficult; it is in fact ordinary information that lets a recruiter compress a schedule if they want to, and withholding it means they cannot.

Note what the strong answer does not do: it does not apologise for the relocation, does not oversell the flexibility, and does not turn a scheduling constraint into a negotiation. It is one sentence of fact per item.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
A candidate spends four minutes answering *tell me about yourself* with a detailed technical account of an estimator they built, including the measurement model and the tuning approach. Why is this a weaker answer at the recruiter screen than it would be at the technical phone screen?
:::

::: answer
Because the two calls are asking different questions with the same words. At the technical screen, an engineer asking about your estimator wants exactly that depth, and giving it is the point of the round. At the recruiter screen, the question is a routing question: the recruiter needs to decide which requisition and which level to consider you against, and needs an answer they can act on. Four minutes of measurement models gives them nothing routable, consumes a large fraction of a twenty-to-thirty-minute call, and signals that the candidate answers the question they prepared rather than the one asked — which is a real negative read, because the same habit would produce the same failure in front of a customer, a review board or a panel.
:::

::: check
Name the six areas a recruiter screen covers, and identify which one can end the process fastest and why.
:::

::: answer
Background, motivation, export-control eligibility, location and relocation, timeline, and compensation expectations. Export-control eligibility can end it fastest, because it is binary and statutory: it is not a judgment a strong interview can change, and every later stage costs engineering time that would be wasted on a candidate who cannot ultimately be hired into the role. That is why it is confirmed at the first live conversation rather than at the offer stage.
:::

::: check
The recruiter does not raise compensation, timeline or next steps, and the call is winding down. What should you do, and why does the answer not depend on knowing how this particular company's process runs?
:::

::: answer
Ask. Specifically, ask which program and team the requisition sits in, what the stages are from here, roughly when the next one would be, and when you should check back if you have not heard. The answer does not depend on company specifics precisely because you are asking for them: this curriculum cannot tell you a per-stage timeline at any employer, and the recruiter is the one person in the process who both knows and is expected to say. Getting it on the call turns a three-week silence later from an anxiety into a data point you can compare against what you were told.
:::

::: check
Why does a defensive or lengthy answer to the export-control eligibility question cost a candidate something even when their answer is "yes"?
:::

::: answer
Because the question is routine, asked of every candidate, and expects a one-word answer, so a long or defensive one changes the register of the call without changing the fact. It reads either as a misunderstanding of what was asked — a logistics confirmation mistaken for an accusation — or as discomfort with a compliance topic that this whole industry runs on. Neither is what you want a recruiter writing in their notes at minute four. The correct register is the one you would use to confirm a start date.
:::

::: check
A candidate is told, at the end of a recruiter screen, that the next step is a technical call with an engineer on the team. They do not ask anything further. Name two concrete things they have given up.
:::

::: answer
First, the timeline and the check-back point: without them, any subsequent silence is uninterpretable, and the candidate has no non-awkward basis for a follow-up note. Second, the identity and scope of the next round: knowing whether it is a team engineer or the hiring manager, and which program the requisition sits in, changes what you prepare — a team engineer on a launch vehicle programme and a hiring manager on a spacecraft programme will pull on different lines of your résumé. Both are freely available for the asking on this call and much harder to obtain afterwards.
:::

## Summary

| Area | What the recruiter is actually resolving | What a good answer looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Background | Which requisition and level to route you to | Two or three sentences ending on a role family and one piece of evidence |
| Motivation | Whether you have a specific reason to be here | A fact about this team connected to something you did |
| Export-control eligibility | A binary, statutory gate | A direct, short, unapologetic answer |
| Location and relocation | A scheduling and logistics filter | A decision, a date, and any constraint, stated plainly |
| Timeline | Their schedule and yours | Your earliest start; their stages and dates, which you ask for |
| Compensation | Whether expectations and band are compatible | Covered in lesson three; never a number below the posted band |
