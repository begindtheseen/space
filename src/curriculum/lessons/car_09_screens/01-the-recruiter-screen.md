Walk into a busy restaurant and the first person you meet is not the chef. It is the host at the front. The host asks a few quick questions: How many of you? Do you have a reservation? Inside or on the patio? The host is not judging your taste in food. The host is deciding which table you go to, and whether there is a table for you at all.

A company's hiring process has a host too. It is called the **recruiter screen** — a short phone or video call with a **[[recruiter|recruiter-job]]**, the person whose job is to find candidates and move them through the process. It is the first live conversation you will have with the company, and nothing else happens unless it goes well.

The previous module drew the whole hiring **[[pipeline|pipeline-map]]**: roughly five to eight weeks and up to about nine touchpoints, from an online application to a decision. This module zooms in on the first two of those touchpoints — the recruiter screen and the technical phone screen. Why spend a whole module on two conversations? Because they gate everything else: no later stage happens unless both are passed. And almost everything that decides them is preparation, not talent.

This first lesson is about the recruiter screen: a call of roughly twenty to thirty minutes, run by someone whose job is not to evaluate your control theory. Most of the work is understanding what that person is assessing and what they are not. People misread this call in two ways. Some treat it as a formality to get through. Some treat it as a technical interview in disguise. Both produce bad calls. The first fails because it is not a formality. The second fails because answering a scheduling question with a page of math makes you look like someone who cannot read the room.

One honest note that applies to every lesson in this module. The structure of these two calls, the topics reported in them, and the durations above come from this curriculum's own source material. No curriculum can tell you how one recruiter at one company will run one call on one Tuesday. Where this module states something, it is because the source states it or because the reason is plain to see. Where it does not know, it says so — and tells you how to find out. For most of these questions, that means asking the recruiter you actually have.

## What the recruiter is for

A recruiter on a screening call does two jobs: **routing** and **filtering**.

**Routing** means deciding which opening you belong against — which **[[requisition|requisition]]**, which team, and which level. A company may have several **GNC** roles open at once — jobs in guidance, navigation and control, the engineering that steers a vehicle and keeps track of where it is. One may fit you well and three may not. A fifteen-minute conversation sorts that out much faster than reading your résumé five times.

**Filtering** means checking the few yes-or-no facts that can end the process however good an engineer you are: eligibility, location, availability, and whether your pay expectations and the budgeted pay range are anywhere near each other.

Neither job needs the recruiter to know what a **[[Kalman filter|kalman-filter]]** does. That is not a flaw. It is a division of labor. The engineer on the next call assesses your technical depth, and does it properly. Expecting depth from the recruiter, and being let down when there is none, is a misreading of the stage.

That gives you the most useful rule for the whole call: **answer the question that was asked, at the level of detail it was asked at.** If a recruiter asks what you have been working on, the answer is two or three sentences a non-specialist can follow, ending somewhere a specialist could pull on. It is not four minutes on **[[covariance propagation|covariance]]**.

::: key
The recruiter screen is roughly twenty to thirty minutes, and it covers background, motivation, confirmation of export-control eligibility as a US person, location and relocation, timeline, and often compensation expectations. It is a screen rather than a conversation: its purpose is routing and filtering, not technical evaluation.
:::

## The six areas, and what each one is really asking

Think of the six areas as a checklist on the recruiter's desk, not six questions read out in a fixed order. Recruiters run the call differently. Some cover an area in one sentence and skip another entirely. So treat the list as the set of things that should be settled before the call ends — and notice which ones have not come up, because you may need to raise them yourself.

### Background

This usually comes first, as an open invitation: *tell me about yourself*, *walk me through your background*, *what have you been working on?* The whole next lesson is about this answer, so here is only one point. It is not small talk. It is the **routing question**. What you say decides which team the recruiter pictures you on and which **role family** — a group of related jobs, such as guidance or navigation — they check you against. An answer that ends nowhere in particular forces the recruiter to guess.

### Motivation

*Why us? Why this role?* Why ask this so early? A candidate sending the same cover letter to forty companies is a different prospect from one with a specific reason to be on this call. The difference shows up fastest when you speak.

A strong answer holds a fact about this company or team that is not true of every employer in the industry, tied to something you have actually done. A weak answer holds enthusiasm that would stay equally true if you swapped the company's name for another.

### Export-control eligibility

This is the one that can end the call in the first five minutes. Space hardware is controlled by US export law, called **[[ITAR|itar-us-person]]** (said "eye-tar"), and many roles can only be filled by a **US person**. The first module of this track covered it in full: the law behind it, who counts as a US person, why it is confirmed at the recruiter screen and not later, and what an honest answer sounds like from either side of the line. Nothing about that changes here.

The only point to add is tone. This is a routine logistics question, asked of everyone. Answer it in the same voice you would use to confirm a start date. A long, defensive or apologetic answer to a yes-or-no question creates an impression that a one-word answer does not.

### Location and relocation

Where you are, where the role is, whether you would move, and when. This is a filter, not a negotiation. Answer precisely, and volunteer your constraint if you have one. *"I can relocate, and the earliest I could start is the first week of March"* is useful to a recruiter. *"I am pretty flexible"* tells them nothing they can plan around.

### Timeline

Yours and theirs. **Yours**: when you could start, and whether you are interviewing elsewhere with a deadline attached. **Theirs**: what the stages are from here and roughly when they happen. Both halves help you, and the second half is the one candidates forget to ask for.

### Compensation expectations

Often, though not always, this comes up at the screen. It is the subject of lesson three, and it has one rule worth stating now: do not volunteer a number below the posted **[[band|pay-band]]** — the pay range the company publishes for the role. Everything else is technique.

::: warning The recruiter screen is a screen, not a conversation
Because it is friendly and low-pressure, it is easy to treat it as a chat where nothing is being judged. Things are being judged, though not the things you prepared for. A vague answer on logistics, a background answer that lands nowhere, or a number said carelessly all have consequences. And they have them at the cheapest stage of the process to lose.
:::

## What you should be getting out of it

The screen runs in both directions. Some information is available on this call and nowhere else at the same quality. A recruiter can tell you, in a way an engineer interviewer usually will not:

- **Which requisition this is.** Which program, which team, which level. You may have applied to one posting and be under consideration for a different one.
- **The stage sequence from here.** What the next call is, who runs it, and what it covers.
- **The timeline.** When the next stage would happen, and when to follow up if you have not heard.
- **The band**, if the posting carries one and you want it confirmed.

Ask for all four. None of them is a favor. A recruiter's job is to keep candidates moving, and a candidate who knows what happens next is easier to move. Write the answers down during the call. The timeline answer in particular is the one you will want in three weeks, when you are deciding whether to send a **[[follow-up note|follow-up]]**.

::: key
Ask the recruiter, on the screening call, for four things: which program and team the requisition sits in, the stage sequence from here, the expected timeline, and when to check back if you have not heard. Write the answers down. This curriculum cannot tell you the per-stage timeline at any employer, and the person who can is on the phone with you.
:::

## Two worked calls

Each example below shows the same question answered two ways, then explains the difference. Read the weak answer first and try to spot what is wrong before you read the explanation.

::: example The routing question, answered two ways
**Recruiter:** So, tell me a bit about yourself and what you have been working on.

**Weak answer:** "Sure — so I have always been fascinated by space, ever since I was a kid. I did my undergrad in mechanical engineering, and I have done a lot of different things: some CAD work, a bit of thermal, some controls. I am pretty flexible about what I work on, honestly. I really want to be part of something like this."

**Strong answer:** "I am a controls engineer. Most of my work has been attitude control and estimation — I spent the last two years on a pointing control loop for an optical payload, and I have built and flown a full [[six-degree-of-freedom|six-dof]] simulation for it. The part I am strongest at is closing loops on a rigid body with real sensors and real noise. I am looking at GNC roles on the vehicle side specifically."

**What makes the difference.** The weak answer gives the recruiter nothing to route on. "Pretty flexible" and "a lot of different things" mean the recruiter must now guess which of four open requisitions to check you against. A guess made in a hurry defaults to the safest option, which is rarely the best one for you. It also ends on a feeling, which cannot be checked and which every other candidate on the list shares.

The strong answer names a discipline in its first four words. (**Attitude control** means keeping a vehicle pointed the right way; **estimation** means working out its state from noisy sensors.) It gives one concrete piece of evidence with a length of time attached. It says what the candidate is good at in a sentence a non-specialist can follow. And it closes by naming the role family. The recruiter can now route in one step — and the engineer on the next call has a thread to pull: *tell me about that pointing loop.* That is the answer working.
:::

::: example The logistics half of the call, and what it costs to be vague
**Recruiter:** And are you currently in the Los Angeles area, or would this be a relocation?

**Weak answer:** "I am in Boston right now, but I am definitely open to relocating — I mean, for the right role, absolutely. Timing-wise I am sort of flexible, I would have to see, but I do not think it would be a problem."

**Strong answer:** "Relocation. I am in Boston, I would move for this, and I could start four weeks after an offer. One thing worth flagging: I have a final round with another company the week of the ninth, so if there is a way to keep this roughly in step, that helps me."

**What makes the difference.** The weak answer is not dishonest, but it is unusable. A recruiter writing notes cannot put "sort of flexible" into a scheduling conversation with a hiring manager. So the vagueness gets resolved either by a second call, which costs time, or by an assumption, which may be wrong.

The strong answer gives three things a recruiter can act on: a decision (relocation, yes), a number (four weeks), and a constraint (the other final round). Candidates often leave out the competing process for fear of seeming difficult. In fact it is ordinary information that lets a recruiter speed up a schedule if they want to. Hide it, and they cannot.

Notice what the strong answer does *not* do. It does not apologize for the move, does not oversell its flexibility, and does not turn a scheduling constraint into a negotiation. It is one sentence of fact per item. Sanity check: could the recruiter fill in a scheduling form from it right now? Yes — which is the test the weak answer fails.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
A candidate spends four minutes answering *tell me about yourself* with a detailed technical account of an estimator they built, including the measurement model and the tuning approach. Why is this a weaker answer at the recruiter screen than it would be at the technical phone screen?
:::

::: answer
Because the two calls ask different questions with the same words. At the technical screen, an engineer asking about your estimator wants exactly that depth, and giving it is the point of the round. At the recruiter screen, the question is a routing question: the recruiter must decide which requisition and which level to consider you against, and needs an answer they can act on. Four minutes of measurement models gives them nothing to route on. It uses up a large share of a twenty-to-thirty-minute call. And it signals that the candidate answers the question they prepared instead of the one asked. That is a real negative, because the same habit would produce the same failure in front of a customer, a review board or a panel.
:::

::: check
Name the six areas a recruiter screen covers, and identify which one can end the process fastest and why.
:::

::: answer
Background, motivation, export-control eligibility, location and relocation, timeline, and compensation expectations. Export-control eligibility can end it fastest, because it is yes-or-no and set by law. It is not a judgment a strong interview can change. Every later stage costs engineering time that would be wasted on a candidate who cannot, in the end, be hired into the role. That is why it is confirmed at the first live conversation and not at the offer stage.
:::

::: check
The recruiter does not raise compensation, timeline or next steps, and the call is winding down. What should you do, and why does the answer not depend on knowing how this particular company's process runs?
:::

::: answer
Ask. Specifically, ask which program and team the requisition sits in, what the stages are from here, roughly when the next one would be, and when you should check back if you have not heard. The answer does not depend on company specifics precisely because you are asking for them. This curriculum cannot tell you a per-stage timeline at any employer; the recruiter is the one person in the process who both knows and is expected to say. Getting it on the call turns a three-week silence later from a worry into a data point you can compare against what you were told.
:::

::: check
Why does a defensive or lengthy answer to the export-control eligibility question cost a candidate something even when their answer is "yes"?
:::

::: answer
Because the question is routine, asked of every candidate, and expects a one-word answer. A long or defensive reply changes the tone of the call without changing the fact. It reads either as a misunderstanding of what was asked — a logistics check mistaken for an accusation — or as discomfort with a compliance topic that this whole industry runs on. Neither is what you want a recruiter writing in their notes at minute four. The right tone is the one you would use to confirm a start date.
:::

::: check
A candidate is told, at the end of a recruiter screen, that the next step is a technical call with an engineer on the team. They do not ask anything further. Name two concrete things they have given up.
:::

::: answer
First, the timeline and the check-back point. Without them, any later silence cannot be interpreted, and the candidate has no comfortable basis for a follow-up note. Second, the identity and scope of the next round. Knowing whether it is a team engineer or the hiring manager, and which program the requisition sits in, changes what you prepare: a team engineer on a launch vehicle program and a hiring manager on a spacecraft program will pull on different lines of your résumé. Both are free for the asking on this call and much harder to get afterward.
:::

## Summary

| Area | What the recruiter is actually resolving | What a good answer looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Background | Which requisition and level to route you to | Two or three sentences ending on a role family and one piece of evidence |
| Motivation | Whether you have a specific reason to be here | A fact about this team connected to something you did |
| Export-control eligibility | A yes-or-no gate set by law | A direct, short, unapologetic answer |
| Location and relocation | A scheduling and logistics filter | A decision, a date, and any constraint, stated plainly |
| Timeline | Their schedule and yours | Your earliest start; their stages and dates, which you ask for |
| Compensation | Whether expectations and band are compatible | Covered in lesson three; never a number below the posted band |

::: context recruiter-job Who the recruiter is
A **recruiter** finds candidates and steers them through a company's hiring process. An **in-house** recruiter works for the company itself. An **agency** recruiter works for an outside firm that companies pay to find people. Either way, recruiters are usually not engineers, and they often handle several open roles at once. Think of them as the person who knows the whole process and every open seat, but not the math behind the work — which is why they can tell you the schedule and cannot grade your controller.
:::

::: context requisition One opening, one record
A **requisition** — "req" for short, said "reck" — is the company's internal record of one approved opening. It is created before the job is posted and usually fixes the team, the level and the budget. It has an ID number, and the recruiter's notes about you are filed against it. That is why "which requisition am I being considered for?" is a real question: two postings that look alike can belong to different teams, levels and pay ranges.
:::

::: context kalman-filter The estimator every GNC team uses
A **Kalman filter** is a recipe for making the best guess of where a vehicle is and how fast it is moving. It blends two things: a prediction from physics ("we were here, moving this fast, so now we should be there") and noisy sensor readings ("the GPS says we are over here"). It trusts each one according to how reliable it is. Rockets, satellites, aircraft and even phones use versions of it. You will build one later in this course; the recruiter never needs to.
:::

::: context covariance Tracking how unsure you are
**Covariance** is a number — really a small table of numbers — that says how uncertain an estimate is, and in which directions. **Propagating** it means working out how that uncertainty grows as time passes without a new measurement, the way your guess of a friend's position gets fuzzier the longer since they last texted. It is central to navigation work, and exactly the level of detail that belongs on the technical call, not this one.
:::

::: context itar-us-person A reminder from the first module
**ITAR**, the International Traffic in Arms Regulations, controls who may see technical data about rockets and spacecraft. For many roles the company may only hire a **US person**: a US citizen or national, a lawful permanent resident (green card holder), a refugee, or an asylee. On the recruiter screen this is confirmed by your own honest answer; documents are checked later, at onboarding.
:::

::: context pay-band What a pay band looks like
Companies usually attach pay to a **level**, and each level has a **band**: a lowest and a highest figure. Many US postings now print that range.

::: context follow-up When to send the note
A **follow-up note** is a short, polite email asking where things stand after a stage has gone quiet. Sending it too early looks anxious; never sending it can leave you waiting on a process that stalled. The timeline the recruiter gives you is what tells you when "quiet" has become "late". The last lesson of this module covers follow-up etiquette in full.
:::
