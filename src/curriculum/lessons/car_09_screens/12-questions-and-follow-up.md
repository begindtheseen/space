Both calls end the same way: *do you have any questions for me?* It arrives with a few minutes left, and most candidates treat it as the part where the interview is over. It is not. It is a section of the call where you set the agenda, and it is the only part of the entire screening process where the information flows toward you at full rate.

There is also a second thing this lesson has to cover, which is what happens in the silence afterwards — the note you send, when to send it, and what to do in the weeks between one stage and the next. This is the part of the process about which a curriculum can say the least, and it is worth being honest about that immediately: nobody can tell you how long a specific company takes between a phone screen and an onsite, and anyone who prints a number for it is guessing. What this lesson can do is tell you how to obtain that number from the one person who has it, and what to do with it once you have.

## Four questions worth asking the engineer

This module's source material gives four, and they are good, so here they are with what each one actually buys you.

**"What did you ship in the last six months?"** This tells you whether the team ships. It surfaces cadence, scope and whether the work is real — a team that can name three things that went out and a team that talks entirely in terms of ongoing efforts are different places to work. It is also a question engineers enjoy answering, which matters at the end of a call, because a question that produces an animated answer changes the tone you end on.

**"What is the hardest technical problem on the team right now?"** The single most useful question for finding out what the job actually is. Postings describe role families; this describes Tuesday. It also gives you material you can use in later rounds — if the answer is something you have relevant experience with, that is a thread you can pick up in the past-project round or the domain round, and having heard it from an engineer on the team is far better than having guessed it.

**"How does flight readiness review work here?"** Asking this tells the interviewer something about you as well as telling you something about them. It says you know that flight hardware passes through a gate, that evidence has to be assembled for it, and that this is a real part of the job rather than an administrative appendix. What it buys you is the shape of the rigour: what is required before something flies, who signs, and how much of an engineer's time the review process occupies.

**"What makes someone unsuccessful in this seat?"** The most informative of the four, for a structural reason: failure modes are specific in a way success descriptions are not. Ask what makes someone successful and you will get adjectives that are true everywhere — smart, driven, collaborative. Ask what makes someone unsuccessful and you get something concrete, because the interviewer is remembering a particular person or a particular pattern. It is also the question most likely to produce an unrehearsed answer, and unrehearsed answers are where the information is.

::: key
Four questions for the engineer: what did you ship in the last six months; what is the hardest technical problem on the team right now; how does flight readiness review work here; and what makes someone unsuccessful in this seat. All four are specific, and all four give you information you actually need.
:::

## What not to ask, and why each one fails

**Benefits, vacation, working hours.** Not because they do not matter — they matter a great deal — but because the engineer is not the right person and this is not the right stage. These are recruiter questions, the recruiter expects them, and asking the engineer converts your one shot at technical information into an exchange that could have happened in an email.

**"How do you think I did?" or "What are my chances?"** This puts the interviewer in a position they cannot comfortably occupy. They usually do not know — the decision is not theirs alone, and it is made after a debrief they have not attended yet — and even where they have a view, saying it to a candidate is not something they can do. So the question produces a deflection, and the deflection costs you the last minute of the call.

**Yes-or-no questions.** "Do you like working here?" is answered in one word and tells you nothing, because almost nobody answers it in the negative to a candidate. The general rule is to ask questions whose answers cannot be predicted before they are given.

**Anything answerable from the careers page.** Asking what the company does, or how many people work there, reads exactly as it is.

::: warning Two questions, prepared, not ten read off a list
The failure at this point of the call is rarely asking too little. It is arriving with a list and working through it, which turns the last five minutes into a questionnaire. Two questions, asked well, with a real follow-up on each answer, produce far more than five asked in sequence — and the follow-up is the part that makes it a conversation rather than an interview in reverse.
:::

## Follow the answer

The move that makes this section of the call work is the second question — the one you did not prepare, built from what they just said.

If the answer to *what is the hardest problem right now* is something about a flexible mode interacting with a control loop, the follow-up is not to move on to your next prepared item. It is: *"Is that showing up in flight data or in simulation first?"* or *"Is the constraint the sensor location or the actuator bandwidth?"* Those questions demonstrate that you were listening and that you understand the problem well enough to ask about its structure. They also, incidentally, let you show technical judgment without it being a test.

Write down what you hear. These answers are the raw material for the past-project presentation and the behavioural rounds that come later in this track, and for the decision you will eventually have to make about whether to accept.

## After the call: the note

Send a short note, the same day or the next. If you do not have the interviewer's address — and after a phone screen you often will not — send it to the recruiter and ask them to pass it on. That is a normal request.

What goes in it:

- **Thanks**, in a sentence.
- **One specific thing** from the conversation, which is what makes it a note rather than a form letter. The specific thing should be technical, and it should be something that could only have come from that conversation.
- **One correction or addition, if you owe one.** This is the genuinely useful part and the reason to send the note at all. If you gave a weak answer, a brief correct version in writing costs nothing and closes the loop. Two or three sentences; not a re-argument of the whole call.
- **Nothing else.** No restatement of your enthusiasm, no summary of your qualifications, no attachments they did not ask for.

Keep it short. If the note takes longer to read than the part of the interview it refers to, something has gone wrong.

Whether any of this changes an outcome is not something this curriculum knows, and you should be suspicious of anyone who claims otherwise. What can be said from the mechanism: the correction is the only channel you have for fixing a specific wrong answer, it costs you a paragraph, and it is read by someone who is about to write or contribute to a debrief note. That is enough reason to send it without needing to believe anything stronger.

::: key
Send one short note, same day or next, via the recruiter if you do not have the interviewer's address. Thanks, one specific technical thing from the conversation, and — if you owe one — a two-or-three sentence correction of an answer you got wrong. Nothing else. Send it once, to one person.
:::

## Timelines: what is known and what is not

Here is what this curriculum can state. The previous module reports the overall pipeline running roughly five to eight weeks from first contact to decision, across up to about nine distinct touchpoints. That is a figure for the whole process, and it is useful mainly for planning your other applications around rather than sitting idle.

Here is what it cannot state: how long any particular employer takes between a screen and the next stage. That varies by company, by team, by requisition, by how many candidates are in the pipeline, and by things entirely unconnected to you. **This curriculum does not know, and the way to find out is to ask the recruiter on the call and write down the answer.**

Two questions produce everything you need:

> "What are the next steps, and roughly when would they happen?"
>
> "If I have not heard by then, when is a good time to check in?"

The second one is doing the real work. It converts a future silence from an ambiguous, anxious situation into a scheduled event that you have explicit permission to act on. Having asked it, you never have to wonder whether following up is appropriate — you were told when.

If you did not ask, a reasonable default is to wait until any stated date has passed, then send one short check-in, and if nothing comes back, one more after a further interval. Not more than that, and never to several people at once.

::: warning Silence is not information
Pipelines stall for reasons that have nothing to do with the candidate: a requisition put on hold, an interviewer travelling, a headcount decision made two levels above the hiring manager, a recruiter carrying more roles than they can move. Reading a rejection into a slow week costs you real effort — candidates withdraw mentally, stop preparing, and are then genuinely unready when the call comes. Treat the stated timeline as the baseline, treat anything before it as normal, and keep your other applications moving regardless.
:::

::: example The last five minutes, two ways
**Interviewer:** We have got about five minutes — any questions for me?

**Weak version:** "Yes, a few. What is the team culture like? … Okay, great. And what is the work-life balance like here — I have heard the hours can be pretty intense? … Right, that makes sense. And how many people are on the team? … Okay. And I guess, how do you think the interview went? Is there anything you would want to see more of from me?"

**Strong version:** "Two things. First — what did the team ship in the last six months?"

*(interviewer answers: a guidance update that flew, plus a sensor-calibration tool)*

"That is interesting, because the calibration tool sounds like the sort of thing that only gets built when the manual process has already hurt someone. Was it driven by a specific flight issue?"

*(interviewer answers)*

"Second question: what makes someone unsuccessful in this seat? Not what makes someone good — I am more interested in the failure mode."

*(interviewer answers: people who cannot let go of an analysis and ship, and people who do not go and look at the hardware)*

"That is useful, thank you. The second one I have some feel for — the pointing-stage work I mentioned was mostly me in the lab with the thing. The first one I would want to ask more about if we talk again, because I would want to know what 'good enough to ship' looks like here."

**What makes the difference:** the weak version asks four questions and receives almost no information. Culture and work-life balance produce answers the interviewer is not positioned to give candidly at this stage; team size is on a careers page or can be asked of a recruiter; and the last question puts the interviewer in an awkward position and burns the final minute on a deflection. It also reveals a candidate whose main concern at the end of a technical call is how they are being judged.

The strong version asks two and gets four answers, because each one is followed. The follow-up on the calibration tool is the strongest single moment: it shows the candidate inferring something about how the team works from what was shipped, which is a piece of engineering judgment delivered as a question. And the closing response to the failure-mode answer does something subtle and valuable — it takes the two failure modes seriously, claims honest ground on one of them with evidence, and flags the other as an open question rather than pretending it is solved. That is the same honesty discipline the rest of this module has been building, applied to the one part of the call where you are the one asking.
:::

::: example The follow-up note, two ways
**Weak version:**

> Dear [interviewer],
>
> Thank you so much for taking the time to speak with me today. I really enjoyed our conversation and I am extremely excited about the possibility of joining the team. As I mentioned on the call, I have spent the last two years working on precision motion control, where I designed the compensator for a two-axis pointing stage, identified the plant from measured frequency response data, and dealt with a structural resonance at forty hertz. I also built a six-degree-of-freedom launch vehicle simulation with a full Monte Carlo dispersion campaign. I believe these experiences have prepared me well for this role, and I am confident I could make an immediate impact on the team. I am a fast learner and I thrive in fast-paced environments.
>
> Please do not hesitate to reach out if there is anything further you need from me. I look forward to hearing about next steps.

**Strong version:**

> Dear [interviewer],
>
> Thank you for the time today — the calibration-tool story was the most useful thing I have heard about how the team works, and it has stayed with me.
>
> One correction I owe you. When you asked about the steady-state error under a constant disturbance torque, I said the derivative gain would reduce it. That is wrong: in steady state there is no rate for the derivative term to act on, so the offset is the disturbance torque divided by the proportional gain alone, and removing it needs integral action or a higher proportional gain. I knew that and said it badly under time pressure, and I would rather correct it than leave it.
>
> Happy to answer anything further.

**What makes the difference:** the weak note is a résumé restated. Everything in it was already said on the call or is already on the page, it takes a minute to read, and its only new content is two claims about the candidate's character that no note has ever made anyone believe. It also carries a small cost: an interviewer who reads three paragraphs of re-pitch learns that this candidate does not distinguish between what has been established and what has not.

The strong note is four sentences and does exactly two things. The first specific reference proves the note was written for this conversation and not pasted. The correction is the substance: it fixes a wrong technical claim on the record, states the right answer compactly and correctly, and gives an honest account of why the wrong one came out. The last clause — *I knew that and said it badly under time pressure* — is a real risk and the right one to take, because it is true, the interviewer already suspects it, and saying it plainly is more credible than either silence or an elaborate justification.
:::

::: example The check-in after silence
A candidate was told at the phone screen that the team expected to make decisions about onsites "in the next couple of weeks." Three weeks have passed with no contact.

**Weak version:** four separate emails over eight days — one to the recruiter, one to the interviewer, one through a mutual contact on a professional network, and a fourth to the recruiter again apologising for the earlier ones and asking whether there is any news, or whether the silence means the candidate should assume the worst.

**Strong version:** one short email to the recruiter.

> Hi [recruiter] — checking in on the GNC role. When we spoke on the [date] you mentioned decisions on onsites in a couple of weeks, so I wanted to see where things stand. No rush if the timeline has moved, I would just like to know roughly where I am.
>
> One thing worth flagging: I have a final round elsewhere the week of the [date], and I would rather make a decision with full information if that is possible.

**What makes the difference:** the weak version treats silence as information and responds to it four times, across three channels, including one that routes around the recruiter. Each message individually is harmless and the sequence is not: it reads as someone who will be difficult to manage under uncertainty, which is a real signal for a job in which schedules slip constantly and the correct response is to keep working.

The strong version is one message, to the right person, anchored to something specific the recruiter actually said, and explicitly generous about the timeline having moved. The second paragraph is the part most candidates omit out of fear of seeming demanding, and it is the most useful sentence in the email: a competing deadline is concrete, actionable information that a recruiter can use to compress a schedule if they want to. Withholding it guarantees they cannot.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
Why does "what makes someone unsuccessful in this seat?" produce better information than "what makes someone successful here?"
:::

::: answer
Because failure modes are specific and success descriptions are generic. Asked what makes someone successful, an interviewer produces adjectives that would be true at any employer — smart, driven, collaborative — which cannot distinguish this team from any other. Asked about failure, they reach for a particular person or a particular recurring pattern, so the answer is concrete and local: an actual behaviour that has actually caused trouble there. It is also less likely to be a rehearsed answer, and unrehearsed answers are where the information is.
:::

::: check
Name three questions you should not ask the engineer conducting your technical screen, and give the specific reason each one fails.
:::

::: answer
Benefits, vacation or hours: the engineer is not the right person and the recruiter is, so asking spends your one opportunity for technical information on an exchange that belongs in an email. "How did I do?" or "what are my chances?": the interviewer usually does not know, since the decision follows a debrief they have not yet attended, and could not say so to a candidate even if they did — the question buys a deflection and costs you the last minute. Yes-or-no questions such as "do you like working here?": the answer is predictable before it is given, and almost nobody answers it negatively to a candidate, so no information is transferred.
:::

::: check
What is the single most useful question to ask about timelines, and why does it matter more than asking how long the process takes?
:::

::: answer
"If I have not heard by then, when is a good time to check in?" It matters more because it converts a future silence from an ambiguous situation into a scheduled event you have explicit permission to act on. Knowing the overall duration tells you how to plan your other applications, but it does not tell you whether a quiet fortnight is normal for this employer or whether following up is welcome. The check-in question settles both in advance, from the only person who knows — and this curriculum cannot supply per-stage timings for any employer, so asking is the method rather than a shortcut.
:::

::: check
What are the three things a follow-up note should contain, and which one is the actual reason to send it?
:::

::: answer
Thanks in a sentence; one specific technical thing from the conversation that could not have come from anywhere else; and, if you owe one, a two-or-three sentence correction of an answer you got wrong. The correction is the reason to send it: it is the only channel available for fixing a specific wrong claim on the record, it costs a paragraph, and it reaches someone who is about to contribute to a debrief. Whether the note changes an outcome is not something this curriculum knows, and the mechanism is sufficient justification without needing to believe anything stronger.
:::

::: check
Why is reading a rejection into two weeks of silence a mistake that costs you something real?
:::

::: answer
Because pipelines stall for reasons unconnected to the candidate — a requisition placed on hold, an interviewer travelling, a headcount decision taken well above the hiring manager, a recruiter carrying too many roles — so silence carries almost no information about your standing. The cost is concrete rather than emotional: a candidate who concludes they have been rejected stops preparing, disengages, and is genuinely unready when the next call does come. The correct handling is to treat any stated timeline as the baseline, treat anything before it as normal, send at most one check-in after it passes, and keep the other applications moving throughout.
:::

::: check
You mention a competing final round to a recruiter during a check-in. Why is this useful information rather than a demand?
:::

::: answer
Because it is concrete and actionable. A recruiter managing a pipeline can sometimes compress a schedule, escalate a decision, or bring an onsite forward, but only if they know there is a reason to — and they cannot know unless you tell them. Stated plainly, with a date and without an ultimatum, it is ordinary scheduling information of the same kind as your availability or your notice period. Withholding it out of a fear of seeming demanding guarantees that nothing can be done about it, and leaves you making a decision on one offer with no information about the other.
:::

## Summary

| Moment | Do | Do not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| End of the technical call | Two prepared questions, each followed up | Work through a list of ten |
| Choosing questions | Shipped work, hardest problem, flight readiness, failure modes | Benefits, your own odds, yes-or-no questions |
| Before hanging up | Ask next steps, timing, and when to check in | Leave the timeline unasked |
| Same day or next | One short note: thanks, one specific, one correction | Restate the résumé; send to several people |
| After the stated date | One check-in to the recruiter; flag any competing deadline | Multiple messages across multiple channels |
| During any silence | Keep other applications moving | Infer a rejection |
