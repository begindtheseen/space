At the end of a tour of a new school, the guide asks, "Any questions?" Most people say "no, thanks" and leave. But that is the one moment someone who knows the place will answer anything you ask.

Both screening calls end the same way: *do you have any questions for me?* Most candidates treat it as the part where the interview is over. It is not. It is where you set the agenda, and the only part of the screening process where information flows toward *you* at full speed.

This lesson also covers the silence afterward: the note you send, and the weeks between stages. An honest limit: nobody can tell you how long a specific company takes between a phone screen and the next stage. This lesson shows you how to get that number from the one person who has it.

## Four questions worth asking the engineer

This module's flashcards give four, and here is what each buys you.

**"What did you ship in the last six months?"** To **[[ship|ship-word]]** something is to finish it and put it into real use. The answer shows whether the team ships at all — its pace, its scope, whether the work is real. A team that names three things that went out and one that talks only about "ongoing efforts" are different places to work. Engineers enjoy answering it, which lifts the ending.

**"What is the hardest technical problem on the team right now?"** The best question for learning what the job is. A posting describes a role family; this answer describes an ordinary Tuesday. It also gives you material: if the answer touches your experience, that is a thread for the past-project or domain round — and hearing it from an engineer beats guessing.

**"How does flight readiness review work here?"** A **[[flight readiness review|frr-gate]]** is the formal meeting where a team shows the evidence that something is safe to fly. Asking tells the interviewer you know flight hardware passes through a gate, that evidence must be built for it, and that this is real engineering, not paperwork. You learn the shape of their rigor — what is needed before something flies, who signs, and how much engineer time it takes.

**"What makes someone unsuccessful in this seat?"** The most informative of the four, because failure is specific in a way success is not. Ask about success and you get adjectives true everywhere — smart, driven, collaborative. Ask about failure and the interviewer remembers a particular person or pattern. It is also the question most likely to get an unrehearsed answer, and unrehearsed answers are where the information is.

::: key
Four questions for the engineer: what did you ship in the last six months; what is the hardest technical problem on the team right now; how does flight readiness review work here; and what makes someone unsuccessful in this seat. All four are specific, and all four give you information you actually need.
:::

## What not to ask, and why each one fails

**Benefits, vacation, working hours.** They matter — but the engineer is the wrong person, at the wrong stage. These are questions for the **recruiter** (the company's hiring organizer), who expects them. Asking the engineer spends your one chance at technical information on something an email could handle.

**"How do you think I did?" or "What are my chances?"** This puts the interviewer somewhere they cannot comfortably stand. Usually they do not know: the decision is not theirs alone, and it comes after a **[[debrief|debrief-meeting]]** they have not attended yet. Even if they have a view, they cannot share it with a candidate. So you get a polite dodge, and the dodge costs you the last minute.

**Yes-or-no questions.** "Do you like working here?" gets one word and tells you nothing, because almost nobody says no to a candidate. The rule: ask questions whose answers you cannot predict before you hear them.

**Anything on the careers page.** Asking what the company does reads exactly as it sounds: you did not look.

::: warning Two questions, prepared, not ten read off a list
The failure here is rarely asking too little. It is arriving with a list and working through it, which turns the last five minutes into a questionnaire. Two questions asked well, each with a real follow-up, get far more than five asked in a row. The follow-up is what makes it a conversation instead of an interview in reverse.
:::

## Follow the answer

What makes this part work is the *second* question — the one you did not prepare, built from what they just said.

Say the answer to "what is the hardest problem right now?" is about a **[[flexible mode|flexible-mode]]** interacting with a control loop. Do not jump to your next prepared item. Follow up: *"Is that showing up in flight data or in simulation first?"* or *"Is the constraint the sensor location or the actuator bandwidth?"* Those show you were listening and understand the problem's structure — technical judgment shown without a test.

Write down what you hear, for later rounds and for deciding whether to accept.

## After the call: the note

Send a short note, the same day or the next. If you do not have the interviewer's address — common after a phone screen — send it to the recruiter and ask them to pass it on; that is normal. It holds:

- **Thanks**, in one sentence.
- **One specific thing** from the conversation — technical, and something that could only have come from that call. This makes it a note, not a form letter.
- **One correction or addition, if you owe one.** The truly useful part, and the reason to send it. If you gave a weak answer, a short correct version in writing closes the loop. Two or three sentences, not a re-argument of the call.
- **Nothing else.** No restated enthusiasm, no summary of your qualifications, no attachments they did not ask for.

If the note takes longer to read than the part of the interview it refers to, something has gone wrong.

Does it change an outcome? This curriculum does not know; distrust anyone who claims to. What is clear: the correction is your only channel for fixing a specific wrong answer, it costs a paragraph, and it is read by someone about to contribute to the debrief. That is reason enough.

::: key
Send one short note, same day or next, via the recruiter if you do not have the interviewer's address. Thanks, one specific technical thing from the conversation, and — if you owe one — a two-or-three sentence correction of an answer you got wrong. Nothing else. Send it once, to one person.
:::

## Timelines: what is known and what is not

What this curriculum can state: the previous module reports the whole pipeline running roughly five to eight weeks from first contact to decision, across up to about nine distinct touchpoints. That is for the whole process, and mainly useful for planning your other applications rather than sitting idle.

What it cannot state: how long any particular employer takes between a screen and the next stage. That varies by company, team, **[[requisition|requisition-word]]**, how many candidates are in the pipeline, and things entirely unconnected to you. **This curriculum does not know, and the way to find out is to ask the recruiter on the call and write down the answer.**

Two questions get you everything you need:

> "What are the next steps, and roughly when would they happen?"
>
> "If I have not heard by then, when is a good time to check in?"

The second does the real work: it turns a future silence into a **[[scheduled event|check-in-timeline]]** you have permission to act on. You were told when, so you never wonder whether following up is welcome.

If you did not ask, a reasonable default: wait until any stated date passes, send one short check-in, and if nothing comes back, one more after a further interval. No more, and never to several people at once.

::: warning Silence is not information
Pipelines stall for reasons that have nothing to do with you: a requisition on hold, an interviewer traveling, a **[[headcount|headcount-word]]** decision made two levels above the hiring manager, a recruiter with too many roles. Reading rejection into a slow week costs something real: candidates pull back, stop preparing, and are unready when the call comes. Treat the stated timeline as the baseline, anything before it as normal, and keep other applications moving.
:::

::: example The last five minutes, two ways
**Interviewer:** We have got about five minutes — any questions for me?

**Weak version:** "Yes, a few. What is the team culture like? … Okay, great. And what is the work-life balance like here — I have heard the hours can be pretty intense? … Right, that makes sense. And how many people are on the team? … Okay. And I guess, how do you think the interview went? Is there anything you would want to see more of from me?"

**Strong version:** "Two things. First — what did the team ship in the last six months?"

*(interviewer answers: a guidance update that flew, plus a sensor-calibration tool)*

"That is interesting, because the calibration tool sounds like the sort of thing that only gets built when the manual process has already hurt someone. Was it driven by a specific flight issue?"

*(interviewer answers)*

"Second question: what makes someone unsuccessful in this seat? Not what makes someone good — I am more interested in the failure mode."

*(interviewer answers: people who cannot let go of an analysis and ship, and people who do not go and look at the hardware)*

"That is useful, thank you. The second one I have some feel for — the pointing-stage work I mentioned was mostly me in the lab with the thing. The first one I would want to ask more about if we talk again, because I would want to know what 'good enough to ship' looks like here."

**What makes the difference:** the weak version asks four questions and learns almost nothing: culture and work-life balance cannot be answered candidly now, team size is a careers-page or recruiter question, and the last one burns the final minute on a dodge. The strong version asks two and gets four answers, because each is followed up. The calibration-tool follow-up is engineering judgment delivered as a question. The close claims honest ground on one failure mode and flags the other as open — this module's honesty, applied where you are the one asking.
:::

::: example The follow-up note, two ways
**Weak version:**

> Dear [interviewer],
>
> Thank you so much for taking the time to speak with me today. I really enjoyed our conversation and I am extremely excited about the possibility of joining the team. As I mentioned on the call, I have spent the last two years working on precision motion control, where I designed the compensator for a two-axis pointing stage, identified the plant from measured frequency response data, and dealt with a structural resonance at forty hertz. I also built a six-degree-of-freedom launch vehicle simulation with a full Monte Carlo dispersion campaign. I believe these experiences have prepared me well for this role, and I am confident I could make an immediate impact on the team. I am a fast learner and I thrive in fast-paced environments.
>
> Please do not hesitate to reach out if there is anything further you need from me. I look forward to hearing about next steps.

**Strong version:**

> Dear [interviewer],
>
> Thank you for the time today — the calibration-tool story was the most useful thing I have heard about how the team works, and it has stayed with me.
>
> One correction I owe you. When you asked about the **[[steady-state error|steady-state-offset]]** under a constant disturbance torque, I said the derivative gain would reduce it. That is wrong: in steady state there is no rate for the derivative term to act on, so the offset is the disturbance torque divided by the proportional gain alone. A higher proportional gain shrinks it; removing it needs integral action. I knew that and said it badly under time pressure, and I would rather correct it than leave it.
>
> Happy to answer anything further.

**What makes the difference:** the weak note is a resume restated. Everything in it was already said or is already on the page, and its only new content is two claims about the candidate's character that no note has ever made anyone believe. The strong note is seven sentences and does two things. The specific reference proves it was written for this call. The correction fixes a wrong technical claim on the record, compactly, and honestly says why it came out wrong — *I knew that and said it badly under time pressure* is a risk worth taking, because it is true, the interviewer probably suspects it, and plain beats silence or an elaborate excuse.
:::

::: example The check-in after silence
At the phone screen, the candidate was told the team expected to decide on onsites "in the next couple of weeks." Three weeks have passed with no contact.

**Weak version:** four emails over eight days — to the recruiter, to the interviewer, through a mutual contact on a professional network, and to the recruiter again, apologizing for the others and asking whether the silence means they should assume the worst.

**Strong version:** one short email to the recruiter.

> Hi [recruiter] — checking in on the GNC role. When we spoke on the [date] you mentioned decisions on onsites in a couple of weeks, so I wanted to see where things stand. No rush if the timeline has moved, I would like to know roughly where I am.
>
> One thing worth flagging: I have a final round elsewhere the week of the [date], and I would rather make a decision with full information if that is possible.

**What makes the difference:** the weak version treats silence as information and answers it four times, across three channels, one going around the recruiter. Together they read as someone hard to work with under uncertainty, in a job where schedules slip constantly. The strong version is one message, to the right person, tied to what the recruiter said, and generous about the timeline moving. Its second paragraph is the part most candidates leave out for fear of seeming demanding, and the most useful sentence in it. A **[[competing deadline|competing-offer]]** is concrete information a recruiter can use to speed up a schedule if they want to. Holding it back guarantees they cannot.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
Why does "what makes someone unsuccessful in this seat?" produce better information than "what makes someone successful here?"
:::

::: answer
Failure modes are specific; success descriptions are generic. Asked about success, an interviewer gives adjectives true anywhere — smart, driven, collaborative. Asked about failure, they think of a particular person or pattern, so the answer is concrete and local: a behavior that has really caused trouble there. It is also less likely to be rehearsed, and unrehearsed answers are where the information is.
:::

::: check
Name three questions you should not ask the engineer running your technical screen, and give the specific reason each one fails.
:::

::: answer
Benefits, vacation or hours: those belong to the recruiter, so asking the engineer wastes your one chance at technical information. "How did I do?" or "what are my chances?": the interviewer usually does not know, since the decision follows a debrief they have not attended, and could not say if they did — you get a dodge and lose the last minute. Yes-or-no questions like "do you like working here?": almost nobody says no to a candidate, so you learn nothing.
:::

::: check
What is the single most useful question to ask about timelines, and why does it matter more than asking how long the process takes?
:::

::: answer
"If I have not heard by then, when is a good time to check in?" It turns a future silence into a scheduled event you have permission to act on. The overall duration helps plan other applications but does not say whether a quiet two weeks is normal here, or whether following up is welcome. The check-in question settles both, from the only person who knows — and since no curriculum can supply per-stage timings for an employer, asking is the method, not a shortcut.
:::

::: check
What are the three things a follow-up note should contain, and which one is the real reason to send it?
:::

::: answer
Thanks in a sentence; one specific technical thing that could only have come from that conversation; and, if you owe one, a two-or-three sentence correction of an answer you got wrong. The correction is the reason: the only channel for fixing a wrong claim on the record, at the cost of a paragraph, read by someone about to contribute to a debrief. Whether it changes an outcome is unknown; the mechanism is justification enough.
:::

::: check
Why is reading a rejection into two weeks of silence a mistake that costs you something real?
:::

::: answer
Pipelines stall for reasons unconnected to you — a requisition on hold, an interviewer traveling, a headcount decision far above the hiring manager — so silence says almost nothing about where you stand. The cost is practical: a candidate who decides they were rejected stops preparing and is unready when the next call comes. Treat the stated timeline as the baseline, send at most one check-in after it passes, and keep other applications moving.
:::

::: check
You mention a competing final round to a recruiter during a check-in. Why is this useful information rather than a demand?
:::

::: answer
It is concrete and actionable. A recruiter can sometimes speed up a schedule or bring an onsite forward — but only if they know there is a reason, and only you can tell them. Stated plainly, with a date and no ultimatum, it is ordinary scheduling information, like your availability. Holding it back guarantees nothing can be done, and leaves you deciding on one offer with no information about the other.
:::

## Summary

| Moment | Do | Do not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| End of the technical call | Two prepared questions, each followed up | Work through a list of ten |
| Choosing questions | Shipped work, hardest problem, flight readiness, failure modes | Benefits, your own odds, yes-or-no questions |
| Before hanging up | Ask next steps, timing, and when to check in | Leave the timeline unasked |
| Same day or next | One short note: thanks, one specific, one correction | Restate the resume; send to several people |
| After the stated date | One check-in to the recruiter; flag any competing deadline | Multiple messages across multiple channels |
| During any silence | Keep other applications moving | Infer a rejection |

::: context ship-word Where "ship" comes from
Software engineers borrowed "ship" from the days when finished products literally went out in boxes on trucks and ships. Now it means released: code merged and running, a tool in use, hardware delivered or flown. In aerospace, "what did you ship?" is a fast way to separate work that reached a vehicle or a customer from work that is still a slide.
:::

::: context frr-gate The last gate before launch
Big space programs pass through a series of formal reviews, each one a gate the design must clear before the work moves on. NASA's version runs from early design reviews through testing to the **flight readiness review**, the final formal check before launch. Commercial launch companies run their own versions, often faster and less formal, but the idea — show the evidence before anything flies — is the same.

::: context debrief-meeting Where the decision is actually made
After a candidate's interviews, the people who met them usually each write up feedback, and then the team compares notes — often in a meeting called a **debrief** — before deciding who moves forward. One interviewer's opinion is only one input. That is why asking "how did I do?" at the end of a call puts them in an impossible position: the verdict does not exist yet.
:::

::: context flexible-mode When the rocket bends
Engineers often model a vehicle as a perfectly stiff block. Real ones bend. A long, thin rocket flexes like a diving board, at its own natural frequencies — its **flexible modes**. If a sensor sits where the body bends, it measures the bending as if it were the vehicle turning, and the controller may push back at the wrong moment and make the wobble worse.

::: context requisition-word What a "req" is
A **requisition** — usually shortened to "req", said "reck" — is an approved, budgeted job opening. Recruiters talk about "working a req" or a "req on hold". If the budget behind it is paused or moved, the req freezes, and every candidate in it waits, however well they did. That is one of the most common reasons for silence that has nothing to do with you.
:::

::: context check-in-timeline A silence with a schedule
Asking "when should I check in?" turns the weeks after a call into a plan. Everything before the stated date is normal quiet. After it, one short message; after a further wait, at most one more.

::: context headcount-word Heads, counted
**Headcount** is the number of people a team or company is allowed to employ, set in its budget. Hiring managers usually cannot add a person without headcount approved above them. When budgets tighten, headcount can be frozen or cut suddenly, and open roles stall mid-process — another silence that says nothing about the candidates.
:::

::: context steady-state-offset Why the derivative gain cannot help
Model the spacecraft's pointing error $\theta$ with inertia $J$, a PD controller with gains $K_p$ and $K_d$, and a constant disturbance torque $T_d$:

$$J\ddot{\theta} = T_d - K_p\theta - K_d\dot{\theta}$$

At steady state nothing is changing, so $\ddot{\theta} = 0$ and $\dot{\theta} = 0$. The derivative term vanishes, leaving $0 = T_d - K_p\theta$, so $\theta_{ss} = T_d / K_p$. For $T_d = 0.001\,\mathrm{N\,m}$ and $K_p = 0.5\,\mathrm{N\,m/rad}$ that is $0.002\,\mathrm{rad}$, about $0.11^\circ$. Doubling $K_p$ halves it; only an integral term, which keeps growing while any error remains, drives it to zero.
:::

::: context competing-offer Why a deadline elsewhere helps both sides
Companies hiring engineers often compete for the same people. A recruiter who learns you have a final round, or an offer with a deadline, elsewhere can sometimes move an onsite forward or speed up a decision — which is good for them if they want you. Say it as a plain fact with a date, never as a threat, and never invent one: recruiters talk, and a made-up deadline is a bluff like any other.
:::
