Every fact this module has to offer is now on the table: the degree line, what this curriculum does and does not build, the three real paths, and their honest timelines. What remains is the actual decision — choosing one of the three deliberately, recognizing the specific trap that keeps people from ever really choosing, and understanding what changes about all of this for a reader who is not starting from zero but is already years into a different career. This is the module's last lesson, and it closes by turning everything before it into something you can actually act on this week.

## The sunk-cost trap, named precisely

A sunk cost is time or money already spent that cannot be recovered no matter what you decide next. The trap is treating that unrecoverable spending as a reason to continue a plan, when the only thing that should determine what to do next is a clear-eyed comparison of the paths still available from today forward. Consider a reader eighteen months into a slow, part-time Path 1 degree who discovers, partway through, that Path 2 would likely have suited their background better. The eighteen months are gone regardless of what happens next — staying on Path 1 does not get them back, and switching does not lose them twice. The only real question is which remaining path is shorter or better from this point forward, and answering that honestly sometimes means switching after real investment, which feels like a loss even when it is the better decision, because the eighteen months feel like they demand justification through completion rather than through the quality of the choice made with them.

The same trap runs the other direction. A reader on Path 3 who watches their chosen company go through a difficult stretch may stay purely out of identity or momentum — having already told colleagues and family "I work in NewSpace now" — rather than a clear-eyed read of what the company's situation actually means for the remaining path. In both directions, the trap has the same shape: treating unrecoverable past investment as though it changes the arithmetic of the choice in front of you, when it does not.

## Choosing deliberately, not by default

This module's own exercises ask you to write a one-page plan for each of the three paths as they apply to you specifically, with dated milestones across the next thirty-six months, an explicit cost in money and hours per week, and the single assumption that would break the plan — then choose one, state why in two sentences, and state the condition under which you would switch. That last piece is the actual defense against the sunk-cost trap, and it is worth understanding why it works. Deciding your switching condition before you start converts a future, emotionally loaded decision into a pre-committed rule made while you were thinking clearly, before any investment existed to feel defensive about. A reader who decided in advance "I will reassess if I am not within six months of SRE, GNC's basic qualifications by month thirty" is checking a fact against a plan, not weighing a sunk cost against an uncertain future — the emotional work of the decision was already done at the point where nothing had been spent yet.

"Choosing by default" usually means one of two things, both of which are real choices with real costs even though neither feels like a decision being actively made: staying on whatever path you happened to start without ever revisiting it, or never starting any of the three while continuing to study broadly and telling yourself the choice is still open. The second is the more common trap for a reader who has absorbed this module carefully — it feels responsible, even diligent, and it quietly costs exactly the years this module has spent six lessons insisting are real.

::: key
A sunk cost is unrecoverable regardless of what you choose next, so it should not weigh in the comparison between the paths still available from today. The eighteen months, or the two years, or the company loyalty already spent are identical under every option going forward — only the remaining time differs, and that is the only thing worth comparing.
:::

::: key
Stating your switching condition before you start — in writing, tied to a specific date and a specific fact you can check — converts a future emotional decision into a pre-committed rule, which is the actual, practical defense against the sunk-cost trap.
:::

## If you are already mid-career in another field

Two genuinely different situations hide inside "mid-career in another field," and this module's earlier material already gives you the tool to tell them apart: check the existing bachelor's degree's field against the basic-qualifications lines from the first lesson.

If the existing degree is already in a qualifying field — an engineering discipline, physics, or computer science, even one far from GNC, such as civil, electrical, or chemical engineering — the degree-field filter is already cleared for most postings. The actual gap for this reader is almost never the credential; it is demonstrated, relevant professional experience in the specific things GNC postings count: control systems, orbital mechanics, classical dynamics, aerodynamics, sensors and actuators, modeling and simulation. Years of senior experience in an unrelated engineering specialty do not automatically supply that, however substantial the seniority. For this reader, Path 1's degree half is usually the wrong, expensive answer to a problem the first degree already solved — a second bachelor's degree repeats work that is not the actual gap. Path 2, Path 3, or an aggressive internal move within a current engineering employer toward a controls- or dynamics-adjacent team, combined with this curriculum's own demonstrated work, is usually the shorter, more honest route.

If the existing degree is in a genuinely non-qualifying field — business, biology, finance, and similar — the field filter is real and unmet, and the accurate options narrow to three. A full engineering, physics, or computer science bachelor's degree through Path 1 remains available, usually the longest version of it since it starts from zero in-major credits, though community college transfer of general-education credits can still shorten it somewhat. An accredited master's in a qualifying field, the conversion route the third lesson named, is often faster than a second bachelor's for a reader with a strong existing quantitative foundation, typically on the order of one and a half to two and a half years, though it still costs real tuition and still does not supply GNC-relevant professional experience on its own. And — worth stating plainly, because it is easy to miss — Site Reliability Engineer, GNC's 4-year in-lieu-of-a-degree branch names no field requirement for the prior degree at all, and no requirement that a degree exist in the first place; a reader with six years of qualifying SRE or DevOps experience, regardless of what their existing degree is in or whether one exists, clears that specific line on the years and the Linux and Python thresholds alone. Of everything this module describes, it is the one door that does not ask what field you started in.

::: warning Years of unrelated professional experience do not count toward a GNC experience line
The single most common misreading this lesson exists to correct: "I have fifteen years of professional experience" is not the same claim as "I have fifteen years of professional experience in GNC or software engineering." Sr. GNC Engineer's 5-year and 7-year branches count experience in the specific domains the posting names, not career tenure in general, however senior. A reader should audit their actual years against the specific domains a posting counts, not against their total time in the workforce.
:::

## What mid-career changes, and what it does not

A reader entering this field mid-career brings real, genuine advantages worth naming alongside the gap: professional discipline built over years of real deadlines, a proven ability to ship work under real constraints, often stronger software and systems fundamentals than a new graduate has had time to build, and frequently more financial stability to fund a slow, steady, part-time path without the acute pressure a 22-year-old just out of school may feel. None of this closes the specific gap this lesson just named, but all of it is real capital this module's earlier lessons did not have room to credit.

The genuine costs are different in shape from a younger reader's, not larger in some general sense. Multi-year timelines cost more against a shorter remaining career runway than the identical years would cost someone earlier in their working life. Family and financial obligations frequently make a slow, part-time shape the only realistic one — which is exactly the shape every path in this module was built to accommodate, and nothing about choosing that pace deserves an apology.

::: key
A qualifying existing degree in an unrelated engineering, physics, or computer-science field already clears the field filter — the real gap is domain-specific professional experience, not the credential. A non-qualifying existing degree leaves the field filter genuinely open, and SRE, GNC's 4-year in-lieu branch is the one door in this module that asks nothing at all about what field, or whether, that existing degree is in.
:::

::: example A sunk-cost decision, worked through with real numbers
A reader is eighteen months into a four-year Path 1 plan and discovers a Path 2 route toward SRE, GNC that, starting fresh today given their actual current software background, would take about two more years to reach a credible application.

Staying on Path 1 from today leaves $4 - 1.5 = 2.5$ years remaining. Switching to Path 2 today leaves 2 years remaining, a difference of $2.5 - 2 = 0.5$ years in Path 2's favor. The eighteen months already spent on Path 1 appear in neither number — they are gone under both options, which is exactly why they do not belong in the comparison. The only fact that should drive the decision is that one remaining path is half a year shorter than the other from today forward, not how much has already been invested in the option that is now the slower one.
:::

::: example Two mid-career starting points, and why they diverge immediately
Reader A holds a bachelor's degree in electrical engineering and has spent ten years in power-grid systems engineering, unrelated to GNC. Checked against the first lesson's basic-qualifications lines, the degree field clears every posting in this module without issue — electrical engineering is squarely an engineering discipline. The ten years, however, are not professional experience in control systems, dynamics, or simulation, so they do not advance Sr. GNC Engineer's 5-year or 7-year branches at all; this reader's honest gap is domain-specific GNC experience, best closed through Path 2, Path 3, or an internal move, paired with this curriculum's demonstrated work.

Reader B holds a bachelor's degree in business administration and has spent six years as a DevOps engineer, the last four of them working daily with Linux and Python in a site-reliability capacity. Checked against SRE, GNC's basic qualifications specifically, the business degree is irrelevant to the in-lieu branch, and the four years of qualifying SRE/DevOps experience, alongside Linux and Python each comfortably past the one-year threshold, clear that posting's basic qualifications today, on paper, regardless of the degree field. Same starting distance from GNC engineering in an informal sense; two entirely different formal positions, because one reader's existing degree already clears a field filter that the other reader's specific target role never applied in the first place.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
Define sunk cost precisely, and explain why it should not factor into a decision between two paths going forward, even when a large amount of time has already been invested in one of them.
:::

::: answer
A sunk cost is time or money already spent that cannot be recovered regardless of what is chosen next. It should not factor into the forward-looking decision because it is identical under every option available from today — already spent whether you stay or switch — so it cannot distinguish between the remaining choices. Only the time or cost still ahead under each option differs between the choices, which is the only thing a rational comparison should weigh.
:::

::: check
Explain why stating a switching condition before starting a path is a more effective defense against the sunk-cost trap than simply "trying to be objective" once already partway through.
:::

::: answer
A switching condition set in advance is decided before any investment exists to feel defensive about, so it is a clean, unemotional rule rather than a live decision made under the pull of already-spent time and effort. Trying to be objective partway through asks you to overcome the sunk-cost pull in the moment it is strongest; a pre-committed condition avoids that fight entirely by turning the later decision into checking a fact against a rule made earlier, when the fact could not yet bias the rule.
:::

::: check
A reader holds a bachelor's degree in mechanical engineering and twelve years of experience in manufacturing process engineering. Using this lesson's framework, identify what is and is not already solved for this reader.
:::

::: answer
Mechanical engineering is an engineering discipline, so the degree-field filter this module's first lesson described is already cleared for essentially every posting in this module. What is not solved is domain-specific professional GNC experience: twelve years in manufacturing process engineering does not count toward Sr. GNC Engineer's 5-year or 7-year branches, which require experience in control systems, dynamics, sensors, or simulation specifically. The honest next step is building that specific experience, through Path 2, Path 3, or an internal move, not re-earning a credential this reader's existing degree already provides.
:::

::: check
Explain precisely why Site Reliability Engineer, GNC's in-lieu-of-a-degree branch is described in this lesson as "field-agnostic," and why that makes it worth naming specifically for a reader whose existing degree is in a non-qualifying field.
:::

::: answer
The 4-year in-lieu branch states a requirement on years and kind of experience — professional software development with site reliability or DevOps experience — and, unlike the degree-and-experience branch beside it, states no requirement on what field a prior degree is in, or that one exists at all. For a reader whose existing degree is in a genuinely non-qualifying field such as business or biology, this is the one door in the whole module where that degree's field is simply not part of what is being checked, making it worth naming explicitly rather than leaving this reader to assume every route requires re-earning a qualifying bachelor's first.
:::

::: check
A reader chose Path 3 two years ago and has just learned their employer is undergoing a significant contraction. Using this lesson's discussion of sunk cost and the previous lesson's discussion of business risk, what is the accurate way to think through what happens next?
:::

::: answer
The two years of genuine GNC experience already gained are real and portable regardless of what happens to the employer, so they are not lost and do not need to be protected by staying loyal to a company whose situation has changed — that would be exactly the sunk-cost trap this lesson describes, misapplied to loyalty rather than to a study plan. The accurate approach is to treat the two years as real progress already banked, and to make the next decision — another Path 3 company, Path 2, or another route — based on where each option leaves the reader from today forward, not on a felt obligation to the company the two years happened to be spent at.
:::

::: check
Why does this module treat "never choosing any of the three paths while continuing to study broadly" as a real cost, rather than as a safely neutral, still-undecided position?
:::

::: answer
Every one of the three paths takes years, as the previous lesson established in detail, and years spent without committing to one of them are years during which none of the three actually advances — the credential is not being earned, the qualifying experience is not accumulating, and the internal track record is not being built. That feels safer than choosing because no specific plan can be judged as the wrong one, but it is not neutral: it is a choice with the same kind of real, unrecoverable time cost as choosing badly, simply less visible because no single decision appears to have caused it.
:::
