Imagine you have paid for a movie ticket, and forty minutes in you realize the film is bad. Do you stay? Many people do, "because I paid for it." But the money is gone whether you stay or leave. The only real question is whether the next eighty minutes are better spent in the seat or somewhere else.

Every fact this module has to offer is now on the table: the degree line, what this curriculum does and does not build, the three real paths, and their honest timelines. What remains is the decision itself. This lesson covers three things:

- how to choose one of the three paths **deliberately**;
- the specific trap — the movie-ticket trap — that keeps people from ever really choosing, or from changing course when they should;
- what changes about all of this for a reader who is not starting from zero, but is already years into a different career.

This is the module's last lesson. It ends by turning everything before it into something you can act on this week.

## The sunk-cost trap, named precisely

A **[[sunk cost|sunk-cost-word]]** is time or money already spent that cannot be recovered, no matter what you decide next. The movie ticket is a sunk cost.

The **sunk-cost trap** is treating that unrecoverable spending as a reason to keep going with a plan. The only thing that should decide what to do next is an honest comparison of the paths still open to you, **from today forward**.

Here is how it looks in this module. A reader is eighteen months into a slow, part-time Path 1 degree. Partway through, they discover that Path 2 would probably have suited their background better.

- The eighteen months are gone no matter what happens next.
- Staying on Path 1 does not get them back.
- Switching does not lose them a second time.

So the only real question is: **which remaining path is shorter, or better, from this point on?** Answering that honestly sometimes means switching after real investment. That feels like a loss, even when it is the better decision. The eighteen months seem to demand to be "justified" by finishing — rather than by how good the choice made from here is.

The same trap runs in the other direction. A reader on Path 3 watches their company go through a hard stretch. They may stay purely out of identity or momentum — they have already told colleagues and family "I work in NewSpace now" — rather than an honest reading of what the company's situation means for the path ahead. That is sunk cost too, paid in loyalty and pride instead of months.

In both directions the trap has the same shape: treating past investment you cannot get back as if it changed the arithmetic of the choice in front of you. It does not.

::: key
A sunk cost is unrecoverable regardless of what you choose next, so it should not weigh in the comparison between the paths still available from today. The eighteen months, or the two years, or the company loyalty already spent are identical under every option going forward — only the remaining time differs, and that is the only thing worth comparing.
:::

::: note Why it has to be true
Write the total time of each option as "time already spent" plus "time still to go."

$$
\text{total}_A = S + R_A, \qquad \text{total}_B = S + R_B
$$

Here $S$ (read "S") is the sunk time, the same under both options because it has already happened. $R_A$ and $R_B$ are the remaining times. Subtract one total from the other:

$$
\text{total}_A - \text{total}_B = (S + R_A) - (S + R_B) = R_A - R_B
$$

The $S$ cancels. Whatever its size — six months or six years — it cannot change which option is shorter. Only $R_A$ and $R_B$ can.
:::

::: example A sunk-cost decision, worked through with real numbers
**The situation.** A reader is eighteen months into a four-year Path 1 plan. They find a Path 2 route toward SRE, GNC that — starting fresh today, given their actual current software background — would take about two more years to reach a credible application.

**Step 1: remaining time if they stay.** Eighteen months is $1.5$ years. Four years minus that:

$$
4 - 1.5 = 2.5 \text{ years remaining on Path 1}
$$

**Step 2: remaining time if they switch.** Given directly: $2$ years remaining on Path 2.

**Step 3: compare only the remaining times.**

$$
2.5 - 2 = 0.5 \text{ years in Path 2's favor}
$$

**Where did the eighteen months go?** [[Into neither number|sunk-bars]]. They are spent under both options, which is exactly why they do not belong in the comparison.

**Sanity check.** The answer "switch saves half a year" depends only on the future. If the reader had been three years into Path 1 instead, Path 1 would have $4 - 3 = 1$ year left, and staying would win. The sunk time mattered only because it changed what *remains* — never as a debt to be repaid by finishing. (This compares time only; a real decision would also weigh cost and the Path 2 transfer phase from the previous lesson.)
:::

## Choosing deliberately, not by default

This module's exercises ask you to write a one-page plan for each of the three paths as they apply to *you*. Each plan must contain:

- dated milestones across the next thirty-six months;
- an explicit cost, in money and in hours per week;
- the single assumption that would break the plan.

Then you choose one, write two sentences on why, and write down **the condition under which you would switch**.

That last piece is the real defense against the sunk-cost trap. Here is why it works. A **switching condition** decided before you start turns a future, emotionally loaded decision into a **[[pre-committed rule|pre-commitment]]** — one made while you were thinking calmly, before any investment existed to feel defensive about.

For example: "I will reassess if I am not within six months of SRE, GNC's basic qualifications by month thirty." A reader who wrote that in advance is later *checking a fact against a plan*. They are not weighing a sunk cost against an uncertain future. The emotional work was already done, back when nothing had been spent. Rocket teams use the [[same trick on launch day|launch-commit]].

::: key
Stating your switching condition before you start — in writing, tied to a specific date and a specific fact you can check — converts a future emotional decision into a pre-committed rule, which is the actual, practical defense against the sunk-cost trap.
:::

**[[Choosing by default|defaults]]** means letting a choice happen without making it. It usually takes one of two forms. Neither feels like a decision, but both are real choices with real costs:

1. **Staying on whatever path you happened to start**, without ever revisiting it.
2. **Never starting any of the three**, while continuing to study broadly and telling yourself the choice is still open.

The second is the more common trap for a reader who has taken this module seriously. It feels responsible, even diligent. And it quietly costs exactly the years this module has spent six lessons insisting are real.

::: warning Keeping your options open is not free
Studying broadly "until you are sure" feels like staying neutral. It is not. Every path takes years, and none of them moves while you wait: no credential is being earned, no qualifying experience is piling up, no internal track record is growing. The time passes either way.
:::

## If you are already mid-career in another field

Two genuinely different situations hide inside "mid-career in another field." This module already gives you the tool to tell them apart: check your existing bachelor's degree's **[[field|midcareer-flow]]** against the basic-qualification lines from the first lesson.

### Case 1: your degree is already in a qualifying field

Say your degree is in an engineering discipline, physics or computer science — even one far from GNC, like civil, electrical or chemical engineering. Then the degree-field filter is already cleared for most postings.

Your real gap is almost never the credential. It is **demonstrated, relevant professional experience** in the specific things GNC postings count: control systems, orbital mechanics, classical dynamics, aerodynamics, sensors and actuators, modeling and simulation. Years of senior experience in an unrelated engineering specialty do not supply that, however senior you are.

For you, Path 1's degree half is usually the wrong, expensive answer. A second bachelor's degree repeats work that is not your actual gap. The shorter, more honest route is usually one of these, combined with this curriculum's demonstrated work:

- Path 2;
- Path 3;
- an aggressive **internal move** within your current engineering employer toward a controls- or dynamics-adjacent team.

### Case 2: your degree is in a non-qualifying field

Say your degree is in business, biology, finance or something similar. Then the field filter is real and not yet met. The accurate options narrow to three.

1. **A full engineering, physics or computer science bachelor's through Path 1.** Still available. It is usually the longest version of Path 1, because it starts from zero credits in the major. Transferring general-education credits (and community college credits) can still shorten it somewhat.
2. **An accredited master's in a qualifying field** — the conversion route from the Path 1 lesson. For a reader with a strong existing math and science foundation, it is often faster than a second bachelor's, typically on the order of one and a half to two and a half years. It still costs real tuition. And it does not, on its own, supply GNC-relevant professional experience.
3. **SRE, GNC's 4-year in-lieu branch.** Worth stating plainly, because it is easy to miss. This branch names **no field requirement** for a prior degree, and does not require that a degree exist at all. A reader with six years of qualifying SRE or DevOps experience clears that line on the years, plus the 1+ year Linux and Python thresholds — whatever their existing degree is in, or whether they have one. Of everything this module describes, it is the one door that does not ask what field you started in.

::: key
A qualifying existing degree in an unrelated engineering, physics, or computer-science field already clears the field filter — the real gap is domain-specific professional experience, not the credential. A non-qualifying existing degree leaves the field filter genuinely open, and SRE, GNC's 4-year in-lieu branch is the one door in this module that asks nothing at all about what field, or whether, that existing degree is in.
:::

::: warning Years of unrelated professional experience do not count toward a GNC experience line
This is the most common misreading this lesson exists to correct. "I have fifteen years of professional experience" is not the same claim as "I have fifteen years of professional experience in GNC or software engineering."

Sr. GNC Engineer's 5-year and 7-year branches count experience in the specific domains the posting names — not total time in the workforce, however senior. **[[Audit your actual years|years-audit]]** against the domains a posting counts, not against your career length.
:::

::: example Two mid-career starting points, and why they split immediately
**Reader A** holds a bachelor's in electrical engineering and has spent ten years in power-grid systems engineering, unrelated to GNC.

- *Degree field:* electrical engineering is squarely an engineering discipline, so it clears the field line on every posting in this module.
- *Experience:* the ten years are not professional experience in control systems, dynamics or simulation, so they do not advance Sr. GNC Engineer's 5-year or 7-year branches at all.
- *Honest gap:* domain-specific GNC experience. Best closed through Path 2, Path 3 or an internal move, paired with this curriculum's demonstrated work.

**Reader B** holds a bachelor's in business administration and has spent six years as a DevOps engineer, the last four of them working daily with Linux and Python in a site-reliability role.

- *Degree field:* business is not a qualifying field — but SRE, GNC's in-lieu branch does not look at the degree at all.
- *Years:* four years of qualifying SRE/DevOps experience meets the 4+ year line: $4 \ge 4$.
- *Linux and Python:* each has four years, well past the 1-year minimum.
- *Result:* Reader B clears SRE, GNC's basic qualifications today, on paper, whatever the degree says.

**Sanity check.** Informally, both readers seem equally far from GNC engineering. Formally, they are in completely different places. Reader A's degree clears a field filter that Reader B's degree fails — yet Reader B's target role never applies that filter in the first place. The field of your degree matters only for doors that ask about it.
:::

## What mid-career changes, and what it does not

A reader who enters this field mid-career brings real advantages, and they deserve naming alongside the gap:

- professional discipline built over years of real deadlines;
- a proven ability to ship work under real constraints;
- often stronger software and systems fundamentals than a new graduate has had time to build;
- often more financial stability to fund a slow, steady, part-time path, without the sharp pressure someone fresh out of school may feel.

None of this closes the specific gap named above. But all of it is real capital that the earlier lessons did not have room to credit.

The real costs are different in *shape* from a younger reader's — not bigger in some general sense:

- A multi-year timeline costs more against a shorter remaining career **[[runway|runway-word]]** than the same years would cost someone earlier in their working life.
- Family and money obligations often make a slow, part-time pace the only realistic one. That is exactly the pace every path in this module was built to allow, and choosing it needs no apology.

::: check
Explain why stating a switching condition before starting a path is a better defense against the sunk-cost trap than trying to "be objective" once you are already partway through.
:::

::: answer
A switching condition set in advance is decided before any investment exists to feel defensive about. So it is a clean, unemotional rule, not a live decision made under the pull of time and effort already spent.

Trying to be objective partway through asks you to overcome the sunk-cost pull at the very moment it is strongest. A pre-committed condition avoids that fight entirely: the later decision becomes checking a fact against a rule made earlier, when the fact could not yet bias the rule.

::: answer
The 4-year in-lieu branch states a requirement on the years and kind of experience — professional software development with site reliability or DevOps experience. Unlike the degree-and-experience branch beside it, it states no requirement on what field a prior degree is in, or that one exists at all. ("Agnostic" here means "takes no position on.")

For a reader whose degree is in something like business or biology, this is the one door in the whole module where that degree's field is not checked at all. Naming it stops that reader from assuming every route requires re-earning a qualifying bachelor's first. (The Linux and Python 1+ year lines still apply.)
:::

::: check
A reader chose Path 3 two years ago and has recently learned their employer is going through a major contraction. Using this lesson's discussion of sunk cost and the Path 3 lesson's discussion of business risk, how should they think through what happens next?
:::

::: answer
The two years of genuine GNC experience are real and travel with the reader, whatever happens to the employer. So they are not lost, and they do not need protecting by staying loyal to a company whose situation has changed. Staying for that reason would be the sunk-cost trap, applied to loyalty instead of a study plan.

The accurate approach is to treat the two years as progress already banked, and to make the next decision — another Path 3 company, Path 2, or another route — based on where each option leaves the reader from today forward, not on a felt debt to the company where those years happened to be spent.
:::

::: check
Why does this module treat "never choosing any of the three paths while continuing to study broadly" as a real cost, not as a safe, neutral, still-undecided position?
:::

::: answer
Each of the three paths takes years, as the previous lesson set out. Years spent without committing to one are years in which none of them advances: the credential is not being earned, the qualifying experience is not building up, and the internal track record is not growing.

It feels safer than choosing, because no specific plan can be judged wrong. But it is not neutral. It carries the same kind of real, unrecoverable time cost as choosing badly — only less visible, because no single decision seems to have caused it.
:::

::: context sunk-cost-word Where "sunk" comes from, and a famous airliner
Economists call money already spent "sunk" because it has gone down like a stone into water — you cannot fish it back out. The trap of throwing more after it is sometimes called the **Concorde fallacy**, after the supersonic airliner that the British and French governments kept funding long after it was clear it would never earn its money back.

The same pull shows up everywhere: finishing a bad book, repairing a car that has already eaten too much money, staying in a queue that is not moving. It is ordinary human thinking, not a personal weakness — which is why it needs a method, not willpower.
:::

::: context pre-commitment Tying yourself to the mast
In Homer's *Odyssey*, Odysseus wants to hear the Sirens, whose song lures sailors onto the rocks. He knows he will not be able to resist once he hears it. So, before the ship gets close, he has his crew tie him to the mast and tells them to ignore any order he gives until they are past.

That is pre-commitment: the calm version of you makes a rule that binds the stressed version of you. A written switching condition is the same move. At month thirty, you do not have to win an argument with yourself — you only have to check whether the condition was met.
:::

::: context launch-commit How launch teams decide before the countdown
Launch teams write down, well before launch day, the exact conditions under which they will not launch: wind speeds above a limit, lightning within a certain distance, a sensor reading outside its allowed range. NASA calls these **launch commit criteria**.

The reason is the same as in this lesson. At T-minus 2 minutes, with years of work and a crowd watching, the pressure to "go anyway" is enormous. A rule written weeks earlier, in a quiet room, makes the call for you. If a criterion is violated, the answer is already decided: hold. Engineers value these rules precisely because they take the heat of the moment out of the decision.
:::

::: context defaults The pull of doing nothing
Researchers who study decisions have found that whatever happens "by default" — if you do nothing — tends to win, even when people say they prefer something else. A well-known example: countries where people are organ donors unless they opt out have much higher donor rates than countries where people must opt in, even when attitudes are similar.

Your career has a default too: whatever you are doing now continues. Studying broadly without committing is that default dressed up as a decision. Writing the three plans and picking one is how you replace the default with a real choice.
:::

::: context runway-word Runway, from airfields to careers
A **runway** is the strip of pavement an airplane uses to speed up and take off. Run out of runway before you reach takeoff speed and you are in trouble. Startups borrowed the word: a company's runway is how many months its money will last.

Applied to a career, runway means the working years you have left. A four-year path is the same four years whenever you start it — but it is a bigger share of a shorter runway. That is a real cost to weigh, not a reason to rule the change out.
:::
