Think about how a young musician gets into a big orchestra. Usually not by walking straight into its audition with no experience. They play in smaller ensembles first, where the audition is informal and the conductor listens to them personally. After a few seasons, they arrive at the big audition with years of real concerts behind them — and that record is what the big orchestra weighs.

Path 3 works the same way. It does not wait outside the target company's filter, like Path 1 working toward the degree. It does not enter through a neighboring job, like Path 2. It gets real, direct GNC experience somewhere else first — at a company whose hiring gate is looser — and lets the experience itself become the thing a later application counts, rather than the credential.

This module names eight companies where that experience is genuinely available: Varda, Stoke, Firefly, Astra, True Anomaly, Impulse, Relativity and Intuitive Machines. Together they are part of what the industry calls **[[NewSpace|newspace-word]]**: younger, privately funded space companies, as opposed to the long-established giants.

## The eight companies, briefly and honestly

These are real, distinct companies building different vehicles for different missions. They are not one interchangeable category. Here is what each does, and the kind of GNC problem that comes with it:

- **Varda** builds **[[reentry capsules|varda-capsule]]** for in-space manufacturing — small spacecraft that make products in orbit and then fall back through the atmosphere to be recovered. That means real atmospheric entry and recovery guidance work.
- **Stoke** is developing a fully reusable two-stage orbital launch vehicle. Its upper stage is designed to come back through the atmosphere using a new, actively cooled heat shield.
- **Firefly** builds the Alpha small launch vehicle and has flown a lunar lander, Blue Ghost. Its GNC work spans rocket ascent guidance and lunar descent and landing.
- **Astra** pursued small orbital launch and went through a well-documented hard period, including mission failures and a move to private ownership. It has since turned part of its business toward spacecraft engines. Its path has been rockier than the others here, and that is worth knowing rather than glossing over.
- **True Anomaly** builds spacecraft for space domain awareness — keeping track of what other satellites are doing. Its central technical problem is **[[rendezvous and proximity operations|rpo]]**: steering one spacecraft safely up close to another.
- **Impulse** builds in-space propulsion and **[[orbital transfer vehicles|space-tug]]**, which carry satellites from where a rocket drops them to where they need to be.
- **Relativity** is developing the Terran R launch vehicle, after retiring its earlier Terran 1 design.
- **Intuitive Machines** builds lunar landers and has flown **[[commercial lunar descent and landing missions|moon-landings]]**.

### A list that moves

Every one of these companies' size, money and hiring needs changes over time — sometimes fast. In this sector that is normal, not unusual. Treat the list as a starting point for research, not a verified snapshot of today.

This module's resources point you to each company's own careers page, and to community tracking sources such as the **[[NASASpaceflight forum|nsf-forum]]**, because staying current matters more here than almost anywhere else in this curriculum.

## Why the credential gate is looser here

Picture two ways a letter gets read. At a huge company, thousands of letters arrive a week, so a mail room sorts them first by fixed rules, and only the survivors reach a person who knows the subject. At a small company, the person who knows the subject opens the mail.

That is roughly the difference in hiring. A smaller, earlier-stage engineering company typically runs a leaner recruiting team than a large, high-volume employer — what the industry often calls a **[[prime|prime-word]]**. It does not get the same flood of applicants per opening, and that flood is what makes a mechanical, resume-line filter necessary in the first place. So a resume for a GNC opening at a company of a few hundred people is more likely to be **[[read personally by an engineer|gate-compare]]** or an engineering manager than run through a large screening system tuned to match a degree field word for word.

The first lesson of this module separated three things: what a posting states, the mechanical filter it becomes, and one person's discretion. At a smaller company, the balance shifts. Personal discretion — the third layer — carries more weight, and the mechanical filter — the second layer — carries less, because the volume that forces mechanical filtering is not there yet.

Be precise about how sure this is. It is a general pattern across smaller, growing technical organizations. It is not a documented policy at any company named here, and it varies from company to company and even from hire to hire.

::: warning "Looser" is relative to a large employer's process, not an absence of a bar
A NewSpace company hiring for GNC still wants evidence of real capability. The demonstrated work this curriculum builds matters here at least as much as anywhere else in this module — arguably more, because it is more likely to land directly in front of the engineer who decides. "Looser gate" describes how strictly the field-of-degree line is enforced, not how much the company cares whether you can do the work.
:::

::: key
A looser credential gate is not the absence of a bar. Demonstrated capability still matters, and often matters more visibly here, because a smaller organization is more likely to have an actual engineer read your work directly.
:::

## What the experience converts into

Recall Sr. GNC Engineer's basic qualifications from the first lesson. It offers three routes, and two of them are about years:

- a qualifying bachelor's degree **plus 5+ years** of professional experience in GNC or software engineering, or
- **7+ years** of professional experience **in lieu of** a degree, or
- a postgraduate degree in a related field.

The senior posting counts professional experience in GNC or software engineering **regardless of employer**. Years earned at Firefly or Stoke are counted, not only years at SpaceX. That is the whole mechanism of Path 3.

::: key
Path 3 — NewSpace ladder: Varda, Stoke, Firefly, Astra, True Anomaly, Impulse, Relativity and Intuitive Machines hire GNC with looser credential gates. Two to three years there converts into the 5+ years professional GNC experience or the 7+ years in lieu of a degree that SpaceX senior postings accept.
:::

### Reading "converts" carefully

Read "converts into" as **adds up toward**, not as a magic exchange rate. Two to three years is not five, and it is not seven. It is a real, substantial, countable piece of either total.

Those years are often worth more per year than the calendar suggests. A small team usually hands each engineer broader **scope** — a wider slice of the work — across guidance, estimation and simulation than one narrow corner of a much larger program would. But how much of the 5-year or 7-year total they supply depends on what other experience you bring with you.

::: key
Two to three years at a NewSpace company accumulates toward Sr. GNC Engineer's 5-year (with-degree) or 7-year (in-lieu) thresholds — a substantial, often broad-scope contribution, not a guaranteed shortcut that completes either threshold by itself.
:::

::: example Counting years toward two different thresholds
A candidate has 1 year of GNC-adjacent professional experience from an earlier job. Then they spend 2.5 years at a NewSpace launch company doing genuine guidance and simulation work. They have no qualifying bachelor's degree.

**Step 1 — total the experience.**

$$
1 + 2.5 = 3.5 \text{ years}.
$$

**Step 2 — compare with the branch that applies now.** With no degree, the 7+ years in-lieu branch is the one that counts:

$$
7 - 3.5 = 3.5 \text{ years remaining}.
$$

That is [[exactly half still to go|years-bars]] ($3.5 / 7 = 0.5$).

**Step 3 — what if the candidate later earns a qualifying degree?** Suppose they finish an accredited qualifying degree through Path 1 at some point. Then the same 3.5 years count toward the 5+ years-with-degree branch instead:

$$
5 - 3.5 = 1.5 \text{ years remaining}.
$$

**Sanity check.** $1.5$ is less than $3.5$, as it must be: the with-degree threshold is lower, so the same experience covers more of it. The gap shrinks by $3.5 - 1.5 = 2$ years, exactly the difference between the two thresholds ($7 - 5 = 2$).

This is why the module does not present the three paths as alternatives you pick once and never revisit. They combine.
:::

## Not only direct employment

The same logic often applies beyond the eight names. **Contract engineering firms** (companies other businesses hire to do engineering work for them) and **suppliers** of hardware or software to these companies can offer the same thing: real GNC-adjacent experience, judged by people with more direct discretion than a big employer's queue, that later counts as professional experience.

This module does not have specific companies to name in that category. If it fits your situation, research it with the same care this module's exercises ask you to apply to the named companies.

## What Path 3 costs, and what it does not solve

**The years.** These are the most visible cost, and they are real years, not a shortcut measured in months.

**Business risk.** Less visible, and worth naming as plainly. An early- or growth-stage space company carries a genuine risk of shrinking, changing direction, or failing outright. A large established employer mostly does not. That risk is part of the honest price of this path, not a remote chance to wave away.

**The field-of-degree question.** Path 3 does not settle it everywhere. Most GNC-Engineer-titled roles, even at senior level, still want the bachelor's, or the 7-year in-lieu route, or the postgraduate route. Path 3 experience feeds the **years** side of that equation, and it is exactly on point for the 7-year in-lieu branch. But at a role with no in-lieu clause at all, it is not a way around the field-of-degree line.

::: key
Path 3 trades a looser, more personally evaluated hiring gate for real GNC experience, at real but genuine business risk. The companies named here — Varda, Stoke, Firefly, Astra, True Anomaly, Impulse, Relativity, and Intuitive Machines — change over time, and current status should always be checked directly.
:::

::: example What survives a company's rough patch, and what does not
A candidate joins a small launch company's GNC team for the broad, hands-on scope a larger company would not offer at the same career stage. For two genuine years they work across guidance, simulation and **[[dispersion analysis|dispersion]]**. Then the company goes through a big contraction, and the role ends.

**What did not survive:** the specific job, and the assumption that this employer would keep existing in its current form. That is a real cost, and one the candidate did not control.

**What did survive, fully and portably:**

- the two years of real, demonstrated GNC work,
- the technical judgment built by handling real problems,
- the professional relationships — with people who now work at a wider spread of companies across the industry.

**The honest reading.** Path 3 did not fail this candidate. The two years are exactly as real, and exactly as countable toward the next application, as they would have been at a company that never shrank. The risk named earlier is real, but it lands on the specific job, not on the experience gained from it.
:::

::: context newspace-word Old space, new space
"NewSpace" is an informal label, not an official category. People started using it in the 2000s for privately funded companies that build rockets and spacecraft as commercial businesses, often moving faster and taking more risk than traditional aerospace. SpaceX was once the leading example; today it is so big that it hires more like a large established company. The eight companies in this lesson are mostly younger and smaller, and that difference in size is what this lesson is really about.
:::

::: context varda-capsule A factory that falls from the sky
Some materials form differently in near-zero gravity — certain drug crystals, for instance, grow more evenly. Varda's idea is to make them in orbit and bring them home. Its first capsule, W-1, launched in 2023, grew crystals of the HIV drug ritonavir, and landed in Utah in February 2024 after a fiery trip through the atmosphere. For a GNC engineer, the hard part is steering a small capsule through reentry so that it lands inside a test range, not somewhere else.
:::

::: context rpo Parking next to a moving car at 7.7 km/s
Rendezvous means bringing two spacecraft to the same place at the same time; proximity operations means flying safely close to each other once there. Both are in orbit at about 7.7 km/s, so the motion relative to each other is what matters — and it is strange. Fire your thruster straight toward a target ahead of you in the same orbit, and you rise above it and drift behind instead.

::: context space-tug The last-mile delivery truck of orbit
A big rocket often drops many satellites off together in one "rideshare" orbit, like a bus dropping everyone at one stop. But each satellite may need a different final orbit. An orbital transfer vehicle — sometimes called a space tug — carries satellites from that drop-off point to where they belong. Its GNC problems are orbital maneuvers: when to fire, which way, and for how long, with accurate knowledge of where it is.
:::

::: context moon-landings Commercial landers on the Moon
In February 2024 Intuitive Machines' Odysseus lander (mission IM-1) became the first privately built spacecraft to land softly on the Moon, and the first US soft landing since Apollo 17 in 1972. It touched down but tipped onto its side. In March 2025 Firefly's Blue Ghost landed upright and operated as planned, and days later Intuitive Machines' second lander also tipped over. Landing on the Moon is a GNC problem through and through: estimating height and speed from sensors, then steering the final descent.
:::

::: context nsf-forum Where the industry news gets tracked
NASASpaceflight is a long-running space news site with a large public forum. Its members follow launch companies closely: test campaigns, launch schedules, funding rounds and layoffs, often with photos and documents. For this path it is useful as an early signal of which companies are growing and which are struggling. Treat forum posts as leads to check, not as confirmed facts — confirm anything important on the company's own site or in its official announcements.
:::

::: context prime-word Why big contractors are called primes
In government contracting, the **prime contractor** is the company that signs the main contract directly with the customer — NASA or the Department of Defense, for instance. It then hires **subcontractors** for parts of the work. Long-established primes include Lockheed Martin, Boeing and Northrop Grumman. People also use "prime" more loosely for any large, established aerospace employer, which is the sense in this lesson: big, with a well-oiled, high-volume hiring machine.
:::

::: context dispersion Dispersion analysis
"Dispersion" means spread. A dispersion analysis asks: if every uncertain thing about a flight varies within realistic limits — engine thrust, winds, sensor errors, the mass of the payload — how far apart do the outcomes land? Engineers run it as a Monte Carlo campaign of thousands of simulated flights, then check that even the worst cases stay inside safe limits. At a small company, the same engineer may write the guidance, build the simulation and run the dispersions — the broad scope this lesson describes.
:::
