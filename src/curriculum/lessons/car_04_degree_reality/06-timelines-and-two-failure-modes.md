Think about planning a long road trip. Before you pick a route, you want to know how long each one really takes — not the time the map app shows at 3 a.m. with empty roads, but the time it takes with traffic, fuel stops and the one stretch of roadworks everybody forgets. And you want to know the two classic ways a trip goes wrong: driving hard in the wrong direction, and stopping halfway because you think you have arrived.

All three paths are now on the table: Path 1 (the degree, taken in parallel with this curriculum), Path 2 (the side door through a software role, then a transfer), and Path 3 (the NewSpace ladder through a smaller company). This lesson does two things with them.

First, it puts their timelines side by side, stated honestly in **years** — not in the months a [[hopeful plan|planning-fallacy]] tends to assume. Second, it names the two specific ways people waste those years. These are people who understand every fact in this module so far. They do not fail from lack of effort. They fail from a wrong picture of how the filter this module has described actually works.

## The three timelines, side by side

Here is each path, with the part people forget spelled out.

**Path 1, degree-parallel: typically three to five years part-time.** That is the range the Path 1 lesson's own worked examples landed inside. It reflects real, accredited coursework at a pace you can keep up alongside a job or other duties. **Part-time** here means taking fewer courses each term than a full-time student, so study fits around work.

**Path 2, the side door: roughly two to four years** of relevant software or site-reliability experience before an application to a role like Site Reliability Engineer, GNC (SRE, said letter by letter) is genuinely credible on paper. Notice what that figure covers. It gets you to a credible *application*. It does not include the internal transfer to a GNC team afterward. The Path 2 lesson was clear that the transfer is not triggered by time served alone, and it adds its own, less predictable span.

**Path 3, the NewSpace ladder: two to three years** of genuine GNC experience at another company — *plus*, separately, however long it takes to get hired there in the first place. That first hiring step is a real span of its own, sometimes a long one. A plan built only around the "two to three years" figure quietly skips it.

::: key
Realistic timeline honesty: degree-parallel is typically three to five years part-time. Side door is roughly two to four years of software experience before the application is credible. NewSpace ladder is two to three years at another company plus the time to get hired there. None of the three is fast, and saying otherwise would be dishonest.
:::

Say this as plainly as the first lesson said the degree line: **[[none of the three paths is fast|timeline-bars]]**. All three are measured in years.

A plan that assumes months, for any of them, is not a faster version of the same plan. It is a different plan, and an inaccurate one. It will produce real disappointment on a real calendar. Worse, it tempts you to give up and restart every few months, because progress always looks "too slow" next to a number that was never true.

::: key
Path 1: typically three to five years part-time. Path 2: roughly two to four years to a credible application, plus a separate, less predictable span for the internal transfer itself. Path 3: two to three years of experience, plus separately the time needed to get hired there first. None is fast, and stating otherwise would not be honest.
:::

::: warning Every path has a second phase that is easy to drop
The headline number for Path 2 stops at a credible application; the transfer comes after. The headline number for Path 3 starts once you are hired; getting hired comes before. If your plan's total equals the headline number exactly, you have probably left one of these phases out.
:::

## Why none of them is a shortcut, and why that is fine

Seeing three different timelines, it is tempting to hunt for the fastest one, as if the paths were three sprints to the same finish line. They are not.

They are three different shapes of the same multi-year commitment, built from different material:

- accredited coursework (Path 1);
- software and infrastructure experience (Path 2);
- direct GNC work at a smaller company (Path 3).

"Which one is shorter?" is the wrong question. Once you count the hidden second phases of Paths 2 and 3 in full, the honest ranges overlap heavily. No path wins on speed alone.

The more useful question is: **which set of years would you rather actually be living, starting from where you are now?** For example:

- Do you have the stability for years of steady part-time coursework?
- Do you already have — or could you build — real software and systems depth?
- Are you drawn to the wide scope, and the bigger ups and downs, of an earlier-stage company?

None of the three is the "correct" answer in general. Choosing well between them is the subject of this module's final lesson. This lesson's job is narrower: to make sure the years each path really costs are not a surprise halfway through.

## Failure mode one: believing a portfolio replaces the degree

Picture someone knocking louder and louder on a door that is locked with a key they do not have. The knocking is real effort. It is also aimed at the wrong thing.

The first lesson of this module set out the mechanism this failure mode ignores. For a posting with **no in-lieu clause** — no written line saying some experience can stand "in place of" the degree — the degree line is checked at the **recruiter stage**. That is the first screen, done by someone whose job is to sort applications fast. It happens before anyone with the engineering judgment to evaluate a portfolio ever reads it.

So no amount of independent project work, however good, changes which line a resume fails at that filter.

::: key
Failure mode one: believing that a sufficiently impressive portfolio makes the degree line irrelevant. It does not: the filter is applied before any human reads the portfolio, at the recruiter stage.
:::

Here is how the failure plays out. The person spends years applying. Between rounds, they make the portfolio stronger. They read each [[rejection|silent-rejections]] as a sign they need one more project. But the real reason for each rejection has nothing to do with the portfolio. The result would be exactly the same if the portfolio were half as strong, or twice as strong.

Are those years wasted? Not in the sense of producing nothing. The capability built is real and it travels with you, as the second lesson explained. But they are wasted against the *specific goal*: clearing a filter that was never going to open for this reason. The same years spent on Path 1, 2 or 3 would have moved toward clearing it.

::: key
Failure mode one: believing a strong enough portfolio makes the degree line optional where it is a hard filter. The filter is applied before the portfolio is read, so the belief does not change the outcome, only how many years are spent discovering that.
:::

::: example Three years spent two different ways
**The situation.** A candidate has no degree and a strong independent portfolio. For three years, every few months, they apply to GNC Engineer Level I to II postings and similar ones. All of these carry a degree-field basic qualification with no in-lieu clause. Between rounds, they grow the portfolio.

**Version A: the three years as spent.** After three years, how much closer are they to clearing that specific filter? Zero. The filter never tested portfolio strength, so improving the portfolio could not move them toward it.

**Version B: the same three years, aimed at a door that can open.** Suppose they had spent those years building qualifying software and site-reliability experience instead. SRE, GNC has a 4-year in-lieu branch: 4+ years of professional experience building software with site reliability or DevOps, in place of a degree. After three years they would have

$$
4 - 3 = 1 \text{ year}
$$

left before a credible application. (The branch also asks for 1+ years each of Linux and Python, which three years of that kind of work would normally supply — but check it, as the Path 2 lesson showed.)

**Sanity check.** Both versions of this candidate worked equally hard for the same three years. Version A is still at 0 of an unreachable goal. Version B is three-quarters of the way to a threshold it can actually reach. The difference is not effort. It is which door the effort was aimed at.
:::

## Failure mode two: believing nothing but the degree matters

The second failure mode is the mirror image. Think of a runner who trains hard only to qualify for a race, qualifies, and then stops training — as if qualifying were winning.

Someone who completes an accredited degree and stops there — no independent projects, no demonstrated work beyond coursework — clears the recruiter-stage filter cleanly. That is real progress. It is not nothing.

But remember the Path 1 lesson's comparison. At the **[[technical interview|technical-interview]]** stage, this person now competes against everyone else who cleared the same filter. Some of those people also built the demonstrated, shipped work this curriculum exists to produce: a 6-DOF simulator, a working state estimator, a guidance law derived and tested.

::: key
Failure mode two: believing that only the degree matters and that technical preparation can wait. The degree gets you past the filter; the demonstrated work is what wins against [[several hundred other people|applicant-pool]] who also cleared it.
:::

In logic words: the degree is **[[necessary but not sufficient|necessary-sufficient]]**. **Necessary** means you cannot get there without it (wherever there is no in-lieu clause). **Not sufficient** means having it does not, by itself, get you there — not anywhere, against a real pool of applicants.

This failure mode wastes years in a different way from the first. It does not fail to make progress. It makes only *half* the progress that was available in the same time. And it discovers the gap late, at the interview stage, when it is expensive to close — instead of early, when it was cheap to work on alongside coursework that was happening anyway.

::: key
Failure mode two: believing the degree alone finishes the job. It clears the filter; it does not, by itself, set you apart from the rest of the pool that also cleared it — the demonstrated work this curriculum builds is what does that.
:::

::: example The hours a degree does not fill by itself
**The situation.** Two candidates each complete the same accredited bachelor's degree over the same four years.

**Candidate one** takes a normal course load and does no independent projects beyond what is graded.

**Candidate two** does the same degree, and also spends 5 hours a week on this curriculum's exercises. Over four years that adds up to:

$$
5 \,\tfrac{\text{hours}}{\text{week}} \times 52 \,\tfrac{\text{weeks}}{\text{year}} \times 4 \text{ years} = 1040 \text{ hours}
$$

Step by step: $5 \times 52 = 260$ hours a year, and $260 \times 4 = 1040$ hours in total. That is alongside the degree, not instead of it.

**At graduation**, both clear the same recruiter-stage filter in exactly the same way. The filter cannot tell them apart.

**At the technical interview**, only candidate two brings a body of demonstrated, inspectable work — built during years both candidates were spending on the degree anyway.

**Sanity check.** Is 1,040 hours a lot? It is the same as $1040 / 40 = 26$ weeks of full-time work — about half a working year, spread thinly across four. Large enough to matter, small enough to fit. Same four years, used two ways, producing two very different positions at the one stage where the degree alone was never going to be enough.
:::

## Holding both truths at once

Here is the position this module has argued since its first lesson. It is not a halfway point between the two failure modes. It is both halves being true at the same time, which is a stronger and more precise claim than either one alone:

- **The degree is not optional** wherever there is no in-lieu clause.
- **The degree is not sufficient** by itself anywhere.

Believe only the first half, and you drift into failure mode two. Believe only the second half, and you drift into failure mode one.

Notice what the error is. In both directions, the person does not believe a false fact — both halves are true. The error is believing only *one* of two true things and treating it as the whole picture. The two halves describe [[two different stages|two-stage-pipeline]] of the same pipeline: the filter stage, and the stage after it where people are compared.

::: warning The two failure modes feel different — learn to notice your own
Failure mode one often feels like **frustration** at a system that seems arbitrary. It shows up as repeated applications to the same kind of posting, while the plan itself never changes.

Failure mode two often feels like **relief** and a sense of being done right after finishing a degree — mistaking the end of one real hurdle for the end of the competition.

Neither feeling is wrong to have. Being stopped by a hard filter is genuinely frustrating, and finishing a degree genuinely deserves relief. But when you notice the feeling, treat it as a signal: check whether your plan accounts for *both* halves of this lesson.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
State the honest timeline range for each of the three paths, including the hidden second phase this lesson adds to Paths 2 and 3.
:::

::: answer
Path 1 is typically three to five years part-time.

Path 2 is roughly two to four years to a credible application, plus a separate, less predictable span for the internal transfer itself, which time served alone does not trigger.

Path 3 is two to three years of genuine experience, plus, separately, whatever time it takes to get hired at one of these companies in the first place — a phase that a plan focused only on the two-to-three-year figure tends to skip.
:::

::: check
Explain why failure mode one and failure mode two are described as opposite errors, rather than the same error in two forms.
:::

::: answer
Failure mode one comes from believing capability alone (a strong portfolio) can stand in for a credential where the credential is a hard filter. That leads to years of effort that never touches the actual obstacle.

Failure mode two comes from believing a credential alone is enough once you have it. That leads to real progress on the filter, but nothing that sets you apart afterward.

One gives the degree too little weight as a filter; the other gives it too much weight, as if it were the whole competition. They fail in opposite directions. That is why the accurate position has to hold both halves at once — the degree matters, and it is not enough — rather than leaning on either one.
:::

::: check
A reader has spent two years building an excellent portfolio and applying only to postings with a stated bachelor's degree basic qualification and no in-lieu clause. No offers. Diagnose what has most likely gone wrong, using this lesson's framing.
:::

::: answer
This matches failure mode one. For these specific postings, the recruiter-stage filter is not testing the portfolio's quality, so improving the portfolio further will not change the outcome.

The accurate diagnosis is not "the portfolio needs to be stronger." It is "these postings were never going to open on portfolio strength alone." The same two years would have made measurable progress if aimed at Path 1, 2 or 3, each of which engages the actual filter instead of pushing on a wall that does not move.
:::

::: check
Why does this lesson insist on stating timelines as ranges in years, rather than giving a single best-case number for each path?
:::

::: answer
A single best-case number invites false precision, which this curriculum avoids throughout. It would also understate the real cost of each path — especially the hidden second phases of Paths 2 and 3, which a best-case framing tends to drop.

A range in years, including the easy-to-miss phases, gives you an accurate basis for choosing and committing. A too-optimistic number predictably feels like failure the first time real progress runs slower than it, which tempts people to abandon a plan that was actually on track.
:::

::: check
A graduate with an accredited qualifying degree and no independent projects asks why they are not getting offers, even though they meet the basic qualifications on paper. Diagnose this using failure mode two.
:::

::: answer
Meeting the basic qualifications explains why the resume clears the recruiter-stage filter and reaches a technical interview. It does not decide how that interview goes. There, the graduate is compared with other candidates who cleared the same filter.

If some of those candidates also have demonstrated, shipped work and this graduate has only coursework, the gap costing offers is not eligibility — that was already settled. It is standing out, which is exactly the piece failure mode two leaves undone.
:::

::: check
Explain why "the degree is not optional" and "the degree is not sufficient" do not contradict each other, using both failure modes as evidence.
:::

::: answer
Each statement guards against a different failure mode. "Not optional" corrects failure mode one, where a strong portfolio is wrongly assumed to replace a hard filter. "Not sufficient" corrects failure mode two, where a cleared filter is wrongly assumed to be the whole competition.

Both can be true at once because they describe two different stages of the same pipeline: the filter stage, and the comparison stage that follows it. Deny either statement and you reproduce one of the two failure modes.
:::

::: context planning-fallacy Why plans come out too short
Psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky gave this a name in the late 1970s: the **planning fallacy**. People estimating their own projects tend to picture the smooth version — every step going right — and forget the delays that almost always happen. So the estimate comes out short, again and again, even for people who have been late before.

One fix is to ask "how long did this take *other* people?" instead of "how long will it take me if all goes well?" That is what this lesson does: it gives ranges drawn from how the paths actually run, not from the best case. Aerospace schedules suffer from the same effect, which is why programs carry schedule margin.
:::

::: context silent-rejections Why a rejection rarely tells you why
Most rejection emails are short and generic: "we have decided to move forward with other candidates." They almost never say *which* line you failed. Companies screening at volume rarely have time to write personal feedback, and some avoid giving reasons at all.

That silence is what makes failure mode one so hard to notice from the inside. With no reason given, people fill the gap with their own guess — usually "my work was not good enough" — and respond by building more work. The honest check is to read the posting's basic qualifications again and ask whether your resume meets each line as printed, before assuming the problem is quality.
:::

::: context technical-interview What a technical interview is
After the recruiter screen, a candidate who clears the basic qualifications meets engineers who test what they can actually do. In a GNC interview that might mean deriving an equation on a whiteboard, explaining how a filter blends two sensors, talking through the design choices in a project on your resume, or writing and debugging code.

This is the stage where a portfolio finally gets read by someone who can judge it — which is exactly why it matters so much here, and so little at the stage before.
:::

::: context applicant-pool How big is "several hundred"?
Nobody outside a company sees its exact numbers, and they vary by role and by year. But a well-known employer posting an entry-level engineering job can easily draw hundreds of applications, and many of those applicants meet the basic qualifications.

Suppose only a small share of qualified applicants reach the final interview round and one or two get offers. Clearing the filter then puts you in a crowd, not at the front of it. What moves you forward inside that crowd is evidence a reviewer can inspect — which is the part failure mode two leaves out.
:::

::: context necessary-sufficient Necessary, sufficient, and the four boxes
Logicians use two precise words. A condition is **necessary** if you cannot succeed without it. It is **sufficient** if having it guarantees success. The degree (on a posting with no in-lieu clause) is necessary but not sufficient.
