All three paths are now on the table. This lesson does two things with them. First, it puts their timelines side by side, stated honestly in years rather than in the months a hopeful plan tends to assume. Second, it names the two specific ways people who understand every fact in this module so far still waste those years — not from lack of effort, but from a wrong model of how the filter this module has described actually works.

## The three timelines, side by side

Path 1, degree-parallel, is typically three to five years part-time — the range the previous lessons' own worked examples landed inside, and a range that reflects real accredited coursework at a sustainable pace alongside continued work or other obligations. Path 2, the side door, is roughly two to four years of relevant software or site-reliability experience before an application to a role like SRE, GNC is genuinely credible on paper — and that figure covers only reaching a credible application, not the internal transfer afterward, which the earlier lesson was explicit is not triggered by tenure alone and adds its own, less predictable span. Path 3, the NewSpace ladder, is two to three years of genuine experience at another company, plus, separately, however long it takes to actually get hired there in the first place — a real, sometimes substantial span in its own right that a plan built only around the two-to-three-year figure quietly skips.

State this as plainly as the degree line itself was stated in the first lesson: none of the three paths is fast. All three are measured in years. Any plan that assumes months, for any of them, is not a more efficient version of the same plan — it is a different, inaccurate plan that will produce real disappointment on a real timeline.

## Why none of them is a shortcut, and why that is fine

It is tempting to read three different timelines and start hunting for whichever one is fastest, as though the paths were competing sprints toward an identical finish line. They are not. They are different shapes of the same multi-year commitment, built from different material — accredited coursework, software and infrastructure experience, or direct GNC work at a smaller company — and the right comparison is not "which one is shorter," since the honest ranges overlap heavily once the unstated second phases of Paths 2 and 3 are counted in full. The more useful comparison is which set of years you would rather actually be living, given where you are now: whether you have the stability for sustained part-time coursework, whether you already have or can build software and systems depth, whether you are drawn to the broader scope and higher variance of an earlier-stage company. None of the three is the "correct" answer in general. Choosing between them well is the subject of this module's final lesson; this lesson's job is only to make sure the years each one actually costs are not a surprise partway through.

::: key
Path 1: typically three to five years part-time. Path 2: roughly two to four years to a credible application, plus a separate, less predictable span for the internal transfer itself. Path 3: two to three years of experience, plus separately the time needed to get hired there first. None is fast, and stating otherwise would not be honest.
:::

## Failure mode one: believing a portfolio replaces the degree

The first lesson of this module established the mechanism this failure mode ignores: for a posting with no in-lieu clause, the degree line is applied at the recruiter stage, before a portfolio is ever read by anyone with the judgment to evaluate it. No quantity or quality of independent project work changes which line a resume fails to check at that specific filter. A reader operating under this failure mode spends years applying, strengthening the portfolio between rounds, reading each rejection as a signal to build one more project — when the actual, structural reason for each rejection has nothing to do with the portfolio at all, and would be exactly the same if the portfolio were half as strong or twice as strong. The years spent this way are not wasted in the sense of producing nothing — the capability described in the second lesson is real and portable — but they are wasted against the specific goal of clearing a specific filter that was never going to open for this reason, when those same years, spent instead on Path 1, 2, or 3, would have produced actual progress toward clearing it.

## Failure mode two: believing nothing but the degree matters

The second failure mode sits on the opposite side of the same error. A reader who completes an accredited degree and stops there — no independent projects, no demonstrated work beyond coursework — clears the recruiter-stage filter cleanly. That is real progress, not nothing. But the third lesson's own comparison showed what happens next: this reader now competes, at the technical interview stage, against other candidates who also cleared the identical filter, some of whom also built the demonstrated, shipped work this curriculum exists to produce. The degree is necessary wherever no in-lieu clause exists; it is not sufficient anywhere, against a real applicant pool. This failure mode wastes years differently from the first — not by failing to make progress, but by making only half the progress available in the same span of time, and discovering the gap only once it is expensive to close, at the interview stage, rather than early, when it was cheap to address alongside the coursework already underway.

## Holding both truths at once

The actual position this module has argued for since its first lesson is not a compromise between these two failure modes — it is the simultaneous truth of both halves, which is a stronger and more specific claim than either failure mode alone. The degree is not optional wherever no in-lieu clause exists, and the degree is not sufficient by itself anywhere. Believing only the first half produces failure mode one. Believing only the second half produces failure mode two. The error, in both directions, is not believing a false fact — both halves are true — the error is believing only one of two true things and treating it as the whole picture.

::: warning The two failure modes have different emotional signatures — learn to notice your own
Failure mode one often shows up as frustration at a system that feels arbitrary, producing repeated applications to the same class of posting with the plan itself never actually changing. Failure mode two often shows up as relief and a sense of completion right after finishing a degree, mistaking the end of one real hurdle for the end of the competition. Neither feeling is wrong to have — clearing a hard filter is genuinely frustrating when it happens to you, and finishing a degree is genuinely worth relief — but noticing the feeling is a useful signal to check whether the underlying plan has actually accounted for both halves of this lesson's argument.
:::

::: key
Failure mode one: believing a strong enough portfolio makes the degree line optional where it is a hard filter. The filter is applied before the portfolio is read, so the belief does not change the outcome, only how many years are spent discovering that.
:::

::: key
Failure mode two: believing the degree alone finishes the job. It clears the filter; it does not, by itself, differentiate you from the rest of the pool that also cleared it — the demonstrated work this curriculum builds is what does that.
:::

::: example Three years spent two different ways
A candidate with no degree and a strong independent portfolio spends three years applying, every few months, to GNC Engineer Level I to II and comparable postings that carry a degree-field basic qualification with no in-lieu clause, growing the portfolio between rounds.

At the end of three years, progress toward clearing that specific filter is the same as it was on day one: zero, because the filter was never testing portfolio strength in the first place. The same three years, spent instead building qualifying software and site-reliability experience toward SRE, GNC's 4-year in-lieu branch, would have produced $4 - 3 = 1$ year remaining to a credible application — real, measurable progress toward a threshold the effort was actually structured to close. Both versions of this candidate worked equally hard for the same three years. Only one of them was working against a filter that could actually open.
:::

::: example The hours a degree does not fill by itself
A candidate completes an accredited bachelor's degree over four years, taking a normal course load and no more, with no independent projects beyond what was assigned for a grade.

A different candidate, over the identical four years and the identical degree, also spends five hours a week on this curriculum's exercises outside of coursework — $5 \times 52 \times 4 = 1040$ hours across the same span, alongside, not instead of, the degree itself. Both candidates clear the same recruiter-stage filter identically at graduation. Only the second candidate arrives at the technical interview with a body of demonstrated, inspectable work built during years that were, for both candidates, already being spent on the degree — the same four years, used two different ways, producing two very different competitive positions at the one stage where the degree alone was never going to be enough.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
State the honest timeline range for each of the three paths, including the unstated second phase this lesson adds to Paths 2 and 3.
:::

::: answer
Path 1 is typically three to five years part-time. Path 2 is roughly two to four years to a credible application, plus a separate, less predictable span for the internal transfer itself, which tenure alone does not trigger. Path 3 is two to three years of genuine experience, plus separately whatever time is needed to get hired at one of these companies in the first place, which a plan focused only on the two-to-three-year figure tends to skip.
:::

::: check
Explain why failure mode one and failure mode two are described as opposite errors rather than the same error in two forms.
:::

::: answer
Failure mode one comes from believing capability alone (a strong portfolio) substitutes for a credential where the credential is a hard filter, leading to years of effort that never engages the actual obstacle. Failure mode two comes from believing a credential alone is sufficient once obtained, leading to real progress on the filter but no differentiation afterward. One under-weights the degree's role as a filter; the other over-weights it as the entire competition. They fail in opposite directions, which is why the accurate position requires holding both halves — the degree matters and is not enough — simultaneously rather than emphasizing either one.
:::

::: check
A reader has spent two years building an excellent portfolio and applying only to postings with a stated bachelor's degree basic qualification and no in-lieu clause, with no offers. Diagnose what has most likely gone wrong, using this lesson's framing.
:::

::: answer
This matches failure mode one: the portfolio's quality is not the variable the recruiter-stage filter for these specific postings is testing, so improving it further will not change the outcome for postings with no in-lieu clause. The accurate diagnosis is not "the portfolio needs to be stronger" but "these specific postings were never going to open on portfolio strength alone" — the two years would have produced measurable progress if directed instead at Path 1, 2, or 3, each of which engages the actual filter rather than working around a wall that does not move.
:::

::: check
Why does this lesson insist on stating timelines in years rather than giving a single best-case number for each path?
:::

::: answer
A single best-case number invites exactly the kind of false precision this curriculum avoids throughout, and it would understate the real, honest cost of each path, particularly the unstated second phases of Path 2 and Path 3 that a best-case framing tends to drop. Stating a range in years, including the phases easy to overlook, gives a reader an accurate basis for choosing and committing, rather than a number that will predictably feel like failure the first time real progress runs slower than an artificially optimistic estimate.
:::

::: check
A graduate with an accredited qualifying degree and no independent projects asks why they are not receiving offers despite meeting the basic qualifications on paper. Diagnose this using failure mode two.
:::

::: answer
Meeting the basic qualifications explains why the resume clears the recruiter-stage filter and reaches a technical interview; it does not explain the outcome of that interview, which is evaluated against other candidates who also cleared the same filter. If those other candidates additionally have demonstrated, shipped work and this candidate has only coursework, the gap costing offers is not eligibility — it was resolved already — it is differentiation, which is exactly what failure mode two describes as the piece left undone.
:::

::: check
Explain why "the degree is not optional" and "the degree is not sufficient" are not in tension with each other, using both failure modes as evidence.
:::

::: answer
Each statement guards against a different failure mode. "Not optional" is the corrective to failure mode one, where a strong portfolio is wrongly assumed to replace a hard filter. "Not sufficient" is the corrective to failure mode two, where a cleared filter is wrongly assumed to be the whole competition. Both statements can be true at once because they describe two different stages of the same pipeline — the filter stage and the differentiation stage that follows it — and denying either statement reproduces one of the two failure modes this lesson names.
:::
