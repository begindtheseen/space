Imagine a talent show where everyone who wants to perform needs a ticket. Getting a ticket does not win you anything — it only puts you on the stage, next to hundreds of others who also have tickets. What wins is what you do once you are up there.

Path 1 is the most direct of this module's three routes because it does not try to get around the degree line at all. It **gets the degree** — arranged to fit around a life already in progress — and it uses this curriculum *the whole time*, not afterward. "Degree-parallel" names that second half, and it is not an extra. A reader who finishes an accredited degree with only coursework behind it clears the recruiter-stage filter from the first two lessons. Then, at the interview, they meet everyone else who cleared it too. What separates you from that crowd is not the degree — every one of them has one. It is demonstrated, **[[shipped|shipped]]** work that almost none of them will have, because an ordinary course of study never asks for it.

This lesson covers both halves. First, what an accredited degree that fits around a job actually looks like. Second, why pairing it with this curriculum is the strongest form of this path, not an optional upgrade.

## What "accredited" means, and why it is not negotiable

The previous lesson described accreditation in general: a trusted third party checks that a program meets a standard. Here it is made concrete.

In the United States, **ABET** accredits individual degree programs through four commissions: engineering, engineering technology, computing, and applied and natural science — that last one covers some physics and other science programs. Not every legitimate physics or computer science degree carries ABET accreditation. Many are covered instead by their university's **[[regional accreditor|institutional-accreditor]]** — the body that accredits the whole institution. For those fields, that is the normal, sufficient kind of accreditation, not a lesser one.

So the line that matters is not "ABET versus nothing." It is **accredited versus not**. The only way to know which a program is: check directly. This module's resources point to ABET's own program search for exactly that reason, and a program with institution-wide accreditation can be checked through the school or its accreditor.

This is not a formality to skim. A program can look exactly like a normal university — a website, a course catalog, tuition bills, a graduation ceremony — and still not be accredited by any body that employers and other colleges recognize. Such a program does not satisfy a posting's degree line at all, however real it looks and however much it costs. That is the **[[diploma mill|diploma-mill]]** trap. Check accreditation before you enroll and before you pay anything, not after.

::: warning An unaccredited program does not satisfy the line, no matter how it looks
Enrolling in a program that looks like a university in every visible way but lacks accreditation from a body employers recognize does not clear a posting's degree-field requirement. The money and time spent there cannot be recovered by explaining the circumstances later. Check accreditation directly — through ABET's own search for engineering, computing and applicable science programs, or through the institution's stated institution-wide accreditor otherwise — before enrolling and before paying anything.
:::

## Programs that fit around a life already in progress

Most realistic routes to an accredited degree while working follow one of three shapes. Learn the shapes first; this module's exercises then ask you to research real programs.

**Shape 1: a part-time or online completion track.** Many established, accredited universities — public ones especially — run evening, weekend or fully online programs for working adults finishing a bachelor's. These typically carry the same accreditation, and usually the same degree title, as the school's on-campus program.

**Shape 2: [[community college|community-college]] transfer.** You take the lower-division work — **[[general education|gen-ed]]** plus introductory courses in your major — at an accredited community college, usually for much less per credit. Then you transfer those credits into a four-year accredited program and finish the upper-division, major-specific courses there. This is a very well-worn pattern in US higher education. Many states formalize it with **[[articulation agreements|articulation]]** — written deals that guarantee how credits transfer. It cuts both the time and the cost of the four-year portion and does not touch accreditation at all.

**Shape 3: a [[competency-based|competency-based]] model.** A small number of accredited, nonprofit institutions let you move forward by *showing* you have mastered each defined skill, whenever you are ready, instead of waiting out a fixed semester. For a disciplined learner who already knows a lot, that can shrink the calendar time.

Which programs currently offer which shape, in which fields, changes over time. So this lesson teaches the three shapes and leaves the current research — cost, transfer policy, completion time — to the exercise built for it.

## The credential is the credential, however it was earned

Once a degree is accredited and granted, a posting's basic-qualifications line generally does not care *how* it was earned. "Bachelor's degree in physics or an engineering discipline" is satisfied by a part-time, online, community-college-transfer degree exactly as by a four-year on-campus one. The recruiter-stage filter checks the field and the fact of the degree — not how it was delivered or how fast.

One honest nuance remains, at the later human-judgment stages. A particular interviewer or hiring manager may, informally and unevenly, weigh a school's or program's reputation as one signal among many once they are already evaluating you. That is the same kind of individual discretion the first lesson warned against building a plan around — in either direction. Do not let it talk you out of an accredited program that fits your life. Do not assume it will never matter, either. It is a small, uneven factor sitting on top of a credential that, at the filter itself, works the same however it was earned.

::: key
Accreditation, not delivery mode, is what a posting's degree line checks. Part-time, online, and community-college-transfer routes to an accredited degree satisfy it exactly as a four-year residential degree does; an unaccredited program, however legitimate it looks, does not satisfy it at all.
:::

## The master's as a conversion route

Everything so far assumes a first bachelor's in a qualifying field. What if your bachelor's is in something else entirely — biology, history, music?

Recall Sr. GNC Engineer's basic qualifications. Beside its 5-year and 7-year branches sits a third: a **[[postgraduate degree|postgrad]]** in a related field, as a qualifying condition all by itself. An accredited master's in an engineering discipline, physics, or a closely related quantitative field can meet that line on its own terms, whatever the earlier bachelor's was in.

Be precise about what this does and does not fix.

- **It fixes** the field-of-degree problem for someone whose first degree is in an unrelated subject. A second bachelor's would fix that too, but usually at far greater cost and time, because a second bachelor's often restarts general-education requirements that a master's does not repeat.
- **It does not** supply the years of GNC-relevant professional experience that most postings below the senior tier also require. And it costs real time — typically on the order of one and a half to two and a half years of study — plus real tuition of its own.

For most readers in this situation, a master's is the right tool *when the experience side is already being built some other way*: through Path 2 or Path 3, or through this curriculum's demonstrated work, read generously at the interview stage. It is not a complete, stand-alone solution.

::: key
A postgraduate degree in a related field is Sr. GNC Engineer's third qualifying route, independent of the field of an earlier bachelor's degree. It solves the field-of-degree problem for a career-changer; it does not by itself supply GNC-relevant professional experience, and it costs real time and tuition of its own.
:::

## Why the pairing is the real differentiator

An ordinary undergraduate program, however demanding, is built from problem sets, labs and separate assignments, each graded on its own. It rarely asks a student to build one whole, working system from end to end — an integrator that does not drift, a filter tuned against real noise, a guidance law that survives a dispersion campaign — and carry it from a blank file to a checked result.

That is exactly what this curriculum's exercises ask for, module after module. And it is exactly what almost no new graduate brings to an interview, because it was never assigned.

From a hiring manager's chair, a graduating **[[cohort|cohort]]** applying to the same postings looks like a big group of people who each cleared the same filter and each hold a broadly similar transcript. The candidate who can *also* walk an interviewer through a shipped 6-DOF simulator, an estimator tested against a known trajectory, and a written-up guidance-law derivation is not offering a slightly better resume. They are offering direct evidence of the very skills the interview exists to test — in a pool where almost nobody else can.

::: key
Path 1 pairs an accredited degree, arranged to fit around work, with this curriculum's demonstrated work the entire time. The degree clears the recruiter-stage filter; the demonstrated work is the differentiator against a cohort that cleared the same filter with coursework alone.
:::

::: example A community-college-transfer route, priced and paced
A reader needs 120 credits for an accredited bachelor's in physics. Under a standard articulation agreement, 60 lower-division credits can come from an accredited community college. That leaves 60 upper-division credits at a four-year university's part-time online completion program.

The prices below are **illustrative** — real rates vary enormously by state, school and residency, so check them directly.

**Cost of the community-college block.** At \$150 **[[per credit|per-credit]]**:

$$
60 \times 150 = 9000 \text{ dollars}
$$

**Cost of the university block.** At \$400 per credit:

$$
60 \times 400 = 24000 \text{ dollars}
$$

**Total:** $9000 + 24000 = 33000$, so about \$33,000. Sanity check: half the credits cost only about a quarter of the money ($9000 / 33000 \approx 0.27$), which is the whole point of the transfer.

**Time for the university block.** At a sustainable part-time pace of six credits per semester:

$$
60 / 6 = 10 \text{ semesters}
$$

With two semesters a year, that is $10 / 2 = 5$ years. Add a summer term each year — three terms a year — and it drops to $10 / 3 \approx 3.3$ years. That sits inside this path's typical three-to-five-year range.

**Sanity check on the whole route.** Those figures count only the university half. A reader starting from zero also has to earn the 60 community-college credits, and at the same six credits a term that is another 10 terms. The full degree at that pace would be $120 / 6 / 3 \approx 6.7$ years. The three-to-five-year range is realistic if some credits are already earned (many career-changers have some) or if the load is heavier — at nine credits a term, three terms a year, $120 / 9 / 3 \approx 4.4$ years.
:::

::: example Two new graduates, same degree, same posting
Two candidates graduate the same term with accredited bachelor's degrees in aerospace engineering. Both apply to the same New Graduate Engineer, GNC posting. Both clear the basic qualifications identically.

- **Candidate one:** a strong transcript and course projects completed for a grade, none published or kept up after being handed in.
- **Candidate two:** over the same four years, on their own time, using a curriculum built for this, also built a fixed-step 6-DOF integrator with unit tests, a Monte Carlo dispersion campaign backing up a landing-accuracy claim, and an attitude estimator written from scratch and checked against a reference trajectory.

**At the resume screen** they look almost the same: same field, similar school, similar experience lines.

**At the technical interview** the difference is no longer subtle. Candidate two can walk an interviewer through real design decisions, real failures found and fixed, and a real checked result — exactly the conversation the interview is built to have. Candidate one's coursework-only record cannot support that conversation at the same depth.

The degree got both of them into the room. The demonstrated work decided what happened once they were in it.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
A reader with limited savings asks whether a part-time online bachelor's from a regionally accredited public university will be read as a lesser credential than a four-year residential degree by a GNC Engineer posting's basic qualifications. Answer precisely.
:::

::: answer
At the basic-qualifications filter itself, no. The posting checks the degree's field and the fact of accreditation, not how it was delivered or how fast, so a regionally accredited part-time online degree satisfies the line exactly as an on-campus one does. At later, human-judgment stages, an individual interviewer *might* informally weigh a school's reputation as one signal among others — unevenly and unpredictably. That is real, but it is not something to plan around, and not a reason to avoid an accredited program that actually fits the reader's life and budget.
:::

::: check
Explain why community college transfer is a genuine cost- and time-saving route rather than a compromise on the quality of the eventual degree.
:::

::: answer
The final degree is granted by the four-year accredited institution the credits transfer into. It carries that institution's accreditation and degree title, wherever the lower-division credits were first earned. Transfer changes *where* and *how cheaply* the general-education and introductory courses are done — often under formal articulation agreements that guarantee how credits apply. It does not change the accreditation or the field of the final degree, which is what a posting's basic qualifications actually check.
:::

::: check
A reader holds a bachelor's degree in biology and eight years of unrelated professional experience. Using this lesson's discussion of the master's-as-conversion route, name the most direct accredited path to satisfying a GNC posting's degree-field line, and state what it would still leave unresolved.
:::

::: answer
An accredited master's in an engineering discipline, physics, or a closely related quantitative field. It meets Sr. GNC Engineer's postgraduate-degree route directly, whatever the bachelor's was in, and is usually faster and cheaper than a second bachelor's starting again from general education. What it leaves unresolved: the years of GNC-relevant professional experience that most postings also weigh or require. Eight years of *unrelated* work does not count toward that. A master's answers the field-of-degree question, not the experience question, which this reader would still need to build through one of the other paths or through demonstrated work.
:::

::: check
Why does this lesson claim that almost no new graduate arrives at an interview with demonstrated, shipped GNC-relevant work, when many graduates complete substantial coursework projects?
:::

::: answer
Ordinary coursework is usually built from problem sets, labs and assignments graded separately, not as one whole system carried from a blank start to a checked, working result — the kind of thing this curriculum's exercises specifically ask for. Because that kind of integrated, self-directed project is rarely assigned, most graduates are never asked to produce one. So a candidate who has one is unusual in the pool, not merely a little ahead.
:::

::: check
A reader has just enrolled in an accredited part-time bachelor's program and asks whether Path 1 is now "handled" for the next three to five years. What is the accurate answer, based on this lesson and the previous one?
:::

::: answer
No. Enrolling handles the *credential* half of Path 1. The previous lesson showed that a credential and demonstrated capability solve different problems at different stages, and this lesson's two-graduates comparison shows that a bare degree leaves a graduate hard to tell apart from a cohort that cleared the same filter. The accurate plan uses the degree's years *in parallel* with this curriculum's demonstrated work, not one after the other. "Handled" would only be true if the plan included both halves from the start.
:::

::: context shipped What "shipped" means
In software, to **ship** something is to finish it and put it out where others can use it — the word comes from literally shipping boxed products to stores. Shipped work is done, tested and usable, not a half-finished experiment on your laptop. For a portfolio, "shipped" means someone else can download your simulator, run it, and get the results you claim.
:::

::: context institutional-accreditor Accrediting a whole school
A **regional accreditor** checks an entire college or university — its finances, teaching, and standards — rather than one program. The US historically had several, each covering a region of the country. Federal rules no longer formally separate "regional" from "national" accreditors, so you may also see the term **institutional accreditor**. The US Department of Education and the Council for Higher Education Accreditation (CHEA) both publish lists of recognized accreditors, which is the place to check that an accreditor is itself real.
:::

::: context diploma-mill Fakes that look real
A **diploma mill** is an outfit that sells degrees with little or no real study, or runs courses without recognized accreditation. Some even invent their own official-sounding "accreditors" — sometimes called **accreditation mills** — so that the claim "we are accredited" is technically true and completely worthless. That is why the check has to be against a list of accreditors that employers and the government actually recognize, not against what the school's own website says.
:::

::: context community-college How transfer fits together
A **community college** is a public, usually two-year college. Its main degree, the **associate degree**, is typically about 60 credits — roughly half a bachelor's — and many students use it as the first half of one.

::: context gen-ed What "general education" is
**General education**, or "gen ed," is the set of broad courses every bachelor's student takes whatever their major: writing, some mathematics, a science, history or social science, arts or humanities. It usually makes up a large share of the first two years. It is the part that transfers most easily from a community college — and the part a second bachelor's may make you repeat.
:::

::: context articulation Where "articulation" comes from
*Articulus* is Latin for "a small joint" — the same root as "articulated" in an articulated bus, which bends at a joint. An **articulation agreement** is the joint between two colleges: a written promise that a course taken at one counts as a specific course at the other. Without one, a receiving school may accept your credits only as vague "electives" that do not count toward your major.
:::

::: context competency-based Learning at your own speed
In a **competency-based** program, you pass a course by showing you can do what it teaches — usually by passing an assessment or project — as soon as you are ready. If you already know half the material, you do not sit through it again. The best-known US example is Western Governors University, a nonprofit, accredited online school; its degrees lean toward computing, business, health and teaching rather than engineering, which is why this route fits some fields better than others.
:::

::: context postgrad Bachelor's, master's, and beyond
**Undergraduate** study leads to a bachelor's degree. Anything after that is **postgraduate** (or **graduate**) study. A **master's** degree usually takes one to two and a half years; a **doctorate** (PhD) usually takes several more. Many master's programs in engineering accept students whose bachelor's is in another field, sometimes after a few catch-up courses in mathematics and physics first.
:::

::: context cohort Standing out in a crowd
*Cohors* was a unit of the Roman army — about a tenth of a legion — whose soldiers trained and fought together. Today a **cohort** is any group that moves through something together, such as everyone graduating in the same year. In a cohort that all cleared the same filter, the transcripts look alike; shipped projects are rare.
