Picture a club with a bouncer at the door. The bouncer has a list. If your name is not on it, you do not get in — and it does not matter how well you dance, because the dance floor is on the other side of the door. Nobody inside ever sees you dance.

A job posting has a list like that too. The previous module, *Levels and the Exact Qualification Lines*, showed you SpaceX's **[[basic qualifications|basic-quals]]** — the must-have lines in a posting — the way they are actually printed. For essentially every GNC Engineer posting, one of those lines is a bachelor's degree in one of a small set of fields. Here is the plain fact this whole module starts from: **no amount of self-study, on this platform or any other, satisfies a line that reads "bachelor's degree in."** That is true however good the self-study is.

Why would a course about guidance, navigation and control spend a module on this? Because getting it wrong costs years. A platform that let you believe a strong portfolio makes the degree optional would send you off to discover the truth one rejected application at a time. A platform that told you the degree is the *only* thing that matters, or that no route exists without one, would be equally dishonest — working GNC engineers reach this field every year by routes other than a traditional four-year degree. The honest position sits between those two mistakes, and it is specific: the degree line is real, it is enforced early and mechanically for the roles that carry it, and there are exactly three routes past it, which this module names and prices.

This lesson is about one piece of that: *where* in a real hiring process the degree line does its work, and why it does it early instead of late. Get this right and the rest of the module stands on firm ground. Get it wrong — imagine, say, that a great interview could make up for a missing degree — and every plan you build on that belief costs time you did not need to spend.

## The lines, as they are actually printed

The argument depends on what the lines really say, so here they are once more.

- **New Graduate Engineer, GNC.** Required: a bachelor's degree in computer science, aerospace, physics, or an engineering discipline, plus software development experience in either C++ or Python.
- **GNC Engineer Level I to II.** A bachelor's degree in physics or an engineering discipline, plus 2+ years of professional experience in control systems, orbital mechanics, classical dynamics, aerodynamics, sensors and actuators, modeling and simulation, and the languages C++ and Python. Some Dragon-specific postings have been seen asking for 1+ years instead.
- **Sr. GNC Engineer.** Three alternatives. A bachelor's in computer science, aerospace, physics or engineering plus 5+ years of professional experience in GNC or software engineering; *or* 7+ years of professional experience **[[in lieu of|in-lieu]]** a degree; *or* a postgraduate degree in a related field.
- **Site Reliability Engineer, GNC** (SRE for short, said letter by letter). A bachelor's in computer science, information systems/IT, engineering, math, or a scientific discipline plus 2+ years of software development experience; *or* 4+ years of professional experience building software with site reliability or DevOps — the work of keeping software systems built, deployed and running — in lieu of a degree. Either way, also 1+ years with Linux and 1+ years with Python.

"In lieu of" means "in place of." Read across the list and the shape of the whole module is already there.

Most of the lines name a bachelor's degree in a specific field with **no alternative at all**. Two lines — the senior role's 7+ years and the SRE role's 4+ years — name a specific number of years of a specific kind of experience as a real substitute. Nothing sits in between. No posting in this set writes "strong independent projects" into its basic qualifications as a stand-in for the degree.

Projects matter a great deal elsewhere in the process — the next lesson is about exactly that. They do not matter *here*, at the one place the requirement is stated.

One honest caution: postings change their wording from year to year. Treat the lines above as a fair picture of how these roles have been described, not a permanent transcript. Read the posting you are actually applying to for its own current text.

## Three things that all sound like "the degree requirement"

The most useful habit this lesson can give you is telling apart three things that all get called "the degree requirement." They are not the same thing, and mixing them up wastes more effort than any other mistake in this corner of a job search.

1. **What the posting states.** The literal sentence in the basic qualifications, printed the same way for every applicant. This tells you the rule as written.
2. **What that sentence does in practice.** For a role with no in-lieu clause, it works as a hard, nearly mechanical **filter** — a yes/no check applied to your resume before anyone with engineering judgment reads it. This tells you what will happen to a stranger's application, which decides the outcome for almost everyone who applies. The rest of this lesson is about this one.
3. **What an individual hiring manager might do** with a borderline case that reaches them directly — through a **[[referral|referral]]**, say, or at a small company where the person reading resumes is also the interviewer. This is real. But it differs from company to company and person to person, you cannot see it from outside, and a plan should not depend on it. It is the same caution the ITAR-gate module gave about a discretionary export-control authorization: exceptions exist somewhere in the system, but that does not mean one is coming for you.

Each of the three answers a different question. Am I reading the requirement correctly? What will actually happen to my application if I do not meet it? Is the system made of people rather than robots? The answer to the third is yes — but that does not give you permission to plan around being the exception.

::: key
Three different things: what a posting states (the printed line); what that line functions as in practice (a filter applied before technical evaluation, for roles with no in-lieu clause); and what an individual hiring manager may do with a borderline case reaching them directly (real, inconsistent, and not a plan).
:::

## Why the recruiter stage, not the interview

After you apply, a typical hiring **[[pipeline|pipeline]]** runs like this:

1. A **recruiter screen** — a short check by a recruiter, whose job is to find candidates and move them through the process.
2. One or more technical conversations with engineers from the team.
3. Eventually, a hiring decision.

Every stage after the first uses the time of people whose job is *not* screening resumes. An engineer reads a take-home exercise. A panel blocks out an afternoon. A hiring manager weighs a final call. That time is a real cost, and the company pays it whether or not the candidate is hired.

Now look at a degree-field requirement from the screener's chair. It is **cheap** to check: one line on a resume. It is **unambiguous**: compare that line to a short list of fields. It needs no judgment about how good a project is. And it is stated as a *requirement*, not a preference.

Cheap, unambiguous, and required — that is exactly the kind of check you put first, before the expensive stages. Ask it late, and you pay for every stage in between on every candidate who was never going to pass. Ask it early, and the expensive stages are spent only on people who could actually get an offer. This is not a claim about one company's **[[software|ats]]**. It is the ordinary arithmetic of screening a large pile of applications — the same reason the bouncer stands at the door, not at the bar.

What this means for you: for a role that lists a bachelor's degree as a basic qualification with no in-lieu clause, the degree is very unlikely to *come up* in an interview — because a resume that does not clear it is very unlikely to *produce* an interview. The fix is not "prepare a better answer for when they ask." The fix comes earlier than that.

::: warning A strong interview does not reach back and fix a missing basic qualification
It is tempting to imagine that a great technical conversation could make a missing degree stop mattering, the way a strong answer can rescue a shaky start in an interview. A basic-qualifications filter does not work that way. For a role where the degree is a stated basic qualification with no in-lieu clause, the filter runs *before* there is an interview to be strong in. Time spent preparing for an interview you are unlikely to be offered is time not spent on one of the three paths the rest of this module describes.
:::

::: key
A basic-qualifications line is cheap to check and unambiguous to check it against, which is exactly why it is applied at the recruiter stage, before any technical evaluation, for roles that carry it with no in-lieu clause. This is ordinary high-volume screening economics, not a claim about any one company's specific tools.
:::

## What "essentially every" is honest about

The phrase "essentially every" is honest in two directions at once.

In one direction it is a strong claim. Across the roles this curriculum has quoted, the degree-field line is present, stated as a requirement, and has no alternative. Do not read hope into a posting that does not offer any.

In the other direction, "essentially every" is not "every, without exception." And the exceptions are not noise. They are SRE, GNC's explicit 4+ years in lieu of a degree and Sr. GNC Engineer's explicit 7+ years in lieu of a degree — written with the same precision as the degree requirement itself. They are the machinery behind two of this module's three real paths.

So the honest summary is neither "the degree is required" nor "the degree is optional." It is narrower and more useful: the degree-field line is a hard filter almost everywhere you look; a small number of specific roles name a specific number of years as a real substitute; and knowing exactly which roles those are is worth more than a strong opinion about the industry in general.

::: key
"Essentially every" is accurate in both directions: the degree-field line is a hard, alternative-free filter on the large majority of GNC Engineer postings, and a small number of specific roles — named by this module's later lessons — state a specific number of years of experience as an explicit, genuine alternative.
:::

::: example Two resumes, one posting, and where each one stops
A recruiter is working through applications for a GNC Engineer Level I to II posting — one of the Dragon-variant postings, whose basic qualifications ask for a bachelor's degree in physics or an engineering discipline plus 1+ years of relevant professional experience.

- **Resume A:** a bachelor's in aerospace engineering, fourteen months of relevant professional experience, and no independent projects beyond coursework.
- **Resume B:** three years of independent, publicly documented work — a fixed-step **[[6-DOF|six-dof]]** simulator, a validated attitude estimator, and a **[[Monte Carlo dispersion campaign|monte-carlo]]** — and no bachelor's degree at all.

Check each against the lines as printed.

Resume A: degree in a named field — yes. Experience: fourteen months is more than the 1+ year asked for — yes. It moves forward, and how strong A really is becomes the interviewer's question.

Resume B: degree — no. This posting has no in-lieu clause to route around that. The quality of the work does not change which box the resume fails to tick. It stops here.

(Sanity check: had this been a standard posting asking for 2+ years, A's fourteen months would have fallen short too — the same printed-line logic cuts both ways.)

Now send the same Resume B to Site Reliability Engineer, GNC instead. The picture changes completely *if* the years and the kind of experience line up, because that posting names an alternative the Level I to II posting does not. Same candidate, same work, two postings, two outcomes — decided by which line each posting contains, not by how good the work is.
:::

::: example A referral reaches a person; a cold application reaches a queue
A candidate's degree is in **[[engineering physics|engineering-physics]]** — a real, accredited field that does not appear word for word in most GNC postings' short list of fields.

Sent in **cold** — through the general online application, with no one inside vouching for them — the resume is read against the literal list. A screener moving through a big stack has neither the time nor, usually, the authority to decide whether "engineering physics" counts as "an engineering discipline." The safe, fast move is to apply the line as printed.

Now the same resume is handed straight to the hiring manager by a current employee who used to work with the candidate. The hiring manager *does* have the standing to read "engineering physics" and decide it satisfies the line, because they are weighing a known person, not a stack entry.

Both outcomes are real. Neither is the one to plan around by default, because a candidate cannot create the second situation on demand. What changed between them was not the strength of the degree. It was whether a human with discretion saw the resume before the mechanical filter would have stopped it.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
A candidate reasons: "the posting says 'engineering discipline,' and my degree is technically an engineering discipline, so a recruiter reading strictly will let it through." Using the three-things distinction from this lesson, explain what this reasoning is actually claiming, and what would make it wrong in practice for a specific candidate.
:::

::: answer
The reasoning mixes up what the posting states (thing one) with what an individual might decide about a borderline case (thing three). Whether "technically an engineering discipline" holds up depends on how closely the degree's actual title matches the posting's field words. A screener working through volume applies the printed line; they do not make a careful case-by-case call on every unusual title. So it goes wrong whenever the degree's name sits far enough from the posting's named fields that a screener reading quickly does not recognize it as a match. That is a real risk for genuinely related but differently named fields, and the word "technically" does not remove it.
:::

::: check
Why does a degree-field requirement get applied earlier in a pipeline than a judgment about the quality of a candidate's independent projects, even when the projects are genuinely strong?
:::

::: answer
A degree-field check takes seconds and needs no engineering judgment: compare one line to a short list. Judging independent projects needs a qualified reviewer's time and technical judgment — the expensive resource the later interview stages exist to spend carefully. Doing the cheap, clear check first means the expensive judgment is spent only on candidates who could actually receive an offer. That is why the degree line is checked at the recruiter stage instead of being weighed alongside project quality in an interview.
:::

::: check
A friend says: "if the degree really mattered that much, they'd bring it up in the interview, and I could make my case then." What is wrong with this plan for a posting where the degree is a basic qualification with no in-lieu clause?
:::

::: answer
The plan assumes the friend will reach an interview whatever happens with the degree line. But the whole point of checking that line at the recruiter stage is to stop exactly that for candidates who do not meet it. With no in-lieu clause, a resume that fails the degree-field line is unlikely to produce a technical interview at all, so there is usually no interview in which to make the case. The place to act is *before* the application is screened — by choosing a path, or a posting, where the line can actually be met or where a real alternative exists.
:::

::: check
State, without hedging, what "essentially every" is claiming about the degree line, and name the two specific exceptions this module's later lessons build on.
:::

::: answer
"Essentially every" claims that the degree-field line is a hard requirement, with no built-in alternative, on the large majority of GNC Engineer postings. The two named exceptions are Site Reliability Engineer, GNC's 4+ years of professional experience in lieu of a degree, and Sr. GNC Engineer's 7+ years of professional experience in lieu of a degree. Both are written into the posting language itself, not guessed at.
:::

::: check
Explain why "the filter is not applied uniformly everywhere" and "a portfolio does not substitute for the degree where the degree is a hard filter" are both true statements at the same time, rather than contradicting each other.
:::

::: answer
They describe different layers. "Not applied uniformly" is about variation *between* doors: across roles (some carry an in-lieu clause, some do not), across companies (a small company may route a resume to a person with more discretion than a big queue does), and now and then across individuals with the standing to make an exception. "Does not substitute" is about *one* door: a specific application to a specific posting with no in-lieu clause and no human discretion stepping in. There the filter is mechanical, and a portfolio does not change which line a resume fails. Which door you knock on varies; how a single closed door works does not bend to the quality of what you bring.
:::

## Summary

| Fact | What it means for you |
| --- | --- |
| Degree-field line, no in-lieu clause | A hard filter, applied at the recruiter stage, before technical evaluation |
| Sr. GNC Engineer's 7+ years | A genuine, stated alternative to the degree, at the senior tier |
| SRE, GNC's 4+ years | A genuine, stated alternative to the degree, tied to software/SRE experience |
| The posting's stated line | What the requirement says — read it for the role you are actually applying to |
| What the line functions as | How a stranger's resume is actually screened against it, for roles with no alternative |
| What a hiring manager may do | Real, inconsistent, reachable mainly through direct human contact — not a plan |

The next lesson turns from the degree line to this curriculum itself: what it can and cannot do about that line — the honest boundary between building real capability and producing a credential — and why that boundary is exactly where the three paths begin.

::: context basic-quals Must-haves and nice-to-haves
Most engineering postings split their wish list in two. **Basic qualifications** (sometimes "required" or "minimum" qualifications) are the must-haves: miss one and the application is usually out. **Preferred qualifications** are the nice-to-haves: they help you stand out, but nobody is rejected for lacking a few. The degree line lives in the first list. That single fact is why the rest of this module exists.
:::

::: context in-lieu Where "in lieu" comes from
*Lieu* is the French word for "place," and it came into English through old legal French. So "in lieu of a degree" means, word for word, "in place of a degree." You will meet the same word in "lieutenant" — someone who holds a place for a higher officer. In a posting, the phrase is precise: it names exactly what the listed years of experience are allowed to replace, and nothing more.
:::

::: context referral What a referral is
A **referral** is when someone who already works at a company passes your name and resume to the hiring team, usually through an internal system, and says "this person is worth a look." Many companies pay employees a bonus when a referred candidate is hired. A referral can get your resume read sooner, and sometimes by a person rather than a queue. It does not rewrite a posting's basic qualifications.
:::

::: context pipeline Where the degree check sits
A hiring pipeline narrows at each stage. The cheap, yes/no checks sit at the wide top; the expensive human judgment sits lower down.

::: context ats Resume-screening software
Many large employers collect applications through an **applicant tracking system**, usually shortened to **ATS** (said letter by letter, "A-T-S"). It stores resumes, tracks which stage each candidate is in, and can ask yes/no questions on the application form — "Do you hold a bachelor's degree in one of these fields?" How any particular company sets its system up is not something you can see from outside. The lesson's point does not depend on it: whether a person or a program does the first pass, a cheap, clear requirement gets checked first.
:::

::: context six-dof Six ways to move
**DOF** stands for "degrees of freedom" — the separate ways something can move. A rigid body in space has six: it can slide along three directions (forward–back, left–right, up–down) and rotate about three axes (roll, pitch, yaw). A **6-DOF simulator** tracks all six at once, which is what you need to fly a rocket or a spacecraft on a computer. Building one is a centerpiece of this curriculum's later modules.

::: context monte-carlo Rolling the dice thousands of times
A **Monte Carlo** run (named after the casino in Monaco) simulates the same flight thousands of times, each with slightly different random values — a heavier payload, a gustier wind, a sensor that reads a little high. A **dispersion campaign** is a planned set of such runs. Instead of one answer, you get a spread of answers, and you can say something like "99% of landings fall within this circle." It is everyday work in real GNC teams.
:::

::: context engineering-physics A degree with an awkward name
**Engineering physics** (sometimes "applied physics") is a degree that mixes a physics major's theory with an engineer's design courses. Many are fully accredited, and some carry ABET engineering accreditation. The trouble is only the name: it is not word for word "physics" or "an engineering discipline," so a fast reader comparing it to a list might hesitate. Degrees like this are exactly where the difference between a queue and a person shows up.
:::
