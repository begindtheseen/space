This is the lesson in the module that looks least like engineering, and it has the highest return per hour of effort in the whole thing. Nothing in it is difficult. Most of it costs nothing. And it addresses a failure mode that reaches candidates who have prepared thoroughly for every round and then lose two of them to something entirely outside the technical material.

The argument is structural, and the fifth lesson already laid its foundation. The onsite is five to seven back-to-back interviews over eight or more hours. The last two rounds of that day are scored exactly like the first two: they generate their own reports, by their own interviewers, who will contribute those reports to the same debrief. But most candidates are measurably worse by then — slower to structure an answer, thinner in the follow-ups, less able to produce a coherent ninety-second story. That gap between how a round is scored and how a candidate actually performs in it is the thing this lesson exists to close.

::: key
Sleep the two nights before, not just the night before. Eat something that does not spike and crash. Carry water. Ask for a five-minute break between rounds if you need one — it is normal and nobody marks you down for it.
:::

## Sleep the two nights before

The instruction is to sleep well on both of the two preceding nights, and the reason is specific rather than general good advice.

The night immediately before a high-stakes day is the night you are least able to control. It is the one where you are running rounds in your head at one in the morning, where you wake at four and check the time, where the alarm you set for six has been checked three times. Treating that night as your sleep plan means your plan depends on the one night most likely to fail.

The night before that is entirely controllable, and it is far enough ahead that nothing is churning yet. Plan around it: that is the night to be asleep early, and it is the night that determines what you have in reserve if the second one goes badly. If both go well, you have lost nothing.

The rest follows from the same logic. Travel the day before if travel is involved, so that a delayed flight is an inconvenience rather than a catastrophe. Know where the building is and how long the journey takes before the morning you have to do it. Lay out what you are taking the night before. Every one of these removes a decision from a morning that will have enough of them.

## Food and water

Eat something that does not spike and crash. That is the card's phrasing and it is the right level of precision: a large fast-carbohydrate lunch is followed by a slump, and in this schedule the slump lands squarely in the early-afternoon rounds. Whatever you normally eat that leaves you level is the right choice; the day of the onsite is not the day to try something new, in either direction.

Carry water. This is the single cheapest item in the lesson and the one most often skipped. You will be handed between rooms with a few minutes in a corridor and there is no guarantee that any of those gaps contains a chance to find a drink. Talking for eight hours is thirsty work and a dry throat in the middle of an explanation is a distraction you do not need to have arranged for yourself.

On caffeine: the honest advice is that people differ enough here that a general prescription would be worse than none. Two things are safe to say. Do not change your habit on the day — a candidate who does not normally drink coffee and has two before a presentation has added a variable she has no data on. And be aware that caffeine taken in the afternoon is still working in the evening for many people, which is how a stimulant taken to rescue the 15:45 round ends up affecting the night after the onsite. The day-simulation exercise in this module is where you find out what your own response actually is, under conditions you can afford to learn it in.

## Asking for a break

Between rounds, if you need five minutes, ask for them. The module states plainly that this is normal and nobody marks you down for it, and there is a mechanism behind that which is worth understanding because it removes the anxiety.

Your interviewer's report is more useful to everyone, including them, if it reflects what you can actually do. An interviewer does not benefit from evaluating a depleted version of you; they have to write down an assessment they will stand behind at the debrief, and an assessment distorted by the fact that you had not had water since nine in the morning is worse for them than a five-minute delay.

How to ask is simple and it helps to have the sentence ready rather than composing it while depleted. "Could I take five minutes before we start?" is sufficient. It does not need a justification, an apology, or an explanation of why. If you want to be more specific, "I'd like to grab some water and use the restroom, could I have five minutes?" is equally fine. What does not work is the thing candidates do instead, which is to say nothing and spend the next forty-five minutes at reduced capacity to avoid a twenty-second exchange.

::: warning The break you did not ask for costs more than the break you did
Five minutes is about one-ninth of a forty-five-minute round. A round you spend distracted, dehydrated or unable to concentrate is not one-ninth degraded — it is a weak report in the debrief. The asymmetry is enormous and it runs entirely one way. If you are wondering whether you need a break, that is already the answer.
:::

## Simulate the day before you live it

The module's third exercise asks you to block out a day and run the rounds back to back: a technical presentation with questions, two coding sessions, a systems discussion, a domain round and a behavioural round, with only short breaks. Then record where your performance degraded and by what hour.

That last instruction is the point of the exercise. You do not have general knowledge of when you fall off; you have specific knowledge, and it is different from other people's. Some candidates lose precision first, some lose speed, some lose the ability to structure a spoken answer while retaining the ability to solve the problem. Knowing which one you are, and at roughly what hour it begins, turns the logistics plan from generic advice into a plan aimed at your actual failure mode.

It is also the only honest way to find out whether your logistics plan works, because a plan that has never been tested is a hypothesis.

::: example What a simulation actually reveals
A candidate blocks out a day two weeks before her onsite and runs six rounds against a timer: a forty-five-minute presentation with questions from a friend who has agreed to be difficult, two forty-five-minute coding sessions, a systems discussion, a domain round and a behavioural round, with ten-minute gaps and a half-hour for lunch.

Starting at 09:00 the schedule runs: presentation to 09:45; first coding session 09:55 to 10:40; second 10:50 to 11:35; lunch to 12:05; systems 12:05 to 12:50; domain 13:00 to 13:45; behavioural 14:00 to 14:45. That is six blocks of forty-five minutes — four and a half hours of actual work inside a day running five hours and forty-five minutes from start to finish.

It is shorter than the real onsite, and that turns out to be the most useful thing about it.

Her notes afterwards: the presentation was strong. The first coding session was fine. The second was noticeably slower to start, and she caught herself writing before she had stated an approach — the exact habit she had spent three weeks building. The systems discussion after lunch was her worst block of the day; she was flat and gave short answers. The domain round recovered somewhat. The behavioural round at 14:00 was poor in a specific way: she knew all her stories, and she could not compress them, so each one ran well past two minutes.

Three findings, all actionable. Her failure mode is not knowledge, it is the discipline that sits on top of knowledge — stating the approach before coding, compressing a narrative — and it begins to go at around the four-hour mark. The post-lunch block is her worst, which is a food question. And all of this happened inside five and three-quarter hours, while the real day is eight or more, which means the version she has just measured is the optimistic one.

Her plan changes accordingly: a lighter lunch, water carried, a deliberate five-minute break before the post-lunch round, and two more weeks of drilling the two disciplines that failed rather than the material that did not.
:::

::: example Two candidates at 15:30
Two candidates reach the second-to-last round of a real onsite day at 15:30. Both have been there since nine. Both have one round after this one.

The first has had a coffee, a sandwich at the lunch table where she was talking to two engineers and ate half of it, and no water since the morning. She has not asked for a break all day, because each time the thought came up the next interviewer was already walking her to the room and it felt like a strange thing to raise. She is not in distress; she is simply tired, slightly dehydrated, and noticeably slower than she was at ten in the morning. In the systems round at 15:30 she gives a reasonable answer that does not develop, and misses the trade-off framing she had practised. In the behavioural round at 16:15 she tells a good story badly, spending ninety seconds on the situation, which is the failure mode she had specifically drilled against.

Neither round is a disaster. Both are weaker reports than she was capable of producing, and both count.

The second candidate, at the 15:20 handoff, says: "Could I take five minutes before we start? I'd like some water." The interviewer says of course and points her at the kitchen. She drinks a full glass, uses the restroom, stands outside for two minutes without looking at her phone, and walks into the systems round at 15:27. It goes well. She does the same thing again before the last round, this time without needing to ask because there is a natural gap, and her behavioural round is as sharp as her morning presentation.

The difference between these two days is roughly ten minutes and one sentence spoken aloud.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
State the structural argument for why stamina planning affects the outcome rather than merely your comfort.
:::

::: answer
The onsite is five to seven consecutive interviews over eight or more hours, and the final rounds are scored exactly like the first ones — each generates its own report from its own interviewer, all of which reach the same debrief. Most candidates perform measurably worse by the end. So degradation does not cost comfort, it costs evaluated rounds, and the interventions that prevent it are cheap.
:::

::: check
Why does the advice specify the two nights before rather than the night before?
:::

::: answer
Because the night immediately before a high-stakes day is the one you are least able to control — that is the night you lie awake rehearsing. Making it your whole sleep plan means the plan rests on the most likely night to fail. The night before that is fully controllable and far enough ahead that nothing is churning, so it is the one that determines what you have in reserve if the second goes badly.
:::

:::

::: answer

The asymmetry is also stark: five minutes is about one-ninth of a forty-five-minute round, while a round spent depleted is a weak report. The sentence is simply "Could I take five minutes before we start?" — no justification and no apology required.
:::

::: check
What is the purpose of recording where your performance degraded and by what hour, rather than simply concluding that long days are tiring?
:::

::: answer
Because the failure mode is individual and the generic conclusion is not actionable. Some candidates lose precision first, some lose speed, some retain the ability to solve a problem while losing the ability to structure a spoken answer. Knowing which one you are, and roughly when it starts, turns a logistics plan into something aimed at your actual weakness — and it is also the only way to test whether the plan works at all, since an untested plan is a hypothesis.
:::

::: check
Why is the day of the onsite the wrong day to change your caffeine or food habits in either direction?
:::

::: answer
Because either change introduces a variable you have no data on, on the one day where the cost of being wrong is highest. A candidate who does not normally drink coffee and has two before a presentation has added an unknown; so has one who skips her usual breakfast. The place to learn your own response is the day-simulation exercise, where the information is free. On the day itself, the objective is to be the version of yourself you have already measured.
:::

## Summary

| Item | Instruction | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sleep | Both of the two nights before | The last night is the one you cannot control |
| Travel | The day before, where applicable | A delay becomes an inconvenience, not a catastrophe |
| Food | Something that does not spike and crash; nothing new | The slump lands in the early-afternoon rounds |
| Water | Carry it | Handoffs do not guarantee a chance to find any |
| Caffeine | Do not change your habit; know your own response | Individual variation is large; afternoon doses have a long tail |
| Breaks | Ask for five minutes; no justification needed | Normal, not marked down, and hugely asymmetric in your favour |
| Rehearsal | Simulate the rounds back to back and record when you degrade | The failure mode is individual and the plan needs testing |
