Picture a soccer tournament where your team plays five games in one day. The last game counts for exactly as much as the first. But by the last game your legs are heavy, you are thirsty, and you are a step slower to every ball. The team that planned its water, food and rest between games often beats a more skilled team that did not.

A job interview day can work the same way. This lesson looks the least like engineering of anything in the module, and it may pay back the most per hour of effort. Nothing in it is hard. Most of it costs nothing. And it fixes a problem that catches candidates who prepared well for every round, then lose two of those rounds to something that has nothing to do with the technical material.

The argument is about structure, and lesson five laid the ground for it. The **[[onsite|onsite-day]]** — the full day of interviews at the company — is five to seven back-to-back interviews over eight or more hours. The last two rounds are scored exactly like the first two. Each produces its own **[[report|interview-report]]**, written by its own interviewer, and every report goes into the same **debrief** — the meeting where the decision gets made. But most candidates are measurably worse by the end of the day. They are slower to organize an answer. Their follow-up answers are thinner. They struggle to tell a clear ninety-second story. The gap between how a round is scored and how a tired candidate performs in it is what this lesson is here to close.

::: key
Sleep the two nights before, not just the night before. Eat something that does not spike and crash. Carry water. Ask for a five-minute break between rounds if you need one — it is normal and nobody marks you down for it.
:::

## Sleep the two nights before

The rule is to sleep well on *both* of the two nights before the onsite. That is not general good advice. There is a specific reason for it.

Think about the night right before a big day — a test, a big game, a flight somewhere new. That is the night you have the least control over. You run interview rounds in your head at one in the morning. You wake at four and check the time. You check the six o'clock alarm three times. If your whole sleep plan depends on that night, it depends on the one night most likely to go wrong.

The night before *that* one is different. You can control it, and it is far enough ahead that your mind is not racing yet. So build your plan around it. Go to sleep early that night. It decides how much you have in reserve if the last night goes badly. If both nights go well, you have lost nothing.

::: note Why two nights beats one
Think of the two nights as two backup systems. Plan on only the last night, and a bad last night leaves you with nothing. Plan on both, and a bad last night is covered by the good one before it. Engineers call this **redundancy**: never let the outcome hang on the single part most likely to fail.
:::

The rest of your preparation follows the same logic: take decisions out of a morning that will already have plenty of them.

- **Travel the day before** if there is travel, so a delayed flight is an annoyance rather than a disaster.
- **Know where the building is** and how long the trip takes before the morning you have to make it.
- **Lay out what you are taking** the night before.

## Food and water

Eat something that does not spike and crash. That is the flashcard's wording, and it is exactly precise enough. A big lunch of **[[fast carbohydrates|spike-and-crash]]** — white bread, sweets, sugary drinks, foods your body turns into sugar quickly — is often followed by a slump. On an onsite schedule, that slump lands right on the early-afternoon rounds.

The right choice is whatever you normally eat that leaves you steady. The onsite is not the day to try something new, in either direction: no unfamiliar big meal, and no skipping the breakfast you always eat.

Carry water. It is the cheapest item in this lesson and the one people skip most. Between rounds you get **[[handed from room to room|handoff]]**, with a few minutes in a hallway. Nothing promises that any of those gaps includes a chance to find a drink. Talking for eight hours is thirsty work. A dry throat in the middle of an explanation is a distraction you do not need to set up for yourself.

### Caffeine

The honest advice is that people differ so much here that one general rule would do more harm than good. Two things are safe to say.

First, do not change your habit on the day. A candidate who does not usually drink coffee and has two cups before her presentation has added a variable she has no data on.

Second, **[[caffeine lasts|caffeine-tail]]** longer than it feels like it does. For many people, caffeine taken in the afternoon is still working in the evening. So a coffee taken to rescue the 15:45 round (quarter to four in the afternoon) can end up affecting your sleep the night after the onsite.

The day-simulation exercise in this module is where you find out your own response — on a day when learning it costs nothing.

## Asking for a break

Between rounds, if you need five minutes, ask for them. The module says plainly that this is normal and nobody marks you down for it. There is a reason behind that, and understanding it takes the worry away.

Your interviewer has to write an assessment they will stand behind at the debrief. That report is more useful to everyone — including the interviewer — if it shows what you can actually do. An interviewer gains nothing from grading a worn-out version of you. A report bent out of shape because you had no water since nine in the morning is worse for them than a five-minute delay.

How to ask is simple. Have the sentence ready in advance, so you are not making it up while tired:

> "Could I take five minutes before we start?"

That is enough. It needs no reason, no apology and no explanation. If you want to say more, "I'd like to grab some water and use the restroom — could I have five minutes?" is equally fine.

What does *not* work is what many candidates do instead: say nothing, and spend the next forty-five minutes below their best to avoid a twenty-second conversation.

::: warning The break you did not ask for costs more than the break you did
Five minutes is about one-ninth of a forty-five-minute round. But a round you spend distracted, thirsty or unable to focus is not one-ninth worse — it becomes a weak report in the debrief. The **[[trade is lopsided|lopsided-trade]]**, and it leans entirely one way. If you are wondering whether you need a break, you already have your answer.
:::

## Simulate the day before you live it

The module's third exercise asks you to block out a day and run the rounds back to back:

- a technical presentation with questions,
- two coding sessions,
- a systems discussion,
- a domain round,
- a behavioral round,

with only short breaks. Then you record *where* your performance dropped, and *by what hour*.

That last instruction is the whole point. You do not need general knowledge that long days are tiring. You need specific knowledge of how *you* wear down, and that differs from person to person. Some people lose precision first. Some lose speed. Some can still solve the problem but can no longer organize a spoken answer. Knowing which one you are, and roughly when it starts, turns generic advice into a plan aimed at your own **[[failure mode|failure-mode]]** — the particular way something breaks.

The simulation is also the only honest way to find out whether your logistics plan works. A plan that has never been tested is a **[[hypothesis|test-like-you-fly]]** — a guess that has not been checked yet.

::: example What a simulation actually reveals
A candidate blocks out a day two weeks before her onsite. She runs six rounds against a timer: a forty-five-minute presentation, with questions from a friend who has agreed to be difficult; two forty-five-minute coding sessions; a systems discussion; a domain round; and a behavioral round. She leaves ten- to fifteen-minute gaps and a half hour for lunch.

Starting at 09:00, her schedule runs:

| Block | Time |
| --- | --- |
| Presentation | 09:00–09:45 |
| Coding 1 | 09:55–10:40 |
| Coding 2 | 10:50–11:35 |
| Lunch | 11:35–12:05 |
| Systems | 12:05–12:50 |
| Domain | 13:00–13:45 |
| Behavioral | 14:00–14:45 |

Count the work: six blocks of 45 minutes is $6 \times 45 = 270$ minutes, which is four and a half hours. The whole day runs from 09:00 to 14:45, which is five hours and forty-five minutes. Sanity check: the gaps are 10 + 10 + 30 + 10 + 15 = 75 minutes, and 270 + 75 = 345 minutes = 5 h 45 min. It adds up.

That is shorter than the real onsite — and that turns out to be the most useful thing about it.

Her notes afterward:

- The presentation was strong.
- The first coding session was fine.
- The second was noticeably slower to start. She caught herself writing code before she had said her approach out loud — the exact habit she had spent three weeks building.
- The systems discussion after lunch was her worst block. She was flat and gave short answers.
- The domain round recovered somewhat.
- The behavioral round at 14:00 was poor in a specific way. She knew all her stories, but could not shorten them, so each one ran well past two minutes.

Three findings, all of which she can act on.

1. Her weak point is not knowledge. It is the discipline that sits on top of knowledge — stating the approach before coding, keeping a story short. It first slipped about two hours in, and it was worst in the afternoon.
2. The block right after lunch was her worst, which points at food.
3. All of this happened inside five and three-quarter hours. The real day is eight or more. So what she has measured is the *optimistic* version.

Her plan changes: a lighter lunch, water carried, a deliberate five-minute break before the first afternoon round, and two more weeks drilling the two habits that failed — not the material that held up.
:::

::: example Two candidates at 15:30
Two candidates reach the second-to-last round of a real onsite at 15:30 (half past three). Both have been there since nine. Both have one round after this one.

The first had a coffee in the morning, and half a sandwich at lunch because she spent lunch talking with two engineers. She has had no water since the morning. She has not asked for a break all day: each time the thought came up, the next interviewer was already walking her to the room, and it felt strange to raise it. She is not in trouble. She is tired, a little dehydrated, and noticeably slower than at ten in the morning.

In the systems round at 15:30 she gives a reasonable answer that never develops, and she misses the trade-off framing she had practiced. In the behavioral round at 16:15 she tells a good story badly. She spends ninety seconds on the situation alone — the exact mistake she had drilled against.

Neither round is a disaster. Both are weaker reports than she could have produced, and both count.

The second candidate, at the 15:20 handoff, says: "Could I take five minutes before we start? I'd like some water." The interviewer says of course and points her to the kitchen. She drinks a full glass, uses the restroom, and stands outside for two minutes without looking at her phone. She walks into the systems round at 15:27, and it goes well. Before the last round there is a natural gap, so she does the same thing without needing to ask. Her behavioral round is as sharp as her morning presentation.

What separates these two days? About ten minutes, and one sentence said out loud.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
Give the structural argument for why stamina planning changes the outcome, not only how comfortable you feel.
:::

::: answer
The onsite is five to seven interviews in a row over eight or more hours. The final rounds are scored exactly like the first ones: each produces its own report from its own interviewer, and all the reports reach the same debrief. Most candidates perform measurably worse by the end. So wearing down costs more than comfort — it costs rounds that are being graded. And the things that prevent it are cheap.
:::

::: check
Why does the advice say the two nights before, rather than the night before?
:::

::: answer
The night right before a high-stakes day is the one you can least control — the night you lie awake rehearsing. If that is your whole sleep plan, the plan rests on the night most likely to fail. The night before that is fully in your control and far enough ahead that your mind is not racing, so it decides what you have in reserve if the last night goes badly.
:::

::: check
A candidate feels she could use five minutes, but does not want to look unable to handle the day. Give the argument that settles this, and the sentence she should use.
:::

::: answer
The module states directly that asking is normal and nobody marks you down for it. The reason is that the interviewer's report is more useful when it shows what she can really do; an assessment skewed by thirst is worse for the interviewer than a five-minute delay. The trade is also lopsided: five minutes is about one-ninth of a forty-five-minute round, while a round spent worn out becomes a weak report. The sentence is "Could I take five minutes before we start?" — no reason and no apology needed.
:::

::: check
Why record where your performance dropped and by what hour, rather than concluding that long days are tiring?
:::

::: answer
Because the way people wear down is individual, and the general conclusion gives you nothing to act on. Some candidates lose precision first, some lose speed, some can still solve a problem but can no longer organize a spoken answer. Knowing which one you are, and roughly when it starts, aims your logistics plan at your actual weak point. It is also the only way to test whether the plan works at all — an untested plan is only a hypothesis.
:::

::: check
Why is the day of the onsite the wrong day to change your caffeine or food habits, in either direction?
:::

::: answer
Either change adds a variable you have no data on, on the one day when being wrong costs the most. A candidate who does not usually drink coffee and has two before a presentation has added an unknown. So has one who skips her usual breakfast. The place to learn your own response is the day-simulation exercise, where the lesson is free. On the day itself, the goal is to be the version of yourself you have already measured.
:::

## Summary

| Item | What to do | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sleep | Sleep well both of the two nights before | The last night is the one you cannot control |
| Travel | Travel the day before, if there is travel | A delay becomes an annoyance, not a disaster |
| Food | Something that does not spike and crash; nothing new | The slump lands on the early-afternoon rounds |
| Water | Carry it | Handoffs do not promise a chance to find any |
| Caffeine | Do not change your habit; know your own response | People differ a lot; afternoon doses last into the night |
| Breaks | Ask for five minutes; no reason needed | Normal, not marked down, and a lopsided trade in your favor |
| Rehearsal | Run the rounds back to back; note when you drop off | How you wear down is individual, and the plan needs testing |

The next lesson turns to one thing you control in every single round, however it has gone: the question you ask when the interviewer asks whether you have any.

::: context onsite-day Why it is called an onsite
The word means "at the site" — at the company's own building, rather than over the phone or a video call. Earlier stages are short calls from home. The onsite is the long day in person, and sometimes it includes a tour of the factory or test stands. Some companies now run a "virtual onsite" over video with the same structure: the name stuck even when the building did not.
:::

::: context interview-report What an interviewer writes down
After each round, the interviewer writes up what they saw: what questions they asked, how you answered, and whether they would hire you. Many companies call this a **scorecard** or a **feedback form**. Because each interviewer writes their own, a tired round at 16:00 leaves a separate, permanent record — it does not get averaged away by a strong round at 10:00. Lesson twelve follows these reports into the debrief, where they are weighed together.
:::

::: context spike-and-crash What "spike and crash" means
Food turns into sugar in your blood, which your brain and muscles use for energy. Fast carbohydrates turn into sugar quickly, so blood sugar can jump up and then fall back, and many people feel sleepy or foggy on the way down. Slower food — with protein, fat or fiber — releases energy more evenly. The picture is a sketch of the idea, not measured data.

::: context handoff How the day moves you around
On an onsite you usually do not stay in one room. One interviewer finishes, then walks you to the next room or hands you to the next person — a **handoff**, like passing the baton in a relay race. These few minutes feel like dead time, but they are the only natural gaps in the day. That is why water in your bag matters: the hallway rarely has a fountain exactly when you need one.
:::

::: context caffeine-tail How long caffeine hangs around
Your body clears caffeine slowly. A common rough figure for adults is a **half-life** of about five hours: after five hours, about half is still in you; after ten, about a quarter. People vary a great deal, from a few hours to much longer.

::: context lopsided-trade Weighing the two costs
Put numbers on it. The break costs 5 of 45 minutes: $5/45 \approx 0.11$, about one-ninth. Skipping it risks the whole round's report being weak — a cost that is not measured in minutes at all, because the report is what the debrief reads. Engineers make this kind of call all the time: a small, certain cost now to avoid a large, likely one later. Paying for a spare part is cheaper than losing the mission.
:::

::: context failure-mode An engineer's word for how things break
A **failure mode** is one specific way something can fail. A bolt can snap, loosen or corrode — three failure modes, each with a different fix. Engineers list them on purpose in reviews, because you cannot guard against a failure you never named. Applying the same word to yourself is useful: "I get tired" is not a failure mode, but "I stop stating my approach before coding after about two hours" is one you can fix.
:::

::: context test-like-you-fly "Test like you fly"
Spacecraft engineers have a saying: **test like you fly**. It means testing hardware in conditions as close to the real mission as possible, because a test under easier conditions can hide the problem that shows up for real. A plan you have never tried is still a **hypothesis** — from the Greek for a "supposition", an idea put forward to be tested. Your day simulation is the test flight for your interview plan, and it is kinder than the real day because it is shorter.
:::
