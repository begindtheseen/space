Imagine you are thinking about joining a new sports team, and the coach says, "Any questions for me?" If you ask, "When is practice?", the coach can tell you right away. If you ask, "What plays does the team struggle with?", the coach can tell you that too — and now the coach knows you are thinking like a player. But if you ask the coach what the team captain eats for breakfast, you get a shrug and you have wasted your turn.

Nearly every conversation in a hiring process ends the same way: the person across from you asks whether you have any questions. Many candidates treat this as the moment the interview ends. It is not. It is part of the interview, and it has two jobs. The second one is easy to miss.

The first job is plain: you need information. You are deciding whether to spend five to eight weeks on this process and, in the end, whether to take the job. Much of what you need to know is not written down anywhere. It is in the head of the person in front of you.

The second job is that **your question is itself evidence**. A question shows what you understand about the work, because you cannot ask a specific question about something you do not understand. That is not a reason to show off — a question asked to display knowledge is easy to spot and lands badly. It is a reason to prepare your questions as seriously as your answers.

This lesson gives you the rule for deciding what to ask whom, the actual questions for each stage, and the kinds of question to hold back, and why.

## The principle: two filters

Before you ask a question, run it through **[[two filters|two-filters]]**, in this order.

**Filter 1 — Can this specific person answer it from their own experience?** A recruiter can tell you which team a **[[requisition|requisition-word]]** (the official job opening) sits in, and what the interview sequence looks like. A recruiter usually cannot tell you the hardest technical problem on that team this quarter. An engineer can tell you the second, and often not the first. Asking someone about something outside their job gets you a vague answer, and it spends one of the few turns you had.

**Filter 2 — Will the answer change something for me?** If the answer, whichever way it goes, changes nothing you will do, you asked the question to fill the silence. The strongest questions are ones where you can say in advance what you would do differently depending on the answer. There are more of those than candidates expect.

## At the recruiter screen

The **recruiter** is the company employee who runs your hiring process — scheduling, answering process questions, passing along results. Ask the recruiter four questions. Each one gives you something you can act on right away.

**1. Which program and team does this requisition sit in?** This one has the biggest effect later on. It tells you which kind of role your application will actually be judged against, and which technical areas you most need to be strong in. Put it together with the second module of this track, and it tells you what the work actually is. Sometimes it also corrects a wrong guess you made from the job posting alone.

**2. What does the interview sequence look like from here, and what timeline should I expect?** The first lesson of this module made the case for this. Five to eight weeks is a general range. The recruiter can tell you the version that applies to *your* opening. The sequence matters as much as the timeline. Knowing there is a presentation, or a take-home, or three coding rounds turns your preparation weeks from a worry into a plan for your hours.

**3. Who will be on the panel?** The **panel** is the set of people who will interview you. Ask by role if not by name. Who is on the panel tells you what the day will test — the same information, seen from another direction.

**4. What is the team working on at the moment?** This gives you something to prepare against and something to think about. You will use the answer in every later round.

::: key
Ask the recruiter which program and team the requisition sits in, the stage sequence and timeline, who will be on the panel, and what the team is currently working on. Save detailed compensation and benefits questions for later unless the recruiter raises them first.
:::

## At the technical phone screen and in the onsite rounds

From the phone screen onward, the person across from you is an engineer or the **hiring manager** — the person you would work for. A different set of questions opens up.

**What have you shipped in the last six months?** This is the most useful single question on the list, and the reason is the time window. "What do you work on?" invites a summary of what the team is *supposed* to do. "What have you **[[shipped|shipped-word]]** in the last six months?" — meaning finished and delivered for real use — invites an account of real work with real results. That is much harder to answer in vague terms, and much more informative when answered.

**What is the hardest technical problem the team is working on right now?** The answer tells you what the team is actually up against. That is the best evidence you can get about what your first year would contain.

**How do code review and flight readiness work here?** This one does more than it seems to. In **[[code review|code-review]]**, other engineers read your code and must approve it before it is merged. **[[Flight readiness|flight-readiness]]** is the process that decides the software and hardware are fit to fly. The tooling module of this track established that GNC engineers (guidance, navigation and control — said "G-N-C") write production flight code themselves, rather than handing prototypes to someone else. How code gets from one engineer's working copy onto a vehicle is the everyday texture of that job. It is also a question only someone who understands the work would think to ask.

**What makes someone unsuccessful in this role?** Ask it that way — not "what makes someone successful?", which gets you a list of virtues. The failure version gets you something concrete, because people have usually watched it happen and can describe it. It is also the single most useful answer for your own decision, because it tells you what the job punishes.

::: key
Ask an engineer interviewer what they shipped in the last six months, what the hardest current technical problem on the team is, how code review and flight readiness work, and what makes someone unsuccessful in this role. Specific questions prove you understand the work.
:::

Two practical points.

- **Phone screen: pick one or two.** In a thirty-minute phone screen there is room for one or two of these, not four. Choose the ones whose answers you most need.
- **Onsite: prepare more, and vary them.** Across a full onsite day you will be offered this moment five to seven times. Prepare more questions than you expect to use, and do not ask everyone the same one. Several people answering one question is a worse use of the day than several people answering different ones.

## What not to ask yet

Some questions are fine later and costly now. Some are costly at any stage.

**Detailed compensation and benefits — not yet.** **Compensation** means pay: salary plus things like stock. **Benefits** are health insurance, vacation and the like. Hold these questions unless the recruiter brings up the subject first. If the recruiter does, answering well is a different skill, and the next module in this track teaches it directly. The reason to wait is not shyness about money. You are entitled to know what a job pays, and you will need to know before you accept. The reason is that at the recruiter screen nothing is on the table yet. No **[[level|job-level]]** has been set. A detailed question about the package spends limited time on the terms of a job that does not exist yet, and it signals that the package is what you came for.

**Asking for a stated qualification to be set aside — never.** Asking whether the degree requirement or the experience requirement can be waived for you signals that you read the posting as a suggestion. The recruiter did not write the requirements and usually cannot change them.

**Anything already answered in the posting — never.** It turns your turn into proof that you did not read carefully.

**Accusations dressed up as questions — never.** "How many people has this team lost recently?" is not really a question about people leaving, and it will not be heard as one. If what you want to know is about stability or pace, there are direct versions that get a real answer instead of a defensive one: what makes someone unsuccessful here, what a heavy week looks like, and how often those weeks come.

::: warning A question asked to show off is visible as one
"I noticed your team published on **[[convex guidance|convex-guidance]]** — how are you handling the discretization error when the thrust bounds are tight?" is a good question *if* you really want the answer and can follow up on it. If you built it to prove you had read something, the follow-up is where it falls apart. You have no second question and no reaction to the answer beyond nodding. Ask what you actually want to know. It is more interesting, and it holds up when the conversation continues.
:::

::: example Four questions, three of them sent to the wrong person
A candidate prepares four questions and asks all four of the recruiter on the first call. Run each one through Filter 1 — can this person answer it?

1. **"What is the hardest technical problem the team is facing?"** The recruiter gives a general answer about the program's goals, because that is what a recruiter can give. The question needed an engineer. *Filter 1: fails.*
2. **"How does code review work?"** Same result: a reasonable, general answer that tells her nothing specific. A question that would have been excellent in the phone screen is used up. *Filter 1: fails.*
3. **"What is the equity part of the package?"** The recruiter says compensation will be discussed later in the process. The exchange cost her a turn, and told the recruiter what she is focused on before anything is known about her. *Wrong timing.*
4. **"What does the interview sequence look like?"** This one works. She learns there is a presentation and two coding rounds, which changes her next three weeks. *Both filters: pass.*

Count the result: four good questions, but only one of the four ($1/4$) reached a person who could answer it well at that moment. She left the call knowing one useful thing when she could have known four. And she now has to ask two of her best questions again later, in a process where turns are not free.
:::

::: example One answer that changed a candidate's preparation and her decision
In an onsite round, a candidate asks an engineer what makes someone unsuccessful in this role.

He thinks for a moment and gives a concrete answer. The people who struggle, he says, build something correct in simulation and then find it does not survive the real vehicle's timing. They treated the **[[real-time constraints|timing-budget]]** — the hard deadlines the flight computer must meet — as a detail to handle later, rather than as part of the design. He gives an example, without details he should not share, of a method that worked beautifully on a desktop computer and could not be made to finish inside the control loop's time budget.

Ten minutes earlier, she had none of the following. Now she has three things.

1. **What this team's work punishes.** That is more useful than any list of good qualities.
2. **A thread for her remaining rounds.** She can raise the subject from genuine interest. Her systems round that afternoon opens with her asking how the timing budget is split across the loop — a much better start than a generic question.
3. **Real information for her own decision.** This is a team where embedded and real-time material matters every day. That tells her what her first year would feel like, and lets her check it honestly against what she wants.

One question, asked of the right person, with an answer that changed what she did in the next hour and what she would weigh if an offer came.
:::

::: answer
First: can this specific person answer it from their own experience? Second: will the answer change something I do? Ignoring the first gets you a vague, general answer — a recruiter asked about the team's hardest technical problem will describe the program's goals. It also spends one of a small number of turns on a question you will have to ask again later, of someone who can actually answer it.
:::

List the four questions for the recruiter screen, and say which has the largest effect on what you do next.
:::

::: answer
Which program and team the requisition sits in; what the interview sequence and timeline look like from here; who will be on the panel; and what the team is currently working on. Detailed compensation and benefits questions are held back unless the recruiter raises them first. The sequence question has the largest *immediate* effect. Knowing whether there is a presentation, a take-home or three coding rounds turns your preparation weeks from general worry into a specific plan for your hours.
:::

::: check
Why is "what makes someone unsuccessful in this role" a better question than "what makes someone successful"?
:::

::: answer
The success version gets a list of virtues — thorough, curious, a good communicator — that fits every job and tells you nothing. The failure version gets something concrete, because people have usually watched it happen and can describe the actual case. It also answers what you most need for your own decision: what this job actually punishes, which is what your first year would be shaped by.
:::

::: check
Why hold detailed compensation questions at the recruiter screen, given that you are entitled to know what the job pays?
:::

::: answer
You are entitled to know, and the timing is still wrong. At that point nothing is on the table and no level has been set. The question spends limited time on the terms of a job that does not exist yet, and it signals that the package is why you are there. The exception is when the recruiter brings it up first. That happens, and it is a separate skill — answering the compensation question without anchoring yourself low is taught in the next module of this track.
:::

::: check
A candidate has one question she thinks is excellent, and plans to ask it in all six of her onsite rounds. What is wrong with that plan?
:::

::: answer
It wastes five of the six turns. Six people answering the same question give her one piece of information, repeated. Six people answering six different questions give her six pieces, from six viewpoints inside the same organization. The right preparation is more questions than she expects to use, chosen round by round for the person in front of her — what an engineer shipped recently, how code review and flight readiness work, what the hardest current problem is, what makes someone unsuccessful.
:::

## Summary

| Ask this person | These questions |
| --- | --- |
| Recruiter | Program and team; stage sequence and timeline; who is on the panel; what the team is working on |
| Engineer or hiring manager | What they shipped in the last six months; the hardest current technical problem; how code review and flight readiness work; what makes someone unsuccessful |
| Behavioral round | What a heavy week looks like on this team, and how often those weeks come |
| Nobody, yet | Detailed compensation and benefits, unless raised first |
| Nobody, ever | Waiving a stated qualification; anything answered in the posting; accusations phrased as questions |

| Filter | Test |
| --- | --- |
| Sent to the right person | Can this person answer it from their own experience? |
| Worth a turn | Will the answer change something I do? |

::: context requisition-word What a "req" is
A **requisition**, often shortened to "req" (said "rec"), is the company's internal record that says "we are approved to hire one person for this job, on this team." The job posting you read is the public face of a requisition. Each req has a team, a manager and a budget behind it, which is why asking which program and team yours sits in tells you so much.
:::

::: context shipped-word Where "shipped" comes from
In software, to **ship** means to finish something and deliver it for real use — once literally, when programs went out on disks in boxes. The word stuck after the boxes disappeared. In spaceflight, "shipped" can mean code that flew on a vehicle, or a tool other engineers now depend on. Either way it means *done and in use*, not "in progress" — which is why asking what someone shipped gets a sharper answer than asking what they work on.
:::

::: context code-review What code review is
In **code review**, before your change joins the main copy of the software, other engineers read it line by line, ask questions and request fixes. Only after they approve does it merge. It catches mistakes early and spreads knowledge across the team. For flight software, where a bug can end a mission, review is taken especially seriously — so how a team does it says a lot about how the work feels day to day.
:::

::: context job-level Why "level" comes first
Companies sort engineers into **levels** — roughly, how senior the job is. Pay is attached to the level through a **pay band**, a range from lowest to highest. The level is usually decided from how you do in the interviews. So early on, nobody can tell you your pay precisely, because nobody knows your level yet. The third module of this track covers levels in detail.
:::

::: context convex-guidance What that show-off question is about
**Convex guidance** is a way to compute a rocket's path — for example, a landing burn — by turning it into a kind of math problem a computer can solve quickly and reliably. **Discretization** means chopping smooth time into small steps so a computer can handle it, which introduces a small error. You will meet both properly in the convex guidance module of the course. The point here: a question like that is only good if you can follow the answer.
:::

::: context timing-budget A deadline that repeats forever
A flight computer runs its control loop over and over at a fixed rate. At 50 times a second, each cycle has $1/50 = 0.02$ seconds, or 20 milliseconds. Everything — read sensors, estimate, compute, command — must finish inside that window, every time. A method that gives the perfect answer in 30 milliseconds is useless in a 20-millisecond loop.
