Nearly every conversation in this process ends the same way: the person you are speaking to asks whether you have any questions for them. Candidates treat this as the moment the interview ends. It is not — it is a part of the interview with two jobs, and the second one is easy to miss.

The first job is obvious: you need information. You are deciding whether to spend five to eight weeks on this process and, eventually, whether to take a job. Much of what you need to know is not written down anywhere and is sitting in the head of the person in front of you.

The second job is that the question itself is evidence. A question reveals what you understand about the work, because you cannot ask a specific question about something you do not understand. That is not a reason to perform — a question asked to display knowledge is transparent and lands badly — but it is a reason to take the preparation of questions as seriously as the preparation of answers.

This lesson gives the principle for deciding what to ask whom, the concrete questions for each stage, and the category of question to hold back and why.

## The principle

Two filters, applied in order.

**Can this specific person answer it from their own experience?** A recruiter can tell you which team a requisition sits in and what the interview sequence looks like; they generally cannot tell you what the hardest technical problem on that team is this quarter. An engineer can tell you the second and often not the first. Asking someone a question outside what they do produces a vague answer and spends a slot you had.

**Will the answer change something for me?** A question whose answer, either way, changes nothing you will do is a question you asked to fill the silence. The strongest questions are the ones where you can say in advance what you would do differently depending on the answer — and there are more of those available than candidates expect.

## At the recruiter screen

Four questions, and each of them returns something you can act on immediately.

**Which program and team does this requisition sit in?** This is the one with the largest downstream effect. It tells you which role family your case will actually be read against, which of the technical areas you should be strongest in, and — combined with the second module of this track — what the work is. It also occasionally corrects a wrong assumption you made from the posting alone.

**What does the interview sequence look like from here, and what timeline should I have in mind?** The first lesson of this module made the case for this at length: the five-to-eight-week window is a general range, and the version of it that applies to your requisition is something the recruiter can simply tell you. The sequence matters just as much as the timeline, because knowing there is a presentation, or a take-home, or three coding rounds, is what turns your preparation weeks into an allocation rather than a worry.

**Who will be on the panel?** By role if not by name. The panel composition tells you what the day will examine, which is the same information from another direction.

**What is the team working on at the moment?** This is the question that gives you something to prepare against and something to think about, and it is the one whose answer you will use in every later round.

::: key
Ask the recruiter which program and team the requisition sits in, the stage sequence and timeline, who will be on the panel, and what the team is currently working on. Save detailed compensation and benefits questions for later unless the recruiter raises them first.
:::

## At the technical phone screen and in the onsite rounds

From the phone screen onward, the person opposite you is an engineer or the hiring manager, and a different set of questions becomes available.

**What have you shipped in the last six months?** This is the most useful single question in the list, and the reason is the specificity of the window. "What do you work on" invites a summary of the team's charter. "What have you shipped in the last six months" invites an account of actual work with actual outcomes, which is far harder to answer abstractly and far more informative when answered.

**What is the hardest technical problem the team is working on right now?** The answer tells you what the team is actually up against, which is the best available evidence about what your first year would contain.

**How do code review and flight readiness work here?** This one is doing more than it looks like. The curriculum's tooling module established that GNC engineers write production flight code themselves rather than handing prototypes over, and how code gets from an engineer's branch to a vehicle is the daily texture of that job. It is also a question that only someone who understands the work would think to ask.

**What makes someone unsuccessful in this role?** Phrased that way — not "what makes someone successful", which produces a list of virtues. The failure version produces something concrete, because people have usually seen it happen and can describe it. It is also the single most useful answer you can get for your own decision, since it tells you what the job punishes.

::: key
Ask an engineer interviewer what they shipped in the last six months, what the hardest current technical problem on the team is, how code review and flight readiness work, and what makes someone unsuccessful in this role. Specific questions prove you understand the work.
:::

Two practical notes. In a thirty-minute phone screen you have room for one or two of these, not four — pick the ones whose answers you most need. Across a full onsite day you will be offered this slot five to seven times, so prepare more questions than you expect to use, and do not ask the same one of everybody: several people answering the same question is a worse use of the day than several people answering different ones.

## What not to ask yet

**Detailed compensation and benefits.** Hold these unless the recruiter raises the subject first, in which case answering is a different skill and the next module in this track teaches it directly. The reason to hold them is not squeamishness about money — you are entitled to know what a job pays, and you will need to know before you accept. It is that at the recruiter screen nothing is on the table yet, no level has been set, and a detailed question about the package spends limited time on the terms of a job that does not exist while signalling that the package is what you came for.

**Anything asking for a stated qualification to be set aside.** Asking whether the degree line or the experience line can be waived for you signals that the posting was read as a suggestion. The recruiter did not write the requisition and generally cannot rewrite it.

**Anything already answered in the posting.** It converts your slot into a demonstration that you did not read carefully.

**Questions that are accusations in disguise.** "How many people has this team lost recently?" is not a question about attrition, and it will not be received as one. If what you actually want to know is about stability or pace, there are direct versions — what makes someone unsuccessful here, what a heavy week looks like and how often those weeks come — that get you a real answer instead of a defensive one.

::: warning A question asked to show off is visible as one
"I noticed your team published on convex guidance — how are you handling the discretisation error when the thrust bounds are tight?" is a good question if you genuinely want the answer and can follow it up. If you constructed it to prove you had read something, the follow-up is where it collapses, because you have no second question and no reaction to the answer beyond nodding. Ask what you actually want to know. It is more interesting, and it survives contact.
:::

::: example Four questions, three of them misaddressed
A candidate prepares four questions and asks all four of the recruiter on the first call.

She asks what the hardest technical problem the team is currently facing is. The recruiter gives a general answer about the program's goals, because that is what a recruiter can give; the question needed an engineer.

She asks how code review works. Same outcome — a reasonable, general answer that tells her nothing specific, and a question burned that would have been excellent in the phone screen.

She asks about the equity component of the package. The recruiter says compensation will be discussed later in the process. The exchange has cost her a slot and has told the recruiter what she is focused on before anything has been established about her.

Her fourth question is about the interview sequence, and it is the one that works. She learns there is a presentation and two coding rounds, which changes her next three weeks.

She had four good questions. One of them was addressed to a person who could answer it. She left the call knowing one thing she could have known four times over, and she now has to re-ask two of her best questions later in a process where slots are not free.
:::

::: example One answer that changed a candidate's preparation and her decision
A candidate asks an engineer in an onsite round what makes someone unsuccessful in this role.

He thinks for a moment and gives a concrete answer: the people who struggle are the ones who build something correct in simulation and then find it does not survive contact with the real vehicle's timing, because they treated the real-time constraints as a detail to be handled later rather than as a property of the design. He gives an example, without specifics he should not give, of a scheme that worked beautifully offline and could not be made to close inside the loop's budget.

She now has three things she did not have ten minutes earlier. She has a clear statement of what this team's work punishes, which is more useful than any list of desirable qualities. She has a subject she can raise in her remaining rounds from a position of genuine interest — the systems round that afternoon opens with her asking how the timing budget is allocated across the loop, which is a much better start than a generic question. And she has real information for her own decision: this is a team where the embedded and real-time material matters daily, which tells her what her first year would feel like and lets her check it honestly against what she wants.

That is one question, asked of the right person, with an answer that changed what she did in the next hour and what she would weigh if an offer came.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
State the two filters for deciding whether to ask a question, and explain what goes wrong when the first one is ignored.
:::

::: answer
First: can this specific person answer it from their own experience? Second: will the answer change something I do? Ignoring the first produces a vague, general answer — a recruiter asked about the team's hardest technical problem will describe the program's goals — and it spends one of a small number of slots on a question that will have to be re-asked later of someone who can actually answer it.
:::

::: check
List the four questions for the recruiter screen, and say which has the largest effect on what you do next.
:::

::: answer
Which program and team the requisition sits in; what the interview sequence and timeline look like from here; who will be on the panel; and what the team is currently working on. Detailed compensation and benefits questions are held back unless the recruiter raises them first. The sequence question has the largest immediate effect, because knowing whether there is a presentation, a take-home or three coding rounds converts the preparation weeks from general anxiety into a specific allocation of hours.
:::

::: check
Why is "what makes someone unsuccessful in this role" a better question than "what makes someone successful"?
:::

::: answer
The success version produces a list of virtues — thorough, curious, a good communicator — which is true of every role and tells you nothing. The failure version produces something concrete, because people have usually watched it happen and can describe the specific case. It also answers the question you most need for your own decision: what this job actually punishes, which is what your first year would be shaped by.
:::

::: check
Why hold detailed compensation questions at the recruiter screen, given that you are entitled to know what the job pays?
:::

::: answer
Because you are, and the timing is still wrong. At that point nothing is on the table, no level has been set, and the question spends limited time on the terms of a job that does not yet exist while signalling that the package is the reason you are there. The exception is when the recruiter raises the subject first, which happens and is a separate skill — answering the compensation question without anchoring yourself low is taught in the next module of this track.
:::

::: check
A candidate has one question she considers excellent and plans to ask it in all six of her onsite rounds. What is wrong with that plan?
:::

::: answer
It wastes five of the six slots. Six people answering the same question gives her one piece of information with some redundancy; six people answering six different questions gives her six, from six vantage points inside the same organisation. The correct preparation is more questions than she expects to use, chosen per round for the person in front of her — what an engineer shipped recently, how code review and flight readiness work, what the hardest current problem is, what makes someone unsuccessful.
:::

## Summary

| Ask this person | These questions |
| --- | --- |
| Recruiter | Program and team; stage sequence and timeline; who is on the panel; what the team is working on |
| Engineer or hiring manager | What they shipped in the last six months; the hardest current technical problem; how code review and flight readiness work; what makes someone unsuccessful |
| Behavioural round | What a heavy week looks like on this team, and how often those weeks come |
| Nobody, yet | Detailed compensation and benefits, unless raised first |
| Nobody, ever | Waiving a stated qualification; anything answered in the posting; accusations phrased as questions |

| Filter | Test |
| --- | --- |
| Addressed correctly | Can this person answer it from their own experience? |
| Worth a slot | Will the answer change something I do? |
