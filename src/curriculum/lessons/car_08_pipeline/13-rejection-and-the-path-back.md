Think about tryouts for a sports team. Getting cut after the first warm-up drill and getting cut after the final practice game are both "I didn't make the team." But they tell you very different things. The first says something basic was missing. The second says you were close, and the gap was somewhere in the game itself. A player who knows *where* she was cut knows what to practice. A player who only knows *that* she was cut does not.

A hiring process with up to nine touchpoints has up to nine places it can end. Meet that without a plan and all you have is a lot of feeling. This lesson is the plan.

Its central claim is narrow, and it is the most useful thing in the module on this subject: **the stage at which the process ended is the most informative piece of evidence you have.** Often it is the only piece. It points at a specific gap with a specific fix. A rejection after the technical phone screen and a rejection after the onsite are not the same event with different amounts of disappointment attached. They say different things about what is missing, and they call for completely different work.

The lesson also has to be honest about the limits of that reasoning. The diagnosis is **noisy** — mixed with random effects that have nothing to do with you — and treating it as clean would send you off to fix the wrong thing.

::: key
After a rejection: ask the recruiter for any feedback, note which stage ended it, and change something material before reapplying. Rejections at the phone screen and at the onsite imply very different gaps and very different remedies.
:::

## Reading the stage

Each stage of the pipeline is a filter. Clearing a filter proves something. So when the process ends at one stage, you learn two things at once: what you *already proved* at the stages before it, and roughly what went missing at this one. Reading the **[[stage|stage-funnel]]** means working out both.

### Before any live conversation

The application did not get past whatever read it first — a person, a piece of **[[software|ats-word]]**, or both. This case tells you little. No reply, or a "no thank you" email, fits three different stories:

1. the resume did not show the **[[basic qualifications|basic-quals]]** well enough for a reader to find them;
2. the case really did not meet them;
3. the opening was filled or closed for reasons that had nothing to do with you.

From outside, you cannot tell those apart. What you *can* do is the one thing that helps in all three stories. Run the honest self-check from the levels module of this track: go clause by clause through the posting's basic qualifications, and put a piece of real evidence — a project, a course, a job — behind anything you rate better than "not met." If that comes back clean, the problem is probably how the resume presents the evidence, and the resume module after it is where to work.

### At the recruiter screen

This stage usually ends for a reason you can name, because it came up on the call. Common ones are **[[export-control eligibility|export-control]]** (the legal rule about who may work on rocket technology), an opening that turned out not to fit you, a basic qualification found to be unmet, or practical matters like location. If you were on the call, you generally know which it was. Each has a different fix, and none of the fixes is "try again with the same application."

### At the technical phone screen

This one is truly diagnostic, and it is worth spelling out what it rules out. Your resume got through. The recruiter screen got through. So the gap is **not** your credentials, **not** how you look on paper, and **not** your eligibility.

What is left is **demonstrated technical fundamentals** — showing, in a conversation with an engineer and with no whiteboard, that you know the core material. For GNC that points at a specific body of work: fluency in control, estimation and **frequency-domain** material (how a system responds to signals of different speeds), plus the habit of delivering it in a clear structure under time pressure. That is a much more solvable problem than "I was rejected." It is the reason this lesson insists that you write down the stage.

### At the take-home

The exercise you handed in did not make the case. The fix lives in the take-home lesson of this module and in the portfolio module: tests that actually prove something works, not tests for show; a README that answers a reader's questions in the order they come up; a layout a stranger can find their way around; and an honest note on what you would do with more time.

### At the onsite

You cleared every earlier filter and reached the most expensive stage in the process. The gap is somewhere inside five to seven rounds — and unlike the phone screen, the stage alone does not say where. This is the case where asking for feedback is most worth doing. It is also where your own notes from the day, written that same evening before they fade, are often the best evidence you will have.

::: example Two rejections, two different weeks of work
Two candidates are rejected in the same month.

**The first was rejected after a technical phone screen.** She writes down the stage, and then what it rules out: her resume cleared, her eligibility cleared, the recruiter call cleared. What she remembers of the screen is this. She was asked how she would judge the **robustness** of a pointing loop (the controller that keeps an instrument aimed) — how much it could be disturbed before it stopped working well. She knew the material. But she spent three minutes building the answer out loud and was steered to the next question before she finished.

Her fix is specific, and it is not "study more control theory." She already has the knowledge. What she lacks is *delivering* it out loud, under time pressure, with no whiteboard. So she spends six weeks on exactly that. She picks twenty questions from the domain material. She answers each one out loud against a timer. Each answer opens with her assumptions and a one-sentence outline before any content. She records each one and listens back. She also asks a friend to interrupt her mid-answer, because being redirected is what actually happened, and she needs to be able to absorb it.

**The second was rejected after an onsite.** The stage tells her much less: she cleared everything, then lost somewhere inside six rounds. That evening, before anything faded, she wrote down each round and how it felt. Reading the notes back, two things stand out. First, under questioning she could not say why one central design decision in her presented project had been made the way it was. Second, her last two rounds of the day were noticeably worse than her first three.

Two fixes follow, and neither is "more study." The first is a portfolio gap, not a knowledge gap: she goes back through her projects and writes down, for every significant design decision, what the alternatives were and why she chose the way she did. The second is a stamina and logistics problem, so she runs the day-simulation exercise to find out at what hour her own performance starts to slip.

**Sanity check.** Same month, same word — "rejected" — yet their next weeks have almost nothing in common. Neither learned anything from being rejected in general. Both learned a great deal from *where* it happened.
:::

## Asking for feedback

Ask the recruiter once, plainly, with no pressure. Something like: "Is there any feedback you can share that would help me prepare for a future application?"

What comes back varies: specifics, a general direction, or a polite no. This course cannot tell you which you will get, and you should not read anything into the reply beyond what it actually says.

Ask once. Whatever comes back, thank them, and do not ask a second time. A candidate who presses is asking someone to reopen a decision, and that is not what the question was for.

Then write your own notes, because they are the part you control:

- which rounds felt weak, and in what specific way;
- which questions you could not answer;
- where in the day your performance started to slide.

This is the same record the day-simulation exercise trained you to produce. It is often more useful than any outside verdict, because it is about exactly what you did.

## The diagnosis is noisy

A rejection is evidence, but it is not clean evidence. Hiring outcomes also depend on things about the opening, not about you. A role can be filled, closed, **[[re-scoped|re-scoped]]** or moved to another team. A strong candidate can be turned down at any stage for reasons that would not happen again next quarter with the very same evidence. From outside, you usually cannot tell those cases apart from a real gap in yours.

The right response to that noise is not to throw the signal away — the stage really does tell you something. It is to give the signal the right weight:

- **One rejection is one observation.** Treat it as a hint, not a verdict.
- **A pattern across several applications** is a much stronger signal.
- **A [[pattern that repeats at the same stage|same-stage-pattern]]** across several applications is the strongest signal you can get, short of someone giving you explicit feedback.

::: warning Do not conclude anything about the field from one outcome
One process ending is a statement about one opening, at one moment, judged by a handful of people. It is not a statement about whether you belong in GNC. This warning exists because the mistake is real and costly. Candidates who read one rejection as a final verdict stop applying and stop building. They quit the one thing that actually changes the outcome: piling up evidence over months.
:::

## Reapplying

Reapplying is normal. But an application is judged on the evidence it shows **now**, so the only question is whether the evidence got better.

That gives a clean test for when to reapply — and the test is not a length of time. It is whether something **[[material|material-word]]** has changed. Material means something a reader has not already seen and turned down:

- a finished project with **verification** behind it (proof that it works, not a claim that it does);
- a programming language you can now write while someone watches, and could not before;
- a job that closed an experience gap the posting named;
- a published paper or a talk.

A rewritten resume that describes the same evidence is not material. Neither is six months passing on its own.

::: key
Reapply when something material has changed — new evidence a reader has not already seen and declined. Elapsed time alone is not a reason, and a better-worded resume describing the same evidence is not new evidence.
:::

Two things to avoid. The resume-and-referrals module of this track covers both at length.

**Do not mass-apply to unrelated openings.** Sending the same application to dozens of jobs that do not match shows a lack of targeting. Readers can see it, and it counts against you. It also wastes your effort: the role-families and levels modules showed why evidence must match one kind of role.

**Do not [[argue with a past decision|relitigate]].** Even an accurate account of why an earlier rejection was wrong spends the reader's attention on a decision that is closed. It pulls their attention away from the new evidence — the only thing that can change the outcome this time.

::: example Two reapplications, seven months apart
Two candidates reapply to a GNC opening seven months after being turned down.

**The first sends a reworked resume.** The bullet points are better written. The layout is cleaner. The projects section now leads with the strongest item. But the evidence underneath is identical: the same three projects, the same experience, the same skills. He adds a short note saying he believes the earlier decision did not fully reflect his fit, and that he has since strengthened his application.

A reader sees material already reviewed and declined, rearranged, plus an invitation to argue about a closed decision. There is nothing new to judge, so the outcome does not change — and would not even if his original application had been strong. The problem is the *absence of new evidence*, not the quality of the old.

**The second candidate did the diagnosis.** Her process had ended at the technical phone screen, so she treated the gap as demonstrated fundamentals. In seven months she did three things:

1. She built and verified an **[[orbit determination|orbit-determination]]** project fitted against real tracking data, with a written account of how she knew the fit was good.
2. She wrote it in C++ with a test suite, because her target role family is embedded software and all her earlier work was in Python.
3. She practiced spoken answers against a timer twice a week.

Her new application leads with the new project and the language change, in two sentences. It says nothing at all about the earlier decision. A reader opening it has something to read that did not exist before.

**Sanity check.** Is her success guaranteed? No — nothing here is. But hers is an application that *can* succeed. The first, however well formatted, structurally could not: it asked the reader to change their mind about evidence already weighed.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
Why is the stage at which a process ended the most useful evidence a rejected candidate has?
:::

::: answer
Because each stage clears a different filter. The stage where it ended tells you what you had already proved, and so what you had not. A rejection after the technical phone screen rules out credentials, how you look on paper, and eligibility. What is left is demonstrated technical fundamentals, delivered out loud under time pressure — a specific problem you can work on. "I was rejected" names nothing and suggests no action. "I was rejected at stage two" names a body of work.
:::

::: check
What does a rejection with no live conversation at all actually tell you, and what should you do with it?
:::

::: answer
Very little on its own. It fits three stories: a resume that did not show the basic qualifications well, a case that really did not meet them, or an opening filled or closed for reasons unrelated to you. You cannot tell those apart from outside. The useful response helps in all three: run the honest self-check against the posting's basic qualifications clause by clause, with real evidence behind anything rated above "not met." If that comes back clean, work on how the resume presents the evidence.
:::

::: check
Give the test for when to reapply, and explain why elapsed time fails as a test.
:::

::: answer
Reapply when something material has changed — new evidence a reader has not already seen and turned down: a finished and verified project, a language you can now write under observation, a job that closed a named experience gap, a paper or a talk. Elapsed time fails because an application is judged on the evidence it shows now. Six months that produced nothing new shows the same case again. A rewritten resume describing the same evidence fails for the same reason.
:::

::: check
Why does arguing with an earlier decision in a reapplication work against you, even when your account of that decision is accurate?
:::

::: answer
Because it spends the reader's attention on something closed and not up for reconsideration, and pulls it away from the new evidence — the only thing that can change the outcome. It also puts the reader in the position of defending or explaining a past call instead of judging your case. Being accurate does not help. A fair complaint about an old decision is still attention taken from the one thing that could work.
:::

::: check
Explain why a single rejection is weak evidence about your candidacy, without concluding that rejections carry no information.
:::

::: answer
Outcomes also depend on things about the opening — it can be filled, closed, re-scoped or moved — and from outside you cannot separate those cases from a real gap. So one rejection is one noisy observation. But it is not zero information: the stage where it happened is real evidence. The right move is to weight it as one observation, and to give much more weight to a pattern — especially one that repeats at the same stage across several applications, which is the strongest signal you can get short of explicit feedback.
:::

::: check
A candidate was rejected after an onsite and has received no feedback. Where should she look first for what went wrong, and why is the stage alone not enough here?
:::

::: answer
She should look first at her own notes from the day, written that evening: which rounds felt weak and how, which questions she could not answer, and when in the day she began to slip. The stage alone is not enough because an onsite rejection only tells her she cleared every earlier filter — resume, eligibility, recruiter screen, phone screen. The gap could be in any of five to seven rounds, or in stamina across the day, and only a round-by-round record can narrow it down.
:::

## Summary

| Ended at | What it rules out | The work that follows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before any conversation | Little; the signal is weak | Self-check against basic qualifications; then the resume |
| Recruiter screen | Usually identifiable from the call itself | Depends on the reason — eligibility, fit, a qualification, logistics |
| Technical phone screen | Credentials, paper, eligibility | Fundamentals, and spoken delivery under time pressure |
| Take-home | Everything up to the exercise | Tests as evidence, a real README, a layout a stranger can follow |
| Onsite | Every earlier filter | Your own notes from the day; feedback if offered; often stamina |
| Any stage | — | Ask once for feedback; record the stage; change something material |

That closes the module, with the whole process in view: a five-to-eight-week pipeline of up to nine touchpoints. It starts with a twenty-to-thirty-minute recruiter screen, where eligibility and fit are settled, and a thirty-minute technical screen with an engineer. Some software and firmware roles add a take-home. Then comes a full day of five to seven back-to-back interviews: a presentation whose topic you do not pick, two to three coding rounds, systems and domain rounds, estimation problems, and a behavioral round answered with STAR. Last comes a debrief that turns five to seven separate reports into one decision. You also know why a moderate difficulty rating fits a demanding process, how to keep the last round as good as the first, what to ask each person, and what to do with either outcome.

What this module has deliberately not done is teach any single round in depth. That is what [[comes next|next-modules]]: the five modules that follow take the screens, the presentation, the domain round, the whiteboard and the behavioral round one at a time, and each goes much further into its stage than a map ever could.

::: context ats-word The software that reads first
Many companies store applications in an **applicant tracking system**, or **ATS** (said "A-T-S"): a database that holds every resume sent to every opening, lets recruiters search and sort them, and tracks each candidate's stage. Some are set up to screen on simple rules, such as a required answer to a yes-or-no question. How much any one company relies on the software versus a person reading is not public, which is one more reason a silent rejection is hard to interpret.
:::

::: context basic-quals Must-haves and nice-to-haves
Most job postings have two lists. **Basic qualifications** (sometimes "minimum" or "required") are the must-haves: a degree type, years of experience, a skill. **Preferred qualifications** are the nice-to-haves that make a candidate stronger. Missing a preferred item is normal. Missing a basic one usually ends the application, which is why the self-check goes through the basic list clause by clause.
:::

::: context export-control Why a legal rule shows up in an interview
Rocket and missile technology is controlled by US export law, mainly **ITAR** (said "eye-tar"), the International Traffic in Arms Regulations. Sharing controlled technical data with someone who is not a "US person" can count as an export. So companies building launch vehicles often confirm on the very first call whether a candidate can legally see the work. The first module of this track covers who counts as a US person and why this gate comes before everything else.
:::

::: context re-scoped When the job itself changes
To **scope** a job is to decide what it covers. A role is **re-scoped** when that changes after it is posted: the team decides it needs a more senior person, or someone with a different specialty, or the budget shrinks. Candidates who fit the old version can be turned down for the new one through no fault of their own — and from outside, it looks exactly like an ordinary rejection.
:::

::: context same-stage-pattern Noise scatters; a gap repeats
Random reasons for rejection — a filled role, a strange hour, a re-scoped job — land at random stages. A real gap lands at the same stage every time, because the same filter keeps catching it.

::: context material-word What makes a change "material"
In business and law, a **material** fact is one big enough that it could change someone's decision. A typo fixed is not material. A new signed contract is. For a reapplication, ask one question: if the reader had known this last time, might they have decided differently? A verified project in the language the role needs passes that test. A new font does not.
:::

::: context relitigate Why arguing a closed case backfires
Lawyers call it **relitigating**: bringing a case back to argue again after it has been decided. Courts mostly refuse to let people do it, because decisions have to end somewhere. Hiring works the same way in practice. The earlier decision was made by other people, on other evidence, and is closed. A reader can only act on what is in front of them now — so every sentence about the old decision is a sentence not spent on the new evidence.
:::

::: context orbit-determination Working out an orbit from measurements
**Orbit determination** means finding a spacecraft's orbit — its path around Earth — from measurements such as radar ranges, telescope angles or GPS positions. Every measurement has errors, so you fit the orbit that best matches all of them together, then check how well the fit really agrees. It is a classic estimation problem, and one of the topics reported for GNC phone screens. The estimation modules later in the course build the tools for it.
:::
