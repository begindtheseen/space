A process with up to nine touchpoints has up to nine places it can end, and a candidate who has not thought about that in advance will meet the outcome with no plan and a great deal of feeling. This lesson is the plan.

Its central claim is narrow and it is the most useful thing in the module on this subject: **the stage at which the process ended is the most informative piece of evidence you have**, often the only piece, and it points at a specific gap with a specific remedy. A rejection after the technical phone screen and a rejection after the onsite are not the same event with different amounts of disappointment attached. They imply different things about what is missing and call for entirely different work.

The lesson also has to be honest about the limits of that inference, because the diagnosis is noisy and treating it as clean would send you off to fix the wrong thing.

::: key
After a rejection: ask the recruiter for any feedback, note which stage ended it, and change something material before reapplying. Rejections at the phone screen and at the onsite imply very different gaps and very different remedies.
:::

## Reading the stage

**Before any live conversation.** The application did not clear whatever read it first. The honest observation about this case is that its information content is low: no response, or a declined application, is consistent with a resume that did not encode the basic qualifications legibly, with a case that genuinely did not clear them, and with the requisition being filled or closed for reasons that have nothing to do with you. You cannot separate those from outside. What you can do is the one thing that helps in all three readings, which is the honest self-assessment from the levels module of this track — clause by clause against the posting's basic qualifications, with an artefact behind anything rated better than "not met" — followed, if that comes back clean, by the resume work from the module after it.

**At the recruiter screen.** This stage ends processes for reasons that are usually identifiable, because they were discussed on the call: export-control eligibility, a requisition that turned out not to fit, a basic qualification established as unmet, or logistics such as location. If you were on the call, you generally know which. The remedies are different for each and none of them is "try again with the same application".

**At the technical phone screen.** This one is genuinely diagnostic, and it is worth stating what it rules out. Your resume cleared whatever read it. The recruiter screen cleared. So the gap is not credentials, not presentation on paper, and not eligibility — it is demonstrated technical fundamentals, in a conversation with an engineer, without a whiteboard. That points at a specific body of work: fluency in control, estimation and frequency-domain material, and the verbal discipline to deliver it in a structured way under time pressure. That is a much more tractable problem than "I was rejected", and it is the reason this lesson insists on recording the stage.

**At the take-home.** The artefact did not carry. The remedy is in the fourth lesson of this module and in the portfolio module: tests that constitute evidence rather than decoration, a README that answers a reader's questions in the order they arise, structure a stranger can navigate, and a stated account of what you would do with more time.

**At the onsite.** You cleared every earlier filter and reached the most expensive stage in the process. The gap is somewhere inside five to seven rounds, and unlike the phone screen, the stage alone does not tell you where. This is the case where asking for feedback is most worth doing, and where your own notes from the day — written the same evening, before they fade — are often the best evidence available to you.

## Asking for feedback

Ask the recruiter, once, plainly, without pressure: whether there is any feedback they can share that would help you prepare for a future application.

What you get back varies. Some recruiters can give specifics, some can give a general direction, and some will decline. This curriculum cannot tell you which you will get, and you should not read anything into the answer beyond what it says. Ask once. Whatever comes back, thank them, and do not ask a second time — a candidate who presses is asking someone to reopen a decision, which is not what the question was for.

Then write your own notes, because they are the part you control. Which rounds felt weak and in what specific way; which questions you could not answer; where in the day your performance started to slide. This is the same record the day-simulation exercise was designed to teach you to produce, and it is considerably more useful than an external verdict would be, because it is specific to what you actually did.

## The diagnosis is noisy

A rejection is evidence, and it is not clean evidence. Hiring outcomes are also determined by things about the requisition rather than about the candidate: a role can be filled, closed, re-scoped or moved, and a strong candidate can be declined at any stage for reasons that would not recur next quarter with the same evidence. From outside, you generally cannot distinguish those cases from a genuine gap in your case.

The right response to that noise is not to dismiss the signal — the stage really is informative — but to weight it correctly. One rejection is one observation. A pattern across several applications is a much stronger signal, and a pattern that repeats at the *same stage* across several applications is the strongest signal available to you short of explicit feedback.

::: warning Do not conclude anything about the field from one outcome
A single process ending is a statement about one requisition, at one moment, evaluated by a handful of people. It is not a statement about whether you belong in GNC. The failure mode this warning exists for is real and expensive: candidates who read one rejection as a verdict stop applying, stop building, and stop the one process — accumulating evidence over months — that would actually change the outcome.
:::

## Reapplying

Reapplying is normal. The thing to understand about it is mechanical: an application is evaluated on the evidence it presents *now*, so the only question that matters is whether the evidence improved. That gives a clean test for when to reapply, and the test is not a length of time. It is whether something material has changed.

Material means something a reader has not already seen and declined: a finished project with verification behind it, a language you can now write under observation and could not before, a professional role that closed a stated experience gap, a publication, a talk. A rewritten resume describing the same evidence is not material. Nor is the passage of six months by itself.

Two things to avoid, both of which the resume-and-referrals module of this track treats at length. **Do not mass-apply across unrelated requisitions** — it signals an absence of targeting, which is visible and counts against you, and it is also a poor use of your own effort given everything the second and third modules of this track taught about matching your evidence to a role family. And **do not argue with a prior decision**. Even an accurate account of why an earlier rejection was wrong spends a reader's attention on a decision that is closed, and directs it away from the new evidence, which is the only thing that can change the outcome this time.

::: example Two rejections, two different weeks of work
Two candidates are rejected in the same month.

The first was rejected after a technical phone screen. She notes the stage, and she notes what it rules out: her resume cleared, her eligibility cleared, the recruiter conversation cleared. What she remembers of the screen is that she was asked about how she would assess the robustness of a pointing loop, that she knew the material, and that she spent three minutes constructing the answer out loud and was redirected before she finished it.

Her remedy is specific and it is not "study more control theory". She has the knowledge; what she does not have is the verbal delivery of it under time pressure with no whiteboard. So she spends six weeks on that: twenty questions from the domain material, each answered out loud against a timer, each opening with assumptions and a one-sentence structure before any content, each recorded and listened back to. She also asks a friend to interrupt her, because being redirected is what happened and she needs to be able to absorb it.

The second was rejected after an onsite. The stage tells her far less: she cleared everything and lost somewhere inside six rounds. That evening — before anything faded — she wrote out each round and how it felt. Reading the notes back, two things stand out: she could not say, under questioning, why one of the central design decisions in her presented project had been made the way it was, and her last two rounds of the day were noticeably worse than her first three.

Two different remedies follow, and neither is more study. The first is to go back through her portfolio projects and write down, for each significant design decision, what the alternatives were and why she chose as she did — which is a portfolio gap, not a knowledge gap. The second is a stamina and logistics problem, and she runs the day-simulation exercise to find out where her own degradation actually starts.

Neither candidate learned anything from being rejected in the abstract. Both learned a great deal from where it happened.
:::

::: example Two reapplications, seven months apart
Two candidates reapply to a GNC requisition seven months after being declined.

The first submits a reworked resume. The bullets are better written, the layout is cleaner, and the projects section is reordered to lead with the strongest item. The underlying evidence is identical: the same three projects, the same experience, the same skills. He includes a short note saying he believes the earlier decision did not fully reflect his fit, and that he has since strengthened his application.

A reader opening this sees material already reviewed and declined, presented again with a different arrangement, accompanied by an invitation to relitigate a closed decision. There is nothing new to evaluate. The outcome does not change, and it would not have changed even if the original application had been strong, because the problem is the absence of new evidence rather than the quality of the old.

The second candidate did the diagnosis. Her process had ended at the technical phone screen, so she treated the gap as demonstrated fundamentals. In seven months she built and verified an orbit determination project fitted against real data, with a documented account of how she knew the fit was good; she wrote it in C++ with a test suite, because her target role family is embedded and her previous work was all in Python; and she drilled verbal answers against a timer twice a week.

Her reapplication leads with the new project and the language change, in two sentences, and says nothing at all about the earlier decision. A reader opening it has something to read that did not exist before. Whether it succeeds is not guaranteed — nothing here is — but it is an application that can succeed, which the first one structurally could not.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
Why is the stage at which a process ended the most useful evidence a rejected candidate has?
:::

::: answer
Because each stage clears a different filter, so the stage identifies what was already cleared and therefore what was not. A rejection after the technical phone screen rules out credentials, paper presentation and eligibility, leaving demonstrated technical fundamentals delivered verbally under time pressure — a specific, tractable problem. "I was rejected" identifies nothing and suggests no action; "I was rejected at stage two" identifies a body of work.
:::

::: check
What does a rejection with no live conversation at all actually tell you, and what should you do with it?
:::

::: answer
Very little on its own: it is consistent with a resume that did not encode the basic qualifications legibly, with a case that genuinely did not clear them, and with the requisition being filled or closed for reasons unrelated to you — and those cannot be separated from outside. The useful response helps in all three readings: run the honest self-assessment against the posting's basic qualifications clause by clause, with an artefact behind anything rated above "not met", and if that comes back clean, work on how the resume encodes the evidence.
:::

::: check
Give the test for when to reapply, and explain why elapsed time fails as a test.
:::

::: answer
Reapply when something material has changed — new evidence a reader has not already seen and declined: a finished and verified project, a language you can now write under observation, a role that closed a stated experience gap, a publication, a talk. Elapsed time fails because an application is evaluated on the evidence it presents now, and six months that produced nothing new presents the same case again. A rewritten resume describing the same evidence fails for the same reason.
:::

::: check
Why does relitigating an earlier decision in a reapplication work against you even when your account of that decision is accurate?
:::

::: answer
Because it spends the reader's attention on something closed and not open for reconsideration, and directs it away from the new evidence, which is the only thing that can change the outcome. It also puts the reader in the position of defending or explaining a previous call rather than evaluating your case. Accuracy does not help: a fair complaint about an old decision is still attention taken from the thing that could actually work.
:::

::: check
Explain why a single rejection is weak evidence about your candidacy, without concluding that rejections carry no information.
:::

::: answer
Outcomes are also determined by properties of the requisition — it can be filled, closed, re-scoped or moved — and from outside you cannot distinguish those cases from a genuine gap. So one rejection is one noisy observation. It is not zero information: the stage it happened at is real evidence. The correct treatment is to weight it as a single observation, and to give much more weight to a pattern, particularly a pattern that repeats at the same stage across several applications, which is the strongest signal available short of explicit feedback.
:::

## Summary

| Ended at | What it rules out | The work that follows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before any conversation | Little; the signal is weak | Self-assessment against basic qualifications; then the resume |
| Recruiter screen | Usually identifiable from the call itself | Depends on the reason — eligibility, fit, a qualification, logistics |
| Technical phone screen | Credentials, paper, eligibility | Fundamentals, and verbal delivery under time pressure |
| Take-home | Everything up to the artefact | Tests as evidence, a real README, navigable structure |
| Onsite | Every earlier filter | Your own notes from the day; feedback if offered; often stamina |
| Any stage | — | Ask once for feedback; record the stage; change something material |

That closes the module. You now have the whole process in view: a five-to-eight-week pipeline of up to nine touchpoints, a twenty-to-thirty-minute recruiter screen where eligibility and fit are settled, a thirty-minute technical screen with an engineer, a take-home for some software and firmware roles, a full day of five to seven back-to-back interviews containing a presentation you do not choose the topic of, two to three coding rounds, systems and domain rounds and estimation problems, a behavioural round answered in STAR, and a debrief that assembles five to seven separate accounts into one decision. You know why a moderate difficulty rating and a demanding process are consistent, how to get through eight hours with the last round as good as the first, what to ask each person you meet, and what to do with either outcome.

What this module deliberately has not done is teach any single round in depth. That is what comes next: the five modules that follow take the screens, the presentation, the domain round, the whiteboard and the behavioural round one at a time, and each goes far further into its stage than a map ever could.
