Think about a science fair. You stand by your poster all afternoon. One judge asks about your method. Another looks only at your graphs. A third chats with you about why you picked the topic. None of them sees the whole visit. Then, after you have gone home, the judges sit down together, put their score sheets side by side, and pick the winners. You are not in that room. You hear the result later.

Hiring works the same way. There is one stage of this process you never attend. After the onsite, the separate reports from five to seven interviewers are brought together, a decision is made, and you are told the outcome. The module calls the group that does this a **[[hiring committee|hiring-committee]]** — the people who read the interview reports and decide. The meeting where the reports are compared is the **[[debrief|debrief-word]]**. The interesting question is how that group handles the situation it almost always faces: reports that do not agree.

This lesson has to be careful about what it claims, so here is the honest line up front. This course does not know who sits in that room. It does not know whether it is a room at all or an exchange of written notes, whether a number scale or a written **[[rubric|rubric-word]]** (a scoring guide) stands behind it, whether any one interviewer can block a hire, how long it takes, or who has the final say. Those are internal matters. They vary between companies, and between teams inside one company. A confident description here would be a description you planned around — and it might be wrong.

What *can* be reasoned about, without inventing anything, is the **shape of the problem** the decision faces. That shape follows from facts the module does state: five to seven interviewers, each seeing one slice, each writing a separate report. It turns out to be more useful than the policy details would be. It tells you which of your rounds get backed up by other rounds and which stand alone.

## The inputs: slices, not the whole day

The onsite does not have one interviewer see everything. Five to seven people each get roughly an hour. Each one looks at a different part of you. Each one writes something down. Nobody in the process has watched your whole day — it is like the old story of the **[[blind men and the elephant|elephant-story]]**, where each person touches one part and describes a different animal.

Two things follow from that.

### The decision is an aggregation, not an observation

An **aggregation** is a total built by combining many separate pieces. An **observation** is something you saw with your own eyes. Whoever decides is reading reports, not remembering you.

So what matters is what was *written*. And what was written is a **[[compression|compression-picture]]** — a long thing squeezed into a short thing. Someone else did the squeezing, from memory, some time after your round ended. An hour of talk becomes a few lines.

A clear, structured answer survives that squeeze well. Here is a sentence an interviewer can write straight from a structured answer: "Stated her assumptions, set up the problem, got to a sensible margin analysis, checked it against a limiting case." (A **margin** is how much room is left before something fails; a **limiting case** is an extreme where you already know the answer.) Now picture a correct answer delivered as one long ramble. The interviewer first has to work out its shape before they can write it down, and some of it gets lost on the way.

That is one more reason — a mechanical one, not a matter of style — that the answering routines in this module keep insisting on structure before content. Structure is what makes it into the report.

### Your day becomes comparable for the first time

During the day, the rounds were separate. That was the point of the onsite lesson: a bad round does not have to spread, because the next interviewer does not know it happened. At the debrief, that separateness ends. The reports sit side by side, and anything that changed between them is now visible.

::: warning An account that drifted is expensive here, and only here
Say you describe a project as central in one round and minor in another. Or your part in it grows a little bigger with each conversation. While it is happening, nobody can see it, because each person hears only one version. The moment the reports are compared, it stands out. And it stands out at the one point in the process where you cannot explain it, because you are not in the conversation. Describe your own work the same way every time. That is easy when the description is the plain truth.
:::

## Why mixed signals are the normal case

It is tempting to picture the decision as sorting strong days from weak ones, like separating good apples from bad. That is usually not the shape of the problem, because of what the day is made of.

Each round measures a different thing. Think of each thing as an **[[axis|axis-word]]** — one direction you can be scored along, the way a graph has an x-axis and a y-axis:

- the **presentation** measures how you communicate and how you defend your design decisions;
- the **coding rounds** measure writing working code while someone watches;
- the **systems rounds** measure reasoning about **trade-offs** — giving up some of one thing to get more of another;
- the **domain rounds** measure how well you own your subject (for GNC, control, estimation and dynamics);
- the **behavioral round** measures how you work with people.

A candidate who is excellent on every axis at once is rare. A candidate who is weak on every axis is also rare at this stage, because the earlier screens filtered most of them out before the onsite. So the typical case that reaches a debrief is strong on some axes and weaker on others. A **mixed signal** means exactly that: some reports say yes and some say not yet.

::: key
Weighing mixed signals is the ordinary job of a debrief, not the exception. The rounds measure different dimensions, uniform excellence is rare, and uniform weakness was mostly filtered out before the onsite.
:::

That changes how you should think about your own day. A weak round is not a disqualifying event that a committee then has to forgive. It is the normal contents of a normal case. The real question is what it sits beside.

## Which weak rounds have backup

Here is the part you can act on. It follows straight from what the onsite is made of.

Some axes get examined more than once. Some get examined exactly once. In the language of measurement, each round is one **[[sample|sampling-noise]]** — one reading of you, taken by one person, in one hour.

- There are **two to three coding rounds**. A single weak coding round sits beside one or two other coding reports, written by different people. If those are strong, there is direct evidence on the same axis pointing the other way. A reader then has a real reason to treat the weak one as a hard problem or a bad hour.
- There are **one to two systems rounds** and **one or more domain rounds**. So the same is sometimes true there, and sometimes not.
- There is exactly **one past-project presentation**. A weak presentation has nothing on the same axis to be read against. Whatever it showed about your communication, your technical depth and your ability to defend a decision under questioning is the only evidence on those axes from the whole day — unless another round happened to touch them.

This gives a clean and honest planning rule: **weight your preparation toward the rounds that happen only once.** That is not because they matter more in principle. It is because there is no second sample to average them with.

The presentation is the clearest case. It is one round. It is the one where the panel picks the topic from your list. And it is the only place several of its axes are examined at all. Picture a candidate deciding how to split her last two weeks between a fourth coding drill and a third full rehearsal of her talk. She should notice that the coding evidence will be gathered two or three times, and the presentation evidence once.

The same logic works in reverse for worries. A concern raised in one round is one person's reading. The same concern raised separately in two rounds, by people who did not talk to each other first, is a different kind of evidence. It is **[[corroboration|corroboration-word]]** — independent support for the same conclusion. A mixed picture tends to settle around whatever the independent reports agree on.

::: key
The onsite gathers two to three coding samples, one to two systems samples and one presentation. A weak round on a dimension sampled several times sits beside contrary evidence; a weak round on a dimension sampled once stands alone. Weight preparation toward the rounds that happen only once.
:::

::: warning Do not read "sampled once" as "matters most"
The rule is about insurance, not importance. Your coding rounds still count, and three weak ones are three weak reports. The point is narrower: when you have spare hours, they buy the most protection on the round that has no second chance to balance it.
:::

::: example Two candidates, one weak round each
Two candidates have onsites with the same shape. Count the rounds: one presentation, three coding rounds, one systems round, one domain round and one behavioral round. That is $1 + 3 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 7$ rounds, at the top of the five-to-seven range.

**The first candidate** has a poor second coding round. She misreads a requirement, notices late, and finishes with code that works only after a hint. Her other two coding rounds are clean, and both interviewers say so. At the debrief, her coding evidence is three reports from three people: two strong, one weak. Whatever conclusion is reached, it is reached with direct evidence on the same axis in both directions. The weak round is *one data point*, not *the data*.

**The second candidate** has a poor presentation. The panel picked the topic she knew least well. Her talk ran long, she rushed the results, and under questioning she could not say why one central design decision had been made the way it was. Her three coding rounds are all strong.

Her situation is built differently — and not because a presentation is somehow heavier. It is because the day produced exactly one report on her communication, her depth on a project she owned, and her ability to defend a decision under direct questioning. That one report is weak. There is nothing else on those axes to read it against. Her three strong coding reports are excellent evidence, but about a different question.

**Sanity check.** Both candidates had exactly one weak round out of seven. What differs is not how many rounds went badly, but how many other samples sat on the same axis: two for the first candidate, zero for the second. Neither knows how her case was decided. What both cases show is where preparation buys the most insurance — the rounds sampled once.
:::

::: example A contribution that grew over a day
A candidate worked on a university-team **avionics** project (the electronics and software that fly a vehicle) with four other people. In her 10:00 presentation she describes it accurately. She owned the sensor interface and the **calibration** routine — the code that corrects a sensor's raw readings. Two teammates wrote the **state estimator**, the code that took her output and worked out where the vehicle was and how it was moving.

In her 13:00 systems round, talking casually about the same project in passing, she calls it "the estimator we built." Nothing dishonest is meant. It is the shorthand any team member uses about the team's work.

In her 14:30 domain round, she is asked about **filter tuning** — adjusting the estimator's settings. She answers from that shorthand and describes tuning decisions as if she had made them. The conversation has drifted, and correcting the frame now feels awkward.

Three people wrote three reports. Read side by side, her role in one project is described three different ways, growing each time: sensor interface, then "we built the estimator," then "I tuned the filter." Nobody in the room saw more than one of those conversations. That is exactly why the mismatch shows up only now — and why she has no chance to explain it.

**Sanity check.** Was her real contribution weak? No. Owning a sensor interface and a calibration routine from start to finish is real work, and it was the version she gave the panel. What cost her was not a weak project but a story that moved. The fix costs nothing: describe the same work the same way at two in the afternoon as at ten in the morning.
:::

## What happens next, and what this lesson will not tell you

You will be told the outcome. Beyond that, a few honest gaps remain, and it is better to name them than to paper over them.

**How long the decision takes.** This course cannot state it. The five-to-eight-week window from the first lesson includes it. The recruiter who owns your **[[requisition|requisition-word]]** — the approved opening you applied to — can give you a real answer for your own process. A general figure quoted here would be a figure you sat and counted days against.

**Whether the answer is only yes or no.** This lesson cannot promise that for this employer either. The levels module of this track noted a general pattern: the level a candidate is considered for is not always fixed at the first read and can move as the **[[loop|loop-word]]** goes on. That is one way a hiring process can produce something other than a plain yes or no. Whether that, or an extra round, or a referral to a different team is possible in your case is a question for the recruiter, not for a course.

**What you can do after the day to change the result.** Nothing — and it is worth saying plainly, because candidates spend real energy believing there is a lever. There is not. The only inputs are the reports, and the reports are about the day. Follow-up manners — whether and how to write afterward — are covered in the next module of this track. Writing afterward is not a way to reopen a round.

## Check yourself

::: check
Why is it accurate to say the decision is an aggregation rather than an observation, and what does that mean for how you answer?
:::

::: answer
No single person watches the whole day. Five to seven interviewers each see roughly an hour, and each writes a separate report. Whoever decides reads those reports. So what matters is what was written, and what was written is a compression made later, from memory, by someone else. A structured answer survives the compression because the interviewer can record its shape directly. A correct but unstructured answer forces them to rebuild the shape first, and some of it is lost. So structure before content is a mechanical requirement, not a matter of style.
:::

::: check
Explain why mixed signals are the ordinary case at a debrief rather than an unusual complication.
:::

::: answer
The rounds measure genuinely different dimensions: communication and design defense in the presentation, writing code under observation in coding rounds, trade-off reasoning in systems rounds, ownership of the subject in domain rounds, and working with people in the behavioral round. Being excellent on all of them at once is rare. Being weak on all of them was mostly filtered out before the onsite. So the typical case at a debrief is strong on some axes and weaker on others, and weighing that is the job itself, not an exception to it.
:::

::: check
A candidate has two weeks left and must choose between a fourth coding drill and a third full rehearsal of her presentation. Argue the choice from what the onsite is made of.
:::

::: answer
The day will gather two to three independent coding samples and exactly one presentation. A weak coding round sits beside one or two other reports on the same axis, written by different people, so a reader has contrary evidence to weigh. A weak presentation has nothing on its axes to be read against — and several of those axes, like defending a design decision under direct questioning and showing depth on work she owns, are examined nowhere else. The rehearsal buys more insurance, because the evidence it produces is sampled only once.
:::

::: check
Why is a concern raised separately in two rounds different in kind from the same concern raised in one?
:::

::: answer
Because the interviewers did not talk to each other first. Two people examining different material and reaching the same concern on their own is corroboration, in a way one person's reading is not. The second report cannot be explained away by the first interviewer's particular framing or one bad hour. Mixed pictures tend to settle around whatever the independent reports agree on.
:::

::: check
What does this lesson refuse to state about the debrief, and why is that refusal useful rather than a gap?
:::

::: answer
It refuses to say who is in the room, whether it is a meeting or an exchange of written notes, whether a rubric or number scale is used, whether any interviewer can block a hire, how long it takes, and who decides. Those are internal and vary between companies and teams. The refusal is useful because an invented account would be planned against — days counted, meaning read into a delay, behavior bent toward a mechanism that may not exist. The reasoning that remains, about which dimensions are sampled once and which several times, is both sound and directly usable.
:::

## Summary

| Element | What can be said |
| --- | --- |
| Inputs | Five to seven separate reports, each on one slice of the day |
| Nature of the decision | An aggregation of written reports, not an observation |
| Consequence for answers | Structure survives compression; unstructured correctness does not |
| Consistency | Becomes checkable at the debrief, and only there |
| Mixed signals | The ordinary case, because the rounds measure different dimensions |

::: context hiring-committee Who reads the reports
A **committee** is a small group given one job to decide together. In hiring, the name covers many real setups. At some companies it is a formal group of people who never met the candidate and read only the written packet, on purpose, so that no single interviewer's mood decides. At others it is the hiring manager plus the interviewers talking it through. The module uses "hiring committee" as a general name for whoever turns the reports into a decision — not as a claim about how any one company does it.
:::

::: context debrief-word Borrowed from pilots
Before a mission, military and airline crews get a **briefing**: short, clear instructions about what is going to happen. Afterward they get a **debriefing**: they report what actually happened, so it can be recorded and learned from. The "de-" means undoing or reversing — the information flows back instead of out. Businesses borrowed the word for any meeting held after an event to compare notes. Engineering teams hold debriefs after tests and flights for the same reason.
:::

::: context rubric-word Why a scoring guide is called a rubric
A **rubric** is a written guide that says what earns which score — the sheet a teacher uses so two graders mark the same essay the same way. The word comes from the Latin for "red." Medieval scribes wrote headings and instructions in red ink, and those red instructions became "rubrics." Today the word means the instructions for judging, whatever color they are in.
:::

::: context elephant-story Many hands, one elephant
In an old fable told in many versions, several blind men meet an elephant. One touches the trunk and says it is like a snake. One touches a leg and says a tree. One touches the side and says a wall. Each is right about his part and wrong about the whole. An onsite works the same way: every interviewer sees one part of you, and only the debrief puts the parts together.

::: context axis-word Scoring along more than one axis
On a graph, an **axis** is a line you measure along — left to right is one, bottom to top is another. Borrowed into everyday speech, an axis is any one quality you can be rated on. A candidate is not one number; she is a point in a space with five or so axes. That is why "strong" and "weak" only make sense once you say *on which axis*. Engineers describe a vehicle the same way: its state has separate axes for position, velocity and attitude.
:::

::: context sampling-noise Why more samples average out the noise
Any single measurement has **noise** — random wobble that is not the true value. A bad hour, a strange question or a tired interviewer is noise on a round. Average several independent readings and the wobbles partly cancel. For $n$ independent samples with the same scatter $\sigma$ (read "sigma"), the scatter of their average is

$$
\sigma_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

Three coding rounds shrink the scatter to $1/\sqrt{3} \approx 0.58$ of one round's. One presentation stays at the full $\sigma$.

::: context corroboration-word Strength from a second source
**Corroborate** comes from the Latin *robur*, "strength" — the same root as "robust." To corroborate a claim is to strengthen it with support from somewhere else. The key word is *independent*: two people who compared notes first count as closer to one source. Spacecraft use the same idea. A navigation system trusts a reading more when two separate sensors, built differently, agree — and grows suspicious when only one says something surprising.
:::

::: context requisition-word The job behind the posting
A **requisition**, or "req" (said "rec"), is the company's internal approval to hire one person for one job on one team. The public job posting is its outside face. Each req has a recruiter who tracks its candidates and a manager who needs the person, which is why the recruiter on your req is the right person to ask about your own timeline.
:::

::: context loop-word Why it is called a loop
Many engineering companies call the full set of onsite interviews the **interview loop**, or "the loop" for short. The name comes from the candidate being passed around a circuit of interviewers, one after another, before the group comes back together. You will hear "she's in the loop next week" or "the loop feedback is in." It means the same thing this module calls the onsite.
:::
