Think about the front desk at a busy doctor's office. Before you see the doctor, someone at the desk checks who you are, why you came in, and whether your insurance works here. That person cannot fix your broken arm. But if your paperwork is wrong, you never get past the desk. And the desk decides which doctor you see.

The first live conversation in the hiring process works the same way. It is a phone or video call with a **recruiter** — the company's hiring organizer — and it lasts **twenty to thirty minutes**. It is short, and it is not technical. That is exactly why it is the stage candidates most often underprepare for. Short and non-technical *feels* like it does not count.

It does count, in a specific way. The recruiter screen cannot get you the job. Nobody on that call is in a position to decide you are a good GNC engineer — GNC, said "G-N-C", is guidance, navigation and control, the part of a rocket that decides where to go and steers it there. Nothing you say about control theory will be graded. What the call *can* do is end your process, and it can shape everything that comes after it. This lesson explains both, and it is honest about where a general account like this one runs out.

The next module in this track, on the screens, takes this call apart in much finer detail: the background story itself, how to answer the pay question without **[[anchoring yourself low|anchoring]]**, and how the questions usually run. Here you get the stage's place on the map and its job in the process.

## What the call is for

The recruiter's job on this call is to answer one question: does it make sense to spend engineering time on you? A second job rides along with it: if you do fit, which opening should you be matched to?

That is a different question from "is this person a good engineer?" Treating the call as a weak, watered-down technical interview is the mistake behind most bad performances in it. It is a **[[screen|screen-word]]** — a filter that lets some cases through and stops others.

The call establishes three things: **background, motivation, and confirmation of export-control eligibility**. Alongside those, it sets expectations on **location**, on **timeline**, and **sometimes on compensation** — compensation meaning pay and benefits.

### Background

You will be asked to explain yourself. Who are you, what have you built, and how did you end up applying for this particular opening?

This is not your resume read aloud. The recruiter already has the resume. What the recruiter does *not* have is the thread that connects its lines to this role. Supplying that thread is the whole task.

The short version is a spoken story you have prepared and practiced. It runs about **[[ninety seconds|ninety-seconds]]**, and it ends by naming a specific **role family** — a group of closely related jobs, such as attitude control or navigation — instead of trailing off. The next module treats this story as its own skill, with drills. What matters on the map is that it is a prepared piece of work, not something you make up on the spot.

Here is why. A candidate who has never said her story out loud before the call will usually ramble for five minutes. On a thirty-minute call, five minutes is $5/30 = 1/6$ of the time. On a twenty-minute call it is $5/20 = 1/4$. So an unprepared story eats between a sixth and a quarter of the whole conversation.

### Motivation

Next comes "Why this company? Why this role?" It is harder than it looks, because the easy answers are all the same. Loving spaceflight does not set you apart on a call with a spaceflight company. Everyone the recruiter speaks to today loves spaceflight.

What *does* set you apart is being specific about the work. Name the program. Name the role family. Say something accurate about what that family actually does day to day. The second module of this track, on role families, exists to let you do exactly that. Specific answers mark your application as *targeted* — aimed at this one job — rather than *broadcast* to every opening in sight. And the recruiter can check your answer against the posting while you are talking.

### Export-control eligibility

This is the **ITAR** confirmation. ITAR, said "eye-tar", is the International Traffic in Arms Regulations — the US rules that control who may work on rocket technology. Rocket technology falls under these rules, so the company must know whether you are a **[[US person|us-person]]** — someone it may share controlled technical work with without first getting government permission.

The first module of this track covered it in full. It covered why the question is asked, and why it is asked at this stage rather than later. It covered that at this stage the question is a **[[self-attestation|self-attestation]]** — you state your status yourself, rather than handing over documents. And it covered how to answer honestly whichever way your status falls. Nothing new is added here.

One point is worth carrying forward: *why it sits at the first call*. Every stage after this one costs engineers' time. If a candidate cannot legally be hired for the role, all that time would be wasted. So the check goes first.

::: key
The recruiter screen is 20 to 30 minutes. It covers background, motivation and confirmation of ITAR US-person eligibility. It also sets expectations on location, timeline and sometimes compensation.
:::

## What gets set that you will live with

Two things get decided or pinned down on this call. Both affect the following weeks more than most candidates expect.

**Which opening you are matched to.** Each job opening has an official record called a **[[requisition|requisition]]**, or "req" for short. A recruiter may move you to a different requisition than the one you applied to. Or the recruiter may tell you your first choice is not the right fit, and another one is. That choice decides who sits on your onsite panel (the engineers who interview you on the big interview day), which domain round you get (the interview on your specialist subject), and which list of **basic qualifications** — the must-have lines in the posting, like a degree or years of experience — your case is read against.

So arrive already knowing which role family your evidence fits. If you can speak to it, you take part in the decision. If you cannot, you only watch it happen.

**The timeline and sequence of your process.** This is the call where you can find out what the remaining stages are, roughly when they will happen, and who will be involved. The previous lesson explained why this matters. Five to eight weeks is a general range. The specific version for your requisition is something the recruiter can tell you — if you ask.

::: key
Good questions for the recruiter: which program and team the requisition sits in, the stage sequence and timeline, who will be on the panel, and what the team is currently working on. Save detailed compensation and benefits questions for later unless the recruiter raises them first.
:::

::: warning Do not ask for a stated qualification to be waived
Asking the recruiter whether the degree requirement or the years-of-experience line can be set aside for you is a poor use of the call. A basic qualification is a filter written into the requisition. The recruiter did not write it and generally cannot change it. The question also signals that you read the posting as a suggestion. Work out honestly whether you clear each line before the call, using the self-assessment from the levels-and-qualifications module — not during it.
:::

## Where this account stops

A general lesson can describe what the call covers. There are several things you might reasonably want to know that it cannot tell you. Making them up would be worse than leaving them out, because you might plan around an invention.

**Whether your recruiter has a technical background.** Some do. How companies staff recruiting for engineering roles varies a lot, even inside a single company. So treat the call as non-technical by default. Explain your work the way you would to a smart friend outside your field. If the recruiter's questions show more depth, step up right away.

**What gets written down afterward, and who reads it.** Some kind of notes must exist — that follows from how the process works, because a later stage has to know you were screened and what came out of it. But their format and their readers are internal. This course has no basis for describing them.

**How fast a recruiter replies, whether there is a second recruiter call, and whether pay comes up at all.** For that last one, the module says "sometimes", and "sometimes" is the accurate word. Prepare for pay to come up, and do not be surprised if it does not.

::: example A call treated as a formality
A candidate has a strong portfolio. She built a **[[6-DOF|six-dof]]** launch vehicle simulation and ran a **dispersion campaign** on it — hundreds of runs with the inputs randomly varied, to see how much the results spread. She also built an attitude filter using **quaternions** (a four-number way to store which way a spacecraft is pointing) and checked that its error estimates were honest.

She has read nothing about the role families. On the same day, she applied to three GNC requisitions across two programs.

Here is how her call goes, step by step:

1. The recruiter asks which of the three openings interests her most. She says she is open to any of them.
2. The recruiter asks what draws her to the role. She talks about being inspired by launches.
3. The recruiter asks about her background. She walks through her resume in date order for four minutes. She ends on a recent project and never connects it to what the requisition asks for.

**Sanity check.** Four minutes of a twenty-minute call is $4/20 = 1/5$ of it, spent on material the recruiter could read off the page.

Nothing she said was false, and nothing was unprofessional. But now the recruiter has no basis for matching her to one specific requisition. There is no evidence the application was targeted. And her background story did not land anywhere. Her strongest asset — two genuinely relevant projects — was right there and went unused.
:::

::: example The same twenty minutes, used
A second candidate has a similar portfolio. She applies to one requisition. Before the call she does three things:

1. She writes a short story about herself that ends on the role family this requisition sits in — and says it out loud several times.
2. She reads what that family actually does, so she can name the *kind of problem* she wants to work on instead of naming a rocket.
3. She writes down three questions.

The call starts the same way: background, motivation, eligibility, location. Her background story takes about ninety seconds and finishes on her attitude-determination work, which is what the requisition is about. Asked about motivation, she names the specific class of problem and says which of her two projects is closest to it.

Then she asks her questions:

- *Which program and team does this requisition sit in?* She learns the team's name — one she had not worked out from the posting.
- *What does the interview sequence look like from here?* She learns there will be a technical phone screen with an engineer on the team, then a full-day onsite, and that the onsite includes a presentation on a past project.
- *What is the team working on right now?* She gets two sentences of real detail.

**Sanity check.** Ninety seconds of a twenty-minute call is $1.5/20 = 0.075$, under a tenth of it — leaving most of the time for the recruiter's questions and her own.

She leaves knowing the team's name, the remaining stages, and that she needs presentation material. That changes what she does with the next three weeks. None of it was on the job posting. All of it cost only the asking.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
Why is it accurate to say the recruiter screen can end your process but cannot get you the job?
:::

::: answer
The call is not a technical evaluation. Nobody on it is in a position to judge whether you can do GNC work, so it has no way of producing a hire.

It does have ways of ending a process. Export-control eligibility is confirmed here. A matching requisition can turn out not to exist. And a basic qualification can turn out to be unmet.

In other words, it is a screen: it removes the cases that cannot go forward and routes the ones that can.
:::

::: check
Name the three things the recruiter screen establishes, and the three things it sets expectations on.
:::

::: answer
It establishes **background**, **motivation**, and **confirmation of ITAR US-person eligibility**.

It sets expectations on **location**, **timeline**, and **sometimes compensation**. "Sometimes" is the accurate word for the last one, because whether pay comes up on this call varies.
:::

::: check
A candidate is about to have a recruiter screen and does not know whether the recruiter is technical. How should she pitch her explanations, and why is that the right default?
:::

::: answer
She should explain things as she would to a smart person outside her field. No unexplained jargon. Describe results by what they achieved, not by the method used. The moment the recruiter's own questions show more depth, she steps up.

It is the right default because recruiting for engineering roles is staffed in many different ways, even inside one company, so the recruiter's technical level is truly unknown beforehand. Pitching too high risks losing the recruiter completely. Pitching plainly costs nothing with a technical recruiter, who will ask a follow-up.
:::

::: check
Why does this lesson decline to describe what a recruiter writes down after the call?
:::

::: answer
Because the format, content and readers of internal notes are internal matters. This course has no basis to describe them, and a confident invention would be worse than silence — a candidate might plan around it.

What can be said follows from the structure alone: some record must exist, because later stages need to know the screen happened and what came out of it. Beyond that, the honest answer is that it is not known here.
:::

::: check
Two candidates ask a question at the end of the recruiter screen. One asks for the largest equity grant the recruiter can approve. The other asks which program and team the requisition sits in and what the interview sequence looks like. Explain what each question does for the candidate asking it.
:::

::: answer
The second question returns information she can act on for weeks: the team's identity, the stages left, and often advance notice of what each stage needs. None of that is on the posting.

The first question asks for a number the recruiter is very unlikely to give at this stage. It spends scarce minutes on terms for a job not yet offered. And it signals a focus on the package rather than the work. (**[[Equity|equity]]** means part-ownership of the company, given as part of pay.)

Detailed pay and benefits questions belong later in the process, unless the recruiter raises the subject first. Answering it then is a separate skill, which the next module teaches directly.
:::

::: check
A candidate spends six minutes on her background story in a twenty-five-minute recruiter screen. What fraction of the call is that, and what would a ninety-second version have left her?
:::

::: answer
Six minutes out of twenty-five is $6/25 = 0.24$, almost a quarter of the call.

A ninety-second story is $1.5$ minutes. That leaves $25 - 1.5 = 23.5$ minutes, compared with $25 - 6 = 19$ minutes for the long version — an extra $23.5 - 19 = 4.5$ minutes for motivation, eligibility and, above all, her own questions about the team and the stages ahead.
:::

## Summary

::: context anchoring The first number sticks
**Anchoring** is a well-studied habit of the human mind: the first number mentioned in a conversation pulls every later number toward it. If you name a low salary figure early, the rest of the discussion tends to stay near it, even if the job's real pay range is higher. That is why the pay question needs a plan before the call, not an answer made up on the spot. The next module, on the screens, gives you scripts for it.
:::

::: context us-person Who counts as a US person
For ITAR, a **US person** is, in short: a US citizen or national, a lawful permanent resident (a "green card" holder, allowed to live in the US for good), or someone granted refugee or asylum protection in the US. SpaceX job postings list exactly these groups, and add that a candidate can also qualify by being eligible to obtain the required authorization from the US Department of State. Being a US person is about legal status, not where you were born or where you live today. The first module of this track goes through it line by line.
:::

::: context self-attestation Saying it yourself
To **attest** is to state that something is true, on your own word. A self-attestation is you declaring your own status — for example, answering "yes" to "Are you a US person?" — rather than showing a passport. At the recruiter screen, that is usually all that is asked. Documents are typically checked later, if you are hired, and the statement you made has to match it. That is one reason to answer this question exactly, never hopefully.
:::

::: context requisition One opening, one record
A **requisition** is a company's official request to fill one job. It carries a number, a team, a budget and a list of qualifications. Recruiters shorten it to "req". When you apply to a posting, you are applying to a requisition. A recruiter can sometimes move you to a different one that fits your evidence better — which is why knowing your own role family matters on this call.
:::

::: context six-dof Six ways to move
**6-DOF**, said "six-D-O-F", means six degrees of freedom: the six independent ways a solid object can move. Three are sliding — forward-back, left-right, up-down. Three are turning — pitch (nose up or down), yaw (nose left or right) and roll (spinning around the long axis). A 6-DOF simulation tracks all six at once, which is what you need to model a real rocket flying and steering. You will build one in the technical track.
:::

::: context equity A slice of the company
**Equity** is part-ownership of a company. Many tech and space companies pay part of an engineer's compensation in shares (or in rights to buy shares) that become yours over several years. Its value depends on how the company does, so it is harder to compare than a salary. Questions about the size of an equity grant belong at the offer stage, when there is an actual offer to discuss.
:::
