The onsite is the centre of gravity of the whole process. It is **five to seven back-to-back interviews, often running more than eight hours**, and sometimes it includes a tour of the facility. Everything before it is a filter; this is the evaluation.

Two features of that description do most of the work, and they are easy to read past. The first is *back-to-back*: the rounds do not have recovery time between them, and you will be handed from one interviewer to the next with a few minutes in a corridor. The second is *eight hours or more*: this is not a long meeting, it is a working day spent being evaluated continuously, and the last hour of it is scored exactly like the first.

This lesson is about the day as a structure — its arithmetic, how the rounds relate to each other, what a fresh interviewer every hour means for how you behave, and what you should find out in advance. The next two lessons cover what the individual rounds contain. The tenth covers how to survive the day physically, which this lesson will argue is a technical question rather than a soft one.

## The arithmetic of eight hours

Work the numbers from the stated shape and the day stops being vague. This module's own day-simulation exercise builds its rounds as forty-five-minute blocks — the presentation with questions, each coding session, the systems, domain and behavioural rounds — so take a round at somewhere between forty-five minutes and an hour. Six rounds at forty-five minutes is four and a half hours of interviewing. Seven rounds at an hour is seven.

Neither of those reaches eight, and yet eight-plus is what gets reported. The difference is everything that is not an interview: handoffs between rooms, an interviewer running five minutes late, a break, something to eat, and — where it happens — a tour. Those are not padding. They are where the day's elapsed time comes from, and they are also the only moments in which you will not be being asked something.

The practical consequence is that the eight hours are not eight hours of performance, but they are eight hours of being present and attentive, and the second is more tiring than most candidates predict. This is where the module's line that physical stamina is a real variable and not a metaphor comes from. It is an arithmetic consequence of the format, not an exhortation.

::: key
The onsite is 5 to 7 back-to-back interviews, often 8+ hours, sometimes including a facility tour. Physical stamina is a real variable, not a metaphor.
:::

## Every round starts from zero

Each round is a new interviewer, and in the general case that interviewer did not watch the previous one. This single structural fact has three consequences that between them shape how you should behave all day.

**A bad round does not have to spread.** The person in front of you at eleven o'clock does not know that the ten o'clock round went badly. If you arrive visibly deflated, they will know something, and what they will know is how you behave after a setback — which is itself a thing people form views about. The round that went badly is over and is already recorded somewhere you cannot reach. The only variable still under your control is the next one.

**You will repeat yourself, and that is correct.** Several interviewers will ask some version of "tell me about your background" or "what have you worked on". Repeating your answer is not a failure of imagination on their part or disorganisation on the hiring side; it is what happens when several people each get one hour with you and each needs context. Deliver it the same way each time, with the same compactness, rather than truncating it on the third telling because you are bored of it. The fourth interviewer is hearing it for the first time.

**Consistency across rounds is visible even though the rounds are not.** The separateness ends at the debrief, where the people who each saw one slice of you compare notes. A project you described as central in one round and peripheral in another, a contribution that grows between ten o'clock and three o'clock, a number that changes — these surface at exactly the point where they are least recoverable. The twelfth lesson of this module takes the debrief in detail; the behaviour it implies starts here, and it is simply to describe your own work the same way every time because the description is true.

::: warning A round that went badly is not a verdict
Candidates who conclude mid-morning that the day is lost proceed to make it so: they disengage, they stop asking questions, they answer the afternoon's rounds at half strength. The day is not scored as a single pass or fail, it is assembled afterwards from several separate reports, and how mixed signals get weighed is exactly the subject of this module's twelfth lesson. Nobody in the afternoon knows about the morning. Behave accordingly.
:::

## What to find out before the day

The day is much easier to manage when its shape is not a surprise, and most of its shape is knowable in advance simply by asking the recruiter or the coordinator who schedules it.

Worth establishing: how many rounds and over what hours; whether a past-project presentation is included, and if so how long it should be and to how many people; whether the day is in person or remote; what you should bring, and in what form your slides need to be if you are presenting; whether there are breaks scheduled and where; and who will be on the panel, by role if not by name.

That last one has a practical use beyond curiosity. Knowing that the day includes, say, two coding rounds and a systems round tells you where your preparation hours should go in the weeks beforehand, and that is a considerably better use of the information than treating the day as an undifferentiated block to be feared.

None of these questions is unusual to ask. A coordinator arranging a day for five to seven engineers has all of this written down already.

## The facility tour

Where a tour happens, it is the only part of the day in which you get to look at the thing the job is about. Take it seriously on those grounds alone: you are evaluating this employer as well as being evaluated, and a tour is a better source of evidence about what the work is actually like than any answer to any question you could ask.

What this curriculum cannot tell you is whether a tour is part of your day — the module's word is "sometimes" — or whether anything said during one is reported back. The sensible posture does not require knowing. Treat everyone you meet during the day as part of the day, because they are people you would work with, and behave the way you would want to have behaved regardless of whether it is recorded. That is not interview strategy; it is the same standard that applies on any day you are a guest somewhere.

::: example A day that matches the stated shape
A candidate is given a schedule the week before: arrive at 9:00, finish by 17:15, six rounds.

It runs: 9:00 a short welcome and the day's plan; 9:30 the past-project presentation, forty-five minutes including questions, to a panel; 10:30 a coding round; 11:30 a second coding round; 12:30 lunch with two engineers; 13:30 a systems and architecture round; 14:30 a domain round on control and estimation; 15:45 a behavioural round; 16:30 a tour of a high bay; 17:15 finish.

That is six rounds including the presentation — a little over five hours actually inside interview rooms, inside a day of eight and a quarter hours from arrival to departure. The three hours that are not interviews are handoffs, lunch and the tour, which is exactly the gap the earlier arithmetic predicted.

Two things are worth noticing in the schedule. The lunch is not a break: two engineers are present, and whether or not anything said over it is reported anywhere, it is not a moment to stop being a professional adult. And the behavioural round is at 15:45, six and three-quarter hours after arrival, which is precisely when a candidate who skipped breakfast and has had no water is least able to tell a coherent ninety-second story.
:::

::: example Two candidates, one bad round each
Both candidates have a coding round at 11:30 that goes poorly. Each gets a problem she has not seen before, spends too long on the wrong representation, and finishes with working code only after a heavy hint. Both walk out knowing it did not go well.

The first candidate spends lunch replaying it. She is quiet with the two engineers at the table, answers their questions briefly, and does not ask them anything. In the 13:30 systems round she is flat and gives short answers; when the interviewer opens a design question with room in it, she supplies a minimal answer rather than exploring it. By the 14:30 domain round she has decided the day is lost, and she performs accordingly. One weak round has become four.

The second candidate does something small and specific in the corridor: she writes down, in two lines, what she would do differently on that problem, and closes the notebook. That is not self-consolation — it is the note she will use to prepare properly afterwards, and writing it is what lets her stop turning the problem over. At lunch she asks the two engineers what they are working on, and gets a genuinely interesting answer about a test campaign. In the systems round she is engaged, and the design discussion goes well. Her afternoon rounds are as strong as her morning presentation was.

Both candidates will have one weak coding report in the debrief. One of them will have five other reports to set against it; the other will have a day-long pattern that looks, to people reading it afterwards, like something other than a single hard problem.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
State the onsite's shape in the module's own terms, and explain from that shape why the elapsed day exceeds the time spent interviewing.
:::

::: answer
Five to seven back-to-back interviews, often more than eight hours, sometimes including a facility tour. Six rounds of forty-five minutes is four and a half hours and seven rounds of an hour is seven, so interviewing alone does not reach eight. The remainder is handoffs between rooms, interviewers running late, a break, food, and where it occurs a tour. The day is long because of everything around the rounds, not because the rounds themselves are long.
:::

::: check
Give the three consequences of each round starting with an interviewer who did not see the previous one.
:::

::: answer
First, a bad round need not spread: the next interviewer does not know it happened unless your behaviour tells them. Second, you will be asked for your background several times and should give the same compact answer each time, because each interviewer is hearing it for the first time. Third, consistency still matters, because the separateness ends at the debrief where the reports are compared — so a project's importance or your contribution to it must not drift between rounds.
:::

::: check
Why is "physical stamina is a real variable, not a metaphor" a claim about arithmetic rather than a motivational statement?
:::

::: answer
Because it follows from the format. The day is eight or more hours of continuous attention, structured as consecutive rounds with only short gaps, and every round is scored the same regardless of when it falls. A candidate whose performance degrades in the last two hours loses real evaluated rounds, not just comfort. The claim is about what the schedule does to a person, and it is checkable against the schedule.
:::

::: check
What should a candidate establish in advance about the day, and why is the panel composition the most useful of those answers?
:::

::: answer
The number of rounds and the hours, whether a presentation is included and its length and audience, in person or remote, what to bring and in what format, where the breaks are, and who will be on the panel. The panel composition is the most useful because it converts the preparation weeks from a general anxiety into an allocation: knowing there are two coding rounds and a systems round tells you where hours should go, which is information no amount of unfocused preparation substitutes for.
:::

::: check
A candidate finishes a coding round at 12:20 knowing it went badly, and has a systems round at 13:30. What is the useful thing to do in the interval, and what is the useless thing?
:::

::: answer
The useful thing is to write down, briefly, what she would do differently — which both captures something worth having afterwards and lets her stop rehearsing the problem — and then to engage with whatever the interval actually contains, including lunch with engineers, which is neither a break from professionalism nor a formal round. The useless thing is replaying the round. It cannot change the report that already exists, it degrades the rounds that are still ahead, and it converts one weak signal into a day-long pattern that reads very differently when the reports are compared.
:::

## Summary

| Element | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| Rounds | 5 to 7, back-to-back |
| Duration | Often 8+ hours from arrival to departure |
| Interview time | Roughly four and a half to seven hours of the eight-plus |
| Facility tour | Sometimes; your best look at the actual work |
| Each round | A fresh interviewer who did not see the last one |
| Therefore | A bad round need not spread; repeat your background willingly; stay consistent |
| Where separateness ends | The debrief, where reports are compared |
| Find out in advance | Round count, hours, presentation, format, breaks, panel composition |
