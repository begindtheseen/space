Picture the last day of a big swim meet. You do not swim one race and go home. You swim, climb out, dry off for a few minutes, and a new official calls you to the next block. The final race of the afternoon counts exactly as much as the first one of the morning — and by then your arms are heavy.

The **[[onsite|onsite-word]]** is that day for an engineering candidate. It is **five to seven back-to-back interviews, often running more than eight hours**, and sometimes it includes a tour of the facility. It is the **[[center of gravity|center-of-gravity]]** of the whole process — the stage everything else balances around. Everything before it is a filter. This is the evaluation.

Two phrases in that description do most of the work, and they are easy to read past.

- **Back-to-back.** There is no recovery time between rounds. One interviewer hands you to the next, with a few minutes in a hallway in between.
- **Eight hours or more.** This is not a long meeting. It is a whole working day spent being judged, and the last hour is scored exactly like the first.

This lesson is about the day as a structure: its arithmetic, how the rounds relate to each other, what a fresh interviewer every hour means for how you behave, and what to find out in advance. The next two lessons cover what the individual rounds contain. Lesson ten covers surviving the day physically — which, as you will see here, is a technical question rather than a soft one.

## The arithmetic of eight hours

Put numbers on the shape and the day stops being vague.

This module's own day-simulation exercise builds its rounds as 45-minute blocks: the presentation with questions, each coding session, the systems, domain and behavioral rounds. So take one round as somewhere between 45 minutes and an hour. Now work out the two ends of the range:

- **Short end:** six rounds at 45 minutes each is $6 \times 45 = 270$ minutes, which is $270 \div 60 = 4.5$ hours.
- **Long end:** seven rounds at an hour each is $7 \times 1 = 7$ hours.

Neither reaches eight. Yet eight-plus is what candidates report. The **[[difference|interview-arithmetic]]** is everything that is not an interview:

- handoffs between rooms;
- an interviewer running five minutes late;
- a break;
- something to eat;
- and, where it happens, a tour.

None of that is padding. It is where the day's extra hours come from. It is also the only time in the day when nobody is asking you a question.

So the eight hours are not eight hours of performing. But they are eight hours of being present and alert, and that is more tiring than most candidates expect. This is where the module's line comes from that **physical stamina is a real variable, not a metaphor**. It is not a pep talk. It falls straight out of the arithmetic of the format.

::: key
The onsite is 5 to 7 back-to-back interviews, often 8+ hours, sometimes including a facility tour. Physical stamina is a real variable, not a metaphor.
:::

## Every round starts from zero

Each round has a new interviewer. In the usual case, that person did not watch the round before. This one fact about the structure has three consequences, and together they shape how you should behave all day.

### A bad round does not have to spread

The person in front of you at eleven o'clock does not know the ten o'clock round went badly. If you walk in visibly deflated, they will learn something — and what they learn is how you act after a setback, which people form views about too.

The bad round is over. It is already written down somewhere you cannot reach. The only thing still under your control is the next one.

### You will repeat yourself, and that is correct

Several interviewers will ask some version of "tell me about your background" or "what have you worked on?" That is not poor imagination on their part or disorganization by the company. It is what happens when several people each get one hour with you and each needs the context.

Give the answer the same way every time, as tight and as complete as the first. Do not shorten it on the third telling because you are bored of it. The fourth interviewer is hearing it for the first time.

### Consistency still shows, even though the rounds are separate

The separateness ends at the **[[debrief|debrief-meeting]]** — the meeting after your day where the interviewers, who each saw one slice of you, compare notes. Anything that does not line up surfaces there:

- a project you called central in one round and minor in another;
- your part in it growing between ten o'clock and three o'clock;
- a number that changes from one telling to the next.

These surface at exactly the moment when you can no longer fix them. Lesson twelve of this module covers the debrief in detail. The behavior it asks for starts here, and it is plain: describe your own work the same way every time, because the description is true.

::: warning A round that went badly is not a verdict
Candidates who decide mid-morning that the day is lost go on to make it so. They disengage, stop asking questions, and answer the afternoon rounds at half strength. But the day is not scored as one pass or fail. It is assembled afterwards from several separate reports, and how mixed signals get weighed is exactly the subject of lesson twelve. Nobody in the afternoon knows about the morning. Behave accordingly.
:::

## What to find out before the day

The day is much easier to manage when its shape is not a surprise. Most of that shape you can learn ahead of time by asking the recruiter or the **[[coordinator|coordinator-role]]** — the person who schedules your day.

Worth finding out:

- how many rounds, and over what hours;
- whether a past-project presentation is included, and if so, how long it should be and for how many people;
- whether the day is in person or remote;
- what to bring, and what format your slides need to be in if you are presenting;
- whether breaks are scheduled, and where;
- who will be on the panel — by role, if not by name.

That last one is useful beyond curiosity. Knowing the day includes, say, two coding rounds and a systems round tells you where your preparation hours should go in the weeks before. That beats treating the day as one big, shapeless thing to dread.

None of these questions is unusual. Someone arranging a day for five to seven engineers already has all of it written down.

## The facility tour

When a tour happens, it is the only part of the day where you get to look at the thing the job is actually about — perhaps a **[[high bay|high-bay]]** where vehicles are assembled. Take it seriously for that reason alone. You are judging this employer as well as being judged, and a tour is better evidence of what the work is really like than any answer to any question you could ask.

What this course cannot tell you is whether a tour is part of your day — the module's word is "sometimes" — or whether anything said during one gets reported back. The sensible approach does not depend on knowing. Treat everyone you meet during the day as part of the day, because they are people you would work with. Behave the way you would want to have behaved whether or not it is recorded. That is not interview strategy. It is the same standard that applies any day you are someone's guest.

::: example A day that matches the stated shape
A candidate gets a **[[schedule|day-timeline]]** the week before: arrive 9:00, finish by 17:15, six rounds. (The schedule uses the 24-hour clock, so 17:15 is 5:15 p.m.)

| Time | What happens | Round length |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 9:00 | Welcome and the day's plan | — |
| 9:30–10:15 | Past-project presentation to a panel, with questions | 45 min |
| 10:30–11:20 | Coding round | 50 min |
| 11:30–12:20 | Second coding round | 50 min |
| 12:30–13:30 | Lunch with two engineers | — |
| 13:30–14:20 | Systems and architecture round | 50 min |
| 14:30–15:30 | Domain round on control and estimation | 60 min |
| 15:45–16:30 | Behavioral round | 45 min |
| 16:30–17:15 | Tour of a high bay | — |

**Step 1 — add up the rounds.** $45 + 50 + 50 + 50 + 60 + 45 = 300$ minutes, which is 5 hours inside interview rooms.

**Step 2 — measure the whole day.** From 9:00 to 17:15 is 8 hours 15 minutes, or 495 minutes.

**Step 3 — find the rest.** $495 - 300 = 195$ minutes, which is 3 hours 15 minutes of welcome, handoffs, lunch and the tour.

Sanity check: six rounds at 45 to 60 minutes should land between 4.5 and 6 hours of interviewing, and 5 hours does. The day still runs past eight hours, because of exactly the gap the arithmetic predicted.

Two things are worth noticing. First, lunch is not a break. Two engineers are at the table, and whether or not anything said there is reported, it is not the moment to stop being a professional adult. Second, the behavioral round starts at 15:45 — that is $6\tfrac{3}{4}$ hours after arrival. That is precisely when a candidate who skipped breakfast and has had no water is least able to tell a clear **[[ninety-second story|ninety-second-story]]**.
:::

::: example Two candidates, one bad round each
Both candidates have the 11:30 coding round, and for both it goes poorly. Each gets a problem she has not seen before, spends too long on the wrong way of storing the data, and only finishes with working code after a heavy hint. Both walk out knowing it went badly.

**The first candidate** spends lunch replaying it. She is quiet with the two engineers, answers their questions briefly, and asks them nothing. In the 13:30 systems round she is flat and gives short answers. When the interviewer opens a design question with room to explore, she gives a minimal answer instead. By the 14:30 domain round she has decided the day is lost, and she performs that way through the behavioral round too. Count her weak rounds: the coding round, then systems, domain and behavioral. **[[One weak round has become four|weak-round-pattern]].**

**The second candidate** does something small and specific in the hallway. She writes two lines: what she would do differently on that problem. Then she closes the notebook. That is not self-comfort. It is the note she will use to prepare properly afterwards, and writing it down is what lets her stop turning the problem over. At lunch she asks the two engineers what they are working on, and hears a genuinely interesting story about a test campaign. In the systems round she is engaged, and the design discussion goes well. Her afternoon rounds are as strong as her morning presentation was.

Both will have one weak coding report in the debrief. The second candidate has five other reports to set against it. The first has a day-long pattern that looks, to the people reading it afterwards, like something other than one hard problem.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
State the onsite's shape in the module's own terms. Then use that shape to explain why the whole day is longer than the time spent interviewing.
:::

::: answer
Five to seven back-to-back interviews, often more than eight hours, sometimes including a facility tour. Six rounds of 45 minutes is 4.5 hours, and seven rounds of an hour is 7 hours, so interviewing alone does not reach eight. The rest is handoffs between rooms, interviewers running late, a break, food, and — where it happens — a tour. The day is long because of everything around the rounds, not because the rounds themselves are long.
:::

::: check
Give the three consequences of each round starting with an interviewer who did not see the one before.
:::

::: answer
First, a bad round need not spread: the next interviewer does not know it happened unless your behavior tells them. Second, you will be asked about your background several times and should give the same compact answer each time, because each interviewer is hearing it for the first time. Third, consistency still matters, because the separateness ends at the debrief, where the reports are compared — so how important a project was, or how big your part in it was, must not drift between rounds.
:::

::: check
Why is "physical stamina is a real variable, not a metaphor" a claim about arithmetic rather than a motivational slogan?
:::

::: answer
Because it follows from the format. The day is eight or more hours of steady attention, built as one round after another with only short gaps, and every round is scored the same no matter when it falls. A candidate whose performance fades in the last two hours loses real, scored rounds — not only comfort. The claim is about what the schedule does to a person, and you can check it against the schedule.
:::

::: check
What should a candidate find out in advance about the day, and why is the panel's makeup the most useful of those answers?
:::

::: answer
The number of rounds and the hours; whether there is a presentation, how long, and for whom; in person or remote; what to bring and in what format; where the breaks are; and who is on the panel. The panel's makeup is most useful because it turns the weeks of preparation from a general worry into a plan: knowing there are two coding rounds and a systems round tells you where the hours should go. No amount of unfocused preparation replaces that.
:::

::: check
A candidate finishes a coding round at 12:20 knowing it went badly, and has a systems round at 13:30. What is the useful thing to do in between, and what is the useless thing?
:::

::: answer
The useful thing is to write down, briefly, what she would do differently. That captures something worth having later and lets her stop rehearsing the problem. Then she should engage with whatever the gap actually holds — including lunch with engineers, which is neither a break from professionalism nor a formal round. The useless thing is replaying the round. It cannot change the report that already exists, it drags down the rounds still ahead, and it turns one weak signal into a day-long pattern that reads very differently when the reports are compared.
:::

## Summary

| Element | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| Rounds | 5 to 7, back-to-back |
| Duration | Often 8+ hours from arrival to departure |
| Interview time | Roughly 4.5 to 7 hours of the eight-plus |
| Facility tour | Sometimes; your best look at the actual work |
| Each round | A fresh interviewer who did not see the last one |
| Therefore | A bad round need not spread; repeat your background willingly; stay consistent |
| Where separateness ends | The debrief, where reports are compared |
| Find out in advance | Round count, hours, presentation, format, breaks, panel makeup |

The next two lessons open the day up. The first covers the rounds that examine what you have already built and can already write; the second covers the rounds that examine how you think when you do not know the answer.

::: context onsite-word Why "onsite" even when it is on a screen
"Onsite" means "at the company's site" — their building, their desks, their factory floor. The name stuck from the years when this stage usually meant traveling there in person. Some companies now run the same full day over video, and people still call it the onsite. What defines it is not the location but the shape: the big, final block of interviews, many in a row, after the screening stages are done.
:::

::: context center-of-gravity Borrowing a word from physics
An object's **center of gravity** is the single point where its whole weight seems to act; balance a ruler on your finger and your finger is under it. People borrow the phrase for "the part everything else is arranged around." That fits the onsite. The recruiter screen and phone screen exist mostly to decide who gets one, and the debrief exists to make sense of it. You will meet the real center of gravity many times in this course — where it sits relative to the engines is central to how a rocket is steered.
:::

::: context debrief-meeting The meeting you are not in
A **debrief** is a meeting held after something is over to go through what happened — pilots and mission teams debrief after flights. After your onsite, the people who interviewed you each write up what they saw, then meet to compare. Each of them saw about an hour of you. The debrief is the first time the whole day is looked at together, which is why a story that changed between rounds gets noticed there. Lesson twelve takes this meeting apart.
:::

::: context coordinator-role The person behind the schedule
Big companies often split hiring work in two. The **recruiter** finds candidates and guides them through the process. A **recruiting coordinator** handles the logistics: booking rooms, lining up five to seven busy engineers into one day, arranging video links, and sending you the schedule. Sometimes one person does both. Either way, the person who sent your schedule is the right one to ask about breaks, room setup, slide format, and whether coding happens on a laptop or a whiteboard.
:::

::: context high-bay A building tall enough for a rocket
A **high bay** is a factory or assembly space with a very tall ceiling and overhead cranes, built so large things can be stood up, lifted and put together indoors. Rockets, stages and spacecraft are assembled and tested in high bays, often in very clean conditions. If your tour goes through one, notice what you can: how the work is laid out, how people move around the hardware, what gets tested where. It is a glimpse of the job that no slide deck can give you.
:::

::: context ninety-second-story The short story you tell about yourself
Behavioral questions — "tell me about a time when…" — are usually answered with a short, structured story: the situation, the task, what you did, and the result. That pattern is called **STAR**, and lesson eight of this module introduces it. A good answer takes about a minute and a half. It sounds easy, but it needs a clear head to pick the right example, keep the order straight and land on a result. That is why it is exactly the kind of answer that falls apart late in a long day without food or water.
:::
