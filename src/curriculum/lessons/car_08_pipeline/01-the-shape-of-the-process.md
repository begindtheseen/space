Think about a school play. Auditions are not one moment. First you sign up. Then someone calls your name for a short read-through. Then there is a call-back, maybe a second one, and then a long wait until the cast list goes up on the board. If nobody told you that was the plan, every quiet day would feel like bad news.

Getting hired as an engineer works the same way. The last seven modules brought you to the moment you press Submit. You know the legal gate called **ITAR** (said "eye-tar"), the export-control rule that decides who may work on rockets. You know the **GNC** (said "G-N-C") role families — guidance, navigation and control — and which one your evidence fits. You know how to read a job level, what the tools really are, and how to build a portfolio and a resume. All of that gets you an application. None of it tells you what happens after you apply.

This module is the map. It walks the hiring **[[pipeline|pipeline-word]]** — the chain of stages from your first conversation to the final answer. For each stage it names who is in the room, what they are judging, and how that stage can end. It does not go deep: the five modules after this one take one stage each and go much further. What this module buys you is that no stage arrives as a surprise. You will have seen its name, its shape and its way of failing before you meet it. That is the difference between preparing for a process and reacting to one.

This first lesson gives you the two numbers that frame everything else, and then an honest account of what those numbers do and do not tell you. Both halves matter. A number you read too much into is worse than no number at all, because you will plan around it.

## The two numbers

The process runs roughly **five to eight weeks** from first contact to decision, across **up to roughly nine distinct touchpoints**.

**First contact** means the first live conversation — usually a call with a **[[recruiter|recruiter-job]]**, the company's hiring organizer. It does *not* mean the moment you submitted the application. The wait between submitting and hearing from a human is the one part of the process with the least structure, so the clock starts later. **Decision** means the moment the company tells you the outcome, whichever way it goes.

::: key
Roughly 5 to 8 weeks from first contact to decision, with up to about nine distinct touchpoints. Plan your other applications around that duration rather than waiting idly.
:::

### What a touchpoint is, and what the count is for

A **touchpoint** is one separate occasion when you and the company interact and you have to be ready or respond. A call is one. So is an exercise handed to you, and the same exercise handed back. So is a scheduling email that needs a real answer, a day of interviews, or a final conversation.

Counted that way, a full process might look like this:

1. the first contact that starts it;
2. the recruiter screen;
3. the technical phone screen;
4. a take-home exercise handed out;
5. the same take-home handed in;
6. a scheduling exchange for the onsite;
7. the onsite day itself;
8. a follow-up round, if the day left a question open;
9. the decision conversation.

That makes nine. But treat the list as an illustration, not an itinerary. Nobody publishes a definition of a touchpoint, and reasonable people [[count differently|counting-touchpoints]]. Whether each of the onsite's five to seven interviews counts on its own, for example, changes the total a lot.

So do not spend effort on the bookkeeping. The number does one useful job. It tells you this is a process with many separate occasions to be ready for, spread over more than a month — not two interviews and an offer. Whether your own process has seven contacts or ten changes nothing about how you prepare.

### Why the elapsed time is almost entirely waiting

Add up the time you actually spend being interviewed:

- a recruiter screen of 20 to 30 minutes;
- a technical phone screen of about 30 minutes;
- the onsite, one day, often more than eight hours.

The two calls come to an hour at most. Add the onsite and your total live time is about one long working day. That one day is spread across five to eight weeks.

The gap is not mysterious, and it is not a judgment about you. Every stage after the recruiter screen needs an *engineer's* calendar, not a recruiter's. Engineers on a launch or spacecraft program spend the rest of their time doing the actual work. The onsite is the extreme case. It needs five to seven of those calendars lined up on one day, in one place. That is a scheduling problem that gets [[much harder than the head count suggests|calendar-odds]]: each extra person makes a shared free day more than one step harder to find. Most of the five to eight weeks is that problem being solved — not you being argued over.

That mechanism is worth taking to heart, because it defuses the most common misreading of a quiet week. In the ordinary case, a gap between stages is evidence about calendars. It is not evidence about you, in either direction.

::: warning Silence is not a signal
A long gap between stages tells you almost nothing about how you are doing. Interviewers travel, launches slip, hiring priorities get reshuffled, people take vacation. Reading a quiet two weeks as a rejection — or as enthusiasm — means acting on information that is not there. If the gap affects a real decision you have to make, ask the recruiter directly instead of guessing.
:::

## What the range does not tell you

This course can tell you the size of the window. It cannot tell you:

- where inside that window *your* process will land;
- how long any particular gap between two stages will be;
- how quickly a decision follows the onsite;
- whether a job is moving faster than usual because a team is short of people.

Those things vary by team, by program, by time of year and by how busy the particular people are. No honest general statement covers them.

That is not a hole in the lesson. It is a description of where the information actually lives: with the recruiter who owns your **[[requisition|requisition-word]]** — the specific opening you applied to. Asking about timing is exactly what a recruiter is there for. It is not pushy; it is the normal, expected use of the first conversation. Lesson eleven covers what to ask at each stage in detail. For now, note that "What does the interview sequence look like from here, and what timeline should I have in mind?" has a real answer, and the answer is about *your* process, not processes in general.

::: key
Where your process lands inside the five-to-eight-week window is not something a curriculum can tell you. It is something the recruiter who owns the requisition can. Ask for the stage sequence and the expected timeline at the recruiter screen, and plan against that answer rather than against the general range.
:::

## Planning around the window

Here is the practical consequence of a five-to-eight-week process: you cannot run it as your only one. Nothing is wrong with the application. The problem is what a long pipeline does to your options when it is all you have.

Run other applications **in parallel** — at the same time — and start them early instead of holding them in reserve. There are two reasons.

- **A rejection becomes survivable.** If this process ends in a "no" at week six, you are six weeks into other pipelines instead of starting from zero.
- **Pressure goes away.** A candidate with nothing else running is under pressure, and pressure shows. It shows in how eagerly you answer a pay question, in whether you push back on an interview date that really does not work, and in how you read a quiet week.

The failure this creates deserves a precise name, because it is the one most likely to bite you: the **forced decision**. Another company makes you an offer with a **[[deadline|exploding-offer]]**, and the deadline lands before this process has finished. That is not a rare edge case. It is ordinary arithmetic when you run a five-to-eight-week process next to shorter ones.

There is no clever way out. What there is:

1. Tell the recruiter the date, plainly and factually, as soon as you have it.
2. Ask whether the remaining stages can fit before it.
3. When the deadline arrives, decide on the information you actually have.

An offer in hand is a real thing. A process at week four is not.

::: example One candidate's calendar, stage by stage
A candidate applies for a GNC opening on 6 October. Here is what happens, with the gap before each step:

- **14 October** — a recruiter emails and they speak that day. That is $14 - 6 = 8$ days after she applied.
- **23 October** — the technical phone screen, $23 - 14 = 9$ days later.
- **12 November** — the onsite. October has 31 days, so from 23 October to the end of the month is $31 - 23 = 8$ days, plus 12 days into November: $8 + 12 = 20$ days. It took longest because it needed a panel of engineers on one day.
- **20 November** — she hears the outcome, $20 - 12 = 8$ days after the onsite.

**From first contact.** Add the gaps after 14 October: $9 + 20 + 8 = 37$ days. Divide by 7 days per week: $37 \div 7 \approx 5.3$ weeks.

**From the application.** Add the first gap too: $8 + 37 = 45$ days, and $45 \div 7 \approx 6.4$ weeks.

**Sanity check.** Both readings sit inside the five-to-eight-week window. This also shows why the window is wide: the answer depends on where you start counting.

**The lesson in the breakdown.** Of those 45 days, her live evaluation was about half an hour with the recruiter, half an hour with an engineer, and [[one long day onsite|calendar-timeline]]. Every other day was a calendar being arranged. Nothing in the twenty-day gap before the onsite was about her.
:::

::: example The forced decision, handled well and handled badly
Two candidates are in week three of a SpaceX process. Both have passed the phone screen; neither has an onsite date yet. Both get an offer from a smaller avionics company with a ten-day deadline.

**The first candidate** tells neither side anything and hopes the SpaceX process speeds up on its own. It does not, because nobody involved knows there is a reason to hurry. On day nine she accepts the other offer with no more information than she had on day one. She spent nine days anxious instead of nine days deciding.

**The second candidate** emails the SpaceX recruiter the same day. She says she has a written offer with a decision date of the 24th, she is still interested in this opening, and she would like to know whether the remaining stages can fit before then. This is a statement of fact, not a bargaining move, and she words it as one.

Three replies are possible, and all three help her:

- The recruiter can speed things up. Now she gets a real comparison.
- The recruiter says plainly it cannot be done. She decides on the offer in hand without wondering.
- The recruiter does not know yet and says so. At least she knows the answer is not a yes.

In every branch she decides *with* information. In none of them has she damaged anything. A candidate with a competing deadline is an ordinary situation that recruiters handle all the time.
:::

## The stages, in order

The rest of this module takes the stages one at a time. The order is fixed. Whether every stage appears is not.

| Stage | Roughly | Who | Lesson |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Recruiter screen | 20 to 30 minutes | A recruiter | 2 |
| Technical phone screen | 30 minutes, sometimes 60 to 90 | A team engineer or the hiring manager | 3 |
| Take-home exercise | Varies; not universal | You, alone | 4 |
| Onsite | 5 to 7 interviews, often 8+ hours | A panel and individual engineers | 5, 6, 7 |
| Behavioral and culture | Numbered stage 5; placement varies | Engineers, managers | 8 |
| Debrief and decision | Not seen by you | The hiring side | 12 |

The **onsite** is the full day of interviews at the company's site. The **hiring manager** is the person who would be your boss. The **[[debrief|debrief-bridge]]** is the meeting, after your onsite, where the interviewers compare notes — you are not in it.

The take-home is the one stage that depends on the *role* rather than on you. Lesson four explains where it does and does not appear. Everything else in the table is the normal path.

## Check yourself

::: check
State the two framing numbers for this process, and say precisely what "first contact" and "decision" mean as endpoints.
:::

::: answer
Roughly five to eight weeks, across up to roughly nine distinct touchpoints.

"First contact" is the first live interaction — normally the recruiter screen — not the moment you submitted. The wait between submitting and hearing anything is the least structured part of the process, so it is left out.

"Decision" is the point at which the company tells you the outcome, whichever way it goes.
:::

::: check
A candidate has heard nothing for eleven days after her technical phone screen. What does that gap tell her about how the screen went, and what should she do with it?
:::

::: answer
It tells her almost nothing about the screen. The main cost in this process is calendar time, not deliberation. An onsite needs five to seven engineers lined up on one day, and arranging that routinely takes longer than the interviews themselves. A gap is evidence about schedules, not about her, in either direction.

If the delay affects a real decision — say, a competing offer's deadline — the useful move is to ask the recruiter for the current timeline, not to read one into the silence.
:::

::: check
Why does this lesson refuse to say how long the gap between the onsite and the decision usually is?
:::

::: answer
Because that particular interval varies by team, program, time of year and how available the people involved are, and no honest general statement covers it. The course knows the overall window. It does not know the inside of your requisition.

The information does exist. It lives with the recruiter who owns your process, and asking for the stage sequence and expected timeline is a normal use of the recruiter screen.
:::

::: check
Explain the forced-decision problem and why running parallel applications does not eliminate it.
:::

::: answer
The forced-decision problem: another company's offer deadline lands before the five-to-eight-week process has finished, so you must choose between a real offer and an unfinished process.

Parallel applications do not remove it. In fact they *cause* it — the competing offer has to come from somewhere. What they do is make the outcome survivable. A rejection at week six leaves you partway through other pipelines instead of at zero. And having a real alternative removes the pressure that bends how you behave inside the long process.

The forced decision itself is handled by telling the recruiter the date plainly and early, then deciding on the information you actually have.
:::

::: check
A candidate counts her touchpoints and gets seven, not nine, and worries that she is in a cut-down or less serious version of the process. What is wrong with that reasoning?
:::

::: answer
The figure is "up to roughly nine", and the unit is not precisely defined. Whether each onsite interview counts separately, whether scheduling emails count, and whether the first contact counts all shift the total.

The number exists to say "this is a long process with many separate occasions to prepare for". It is not a checklist to audit a real process against. Seven contacts fits the same process perfectly well and says nothing about how seriously she is being taken.
:::

## Summary

| Fact | Value | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total length | Roughly 5 to 8 weeks, first contact to decision | You cannot run it as your only process |
| Touchpoints | Up to roughly nine | Many separate occasions to prepare for, not two interviews |
| Live evaluation time | Under an hour of calls, plus one long onsite day | The rest of the calendar is scheduling, not deliberation |
| A quiet gap | Evidence about calendars | Not evidence about you, either way |
| Where in the range you land | Not knowable from a course | Ask the recruiter for the sequence and timeline |
| Parallel applications | Start early, do not hold in reserve | Makes a rejection survivable and removes distorting pressure |

::: context recruiter-job What a recruiter does
A recruiter is a company employee whose job is to find candidates and move them through hiring. The recruiter is usually not an engineer. They schedule the interviews, answer questions about the process, collect results, and deliver the offer or the "no". Think of them as the stage manager of your process: not in the play, but the person who knows when every scene happens. That is why timing questions go to them.
:::

::: context counting-touchpoints Nine, or fifteen?
Here is how much the counting rule matters. The list in the lesson treats the whole onsite day as one touchpoint and gets nine. Count each onsite interview separately instead: remove the one "onsite" item and add five to seven interviews. That gives $9 - 1 + 5 = 13$ at the low end and $9 - 1 + 7 = 15$ at the high end. Same process, very different number — which is why the count is a sense of scale, not a checklist.
:::

::: context calendar-odds Why more people is much harder
Suppose, as a made-up but fair example, that each engineer is free on a given day 7 times out of 10, independently of the others. For one engineer the chance a day works is $0.7$. For two it is $0.7 \times 0.7 = 0.49$. For six it is $0.7^6 \approx 0.118$ — about one day in eight or nine.

::: context requisition-word "Req" means one opening
A **requisition** is a company's official request to fill one job: it has a number, a team, a budget and a list of qualifications. Recruiters and managers shorten it to "req". When you apply to a job posting, you are applying to a requisition. It matters because the requisition decides who interviews you and what the bar is — and a recruiter can sometimes move you to a different one that fits you better.
:::

::: context exploding-offer The offer that expires
An offer with a short acceptance deadline is sometimes called an **exploding offer**: if you do not say yes by the date, it goes away. Companies set deadlines because they need to plan, and if you decline they want to move to their next candidate. A deadline is normal and is not a trick. What matters is that you learn the date early and tell your other processes about it early, while there is still time for them to respond.
:::

::: context debrief-bridge The meeting you never see
After the onsite, the interviewers each write up what they saw, and the group meets to weigh it — often with the hiring manager and recruiter. You are not there, and you usually do not hear what was said. Lesson twelve explains how that meeting weighs mixed results, such as a strong coding round next to a weak systems round, and why "mixed" is the normal case rather than a bad sign.
:::
