Seven modules of this track have brought you to the moment you press Submit. You know which legal gate stands in front of an export-controlled role and where it is checked; you know the GNC role families and which one your evidence actually fits; you know how a level is read off a posting and how to assess yourself honestly against one; you know what the tooling really is; you have built a portfolio and a resume that put the strongest evidence where a fast reader looks. All of that is preparation for an application. None of it tells you what happens after the application exists.

This module is the map. It walks the process from first contact to decision, names every stage, says who is in the room, what is being evaluated, and how the thing ends. It is deliberately not a deep dive: the five modules that follow take one stage each and go far further into it than this one will. What this module buys you is that no stage of a real application ever arrives as a surprise — you will have seen its name, its shape and its failure mode before it is in front of you, which is the difference between preparing for a process and reacting to one.

This first lesson gives you the two numbers that frame everything else, and then the honest account of what those numbers do and do not tell you. Both of those halves matter. A number you over-read is worse than no number at all, because you will plan around it.

## The two numbers

The process runs roughly **five to eight weeks** from first contact to decision, across **up to roughly nine distinct touchpoints**.

"First contact" means the first live interaction — usually the recruiter screen — rather than the moment you submitted the application, because the interval between submission and any human response is the one part of the process with the least structure to it. "Decision" means the point at which the company tells you the outcome, whichever way it goes.

::: key
Roughly 5 to 8 weeks from first contact to decision, with up to about nine distinct touchpoints. Plan your other applications around that duration rather than waiting idly.
:::

### What a touchpoint is, and what the count is for

A touchpoint is a distinct occasion on which you and the company interact and you have to be prepared or responsive: a call, an exercise handed over and handed back, a scheduling exchange that needs a real answer from you, a day of interviews, a final conversation. Counted that way, a full process might run: recruiter screen; technical phone screen; a take-home handed out; the same take-home submitted; a scheduling exchange for the onsite; the onsite day itself; a follow-up or additional round if the day left a question open; and a decision conversation. Add the initial contact that started it and the count sits near nine.

That enumeration is an illustration, not an itinerary. Nobody publishes a definition of the unit, and different reasonable people would count differently — whether each of the onsite's five to seven interviews is its own touchpoint, for instance, changes the total considerably. Do not spend effort on the bookkeeping. The number is doing one job, and it is a useful one: it tells you that this is a process with many separate occasions to be ready for, spread over more than a month, rather than two interviews and an offer. Whether your own process turns out to have seven contacts or ten changes nothing about how you prepare for it.

### Why the elapsed time is almost entirely waiting

Add up the time you actually spend being interviewed. A recruiter screen of twenty to thirty minutes, plus a technical phone screen of thirty minutes, comes to under an hour. Add the onsite — one day, often more than eight hours — and the total live evaluation time is on the order of a single long working day. That day is spread across five to eight weeks.

The gap is not mysterious and it is not a judgment about you. Every stage after the recruiter screen needs an engineer's calendar, not a recruiter's, and engineers on a launch or spacecraft program are doing the actual work the rest of the time. The onsite is the extreme case: it needs five to seven of those calendars aligned on a single day, in a single place, which is a scheduling problem that grows much harder than linearly with the number of people involved. Most of the five to eight weeks is that problem being solved, not you being deliberated over.

That mechanism is worth internalising because it defuses the most common misreading of a quiet week. A gap between stages is, in the ordinary case, evidence about calendars. It is not evidence about your candidacy, in either direction.

::: warning Silence is not a signal
A long gap between stages tells you almost nothing about how you are doing. Interviewers travel, launches slip, requisitions get re-prioritised, people take leave. Reading a quiet fortnight as a rejection — or as enthusiasm — means acting on information that is not there. If the gap is affecting a real decision you have to make, ask the recruiter directly rather than inferring.
:::

## What the range does not tell you

This curriculum can tell you the shape of the window. It cannot tell you where inside it your own process will land, how long any particular gap between two stages will be, how quickly a decision follows an onsite, or whether a given requisition is moving faster than usual because a team is short-handed. Those things vary by team, by program, by time of year and by how busy the specific people involved happen to be, and no honest general statement covers them.

That is not a gap in the lesson. It is a description of where the information actually lives, which is with the recruiter who owns your requisition. This is exactly the kind of question a recruiter is there to answer, and asking it is not presumptuous — it is the normal, expected use of the first conversation. The eleventh lesson of this module covers what to ask at each stage in detail; for now, note that "what does the interview sequence look like from here, and what timeline should I have in mind" is a question with a real answer, available to you, about your process rather than about processes in general.

::: key
Where your process lands inside the five-to-eight-week window is not something a curriculum can tell you. It is something the recruiter who owns the requisition can. Ask for the stage sequence and the expected timeline at the recruiter screen, and plan against that answer rather than against the general range.
:::

## Planning around the window

The practical consequence of a five-to-eight-week process is that you cannot run it as your only process. Not because anything is wrong with the application, but because of what a long pipeline does to your options if it is the only one you have.

Run other applications in parallel, and start them early rather than holding them in reserve. Two reasons. First, if this process ends in a rejection at week six, a parallel search means you are six weeks into other pipelines rather than starting from zero at that point. Second, and less obvious: a candidate with nothing else running is a candidate under pressure, and pressure shows. It shows in how eagerly you answer a compensation question, in whether you push back on a scheduling request that genuinely does not work, and in how you read a quiet week.

The failure this creates is worth naming precisely, because it is the one most likely to actually bite you: the forced decision. Another company makes you an offer with a deadline, and the deadline lands before this process has finished. That is not a hypothetical edge case — it is the ordinary arithmetic of running a five-to-eight-week process alongside shorter ones. There is no clever way out of it. What there is: tell the recruiter the date plainly and factually as soon as you have it, ask whether the remaining stages can fit inside it, and then make the decision on the information you actually have when the deadline arrives. An offer in hand is a real thing. A process at week four is not.

::: example One candidate's calendar, stage by stage
A candidate submits an application for a GNC requisition on 6 October. A recruiter emails on 14 October and they speak the same day — eight days after submission. The technical phone screen is scheduled for 23 October, nine days later. The onsite takes the longest to arrange: it lands on 12 November, twenty days after the phone screen, because it needs a panel of engineers on one day. She hears the outcome on 20 November, eight days after the onsite.

From first contact on 14 October to the decision on 20 November is 37 days, a little over five weeks. From submission on 6 October to decision, it is 45 days — about six and a half weeks. Both readings sit inside the five-to-eight-week window, which is a good illustration of why the window is broad: the answer depends on where you start counting.

The instructive part is the breakdown. Of those 45 days, her live evaluation time was roughly half an hour on the phone with a recruiter, half an hour with an engineer, and one long day onsite. Every other day was a calendar being negotiated. Nothing in the twenty-day gap before the onsite was about her.
:::

::: example The forced decision, handled well and handled badly
Two candidates are in week three of a SpaceX process — phone screen cleared, onsite not yet scheduled. Both receive an offer from a smaller avionics company with a ten-day acceptance deadline.

The first candidate says nothing to either party and hopes the SpaceX process accelerates on its own. It does not, because nobody involved knows there is a reason for it to. On day nine she accepts the other offer with no more information than she had on day one, having spent nine days anxious instead of nine days deciding.

The second candidate emails the SpaceX recruiter the same day: she has a written offer with a decision date of the 24th, she remains interested in this requisition, and she wants to know whether the remaining stages can realistically fit before then. This is a statement of fact, not leverage, and it is phrased as one. Three outcomes are possible and all three are useful. The recruiter may be able to compress the schedule, in which case she gets a real comparison. The recruiter may say plainly that it cannot be done, in which case she decides on the offer in hand without wondering. Or the recruiter may not know yet and say so, which at least tells her the answer is not a yes. In every branch she is deciding with information rather than without it, and in none of them has she damaged anything — a candidate with a competing deadline is an ordinary situation that recruiters handle constantly.
:::

## The stages, in order

The rest of this module takes these one at a time. The order is fixed; the presence of one of them is not.

| Stage | Roughly | Who | Lesson |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Recruiter screen | 20 to 30 minutes | A recruiter | 2 |
| Technical phone screen | 30 minutes, sometimes 60 to 90 | A team engineer or the hiring manager | 3 |
| Take-home exercise | Varies; not universal | You, alone | 4 |
| Onsite | 5 to 7 interviews, often 8+ hours | A panel and individual engineers | 5, 6, 7 |
| Behavioural and culture | Numbered stage 5; placement varies | Engineers, managers | 8 |
| Debrief and decision | Not observed by you | The hiring side | 12 |

The take-home is the one stage genuinely conditional on the role rather than on you, and lesson four explains where it does and does not appear. Everything else in that list is the normal path.

## Check yourself

::: check
State the two framing numbers for this process, and say precisely what "first contact" and "decision" mean as endpoints.
:::

::: answer
Roughly five to eight weeks, across up to roughly nine distinct touchpoints. "First contact" is the first live interaction, normally the recruiter screen, rather than the moment of submission — the interval between submitting and hearing anything is the least structured part of the process. "Decision" is the point at which the company tells you the outcome, whichever way it goes.
:::

::: check
A candidate has heard nothing for eleven days after her technical phone screen. What does that gap tell her about how the screen went, and what should she do with it?
:::

::: answer
It tells her essentially nothing about the screen. The dominant cost in this process is calendar time, not deliberation — an onsite needs five to seven engineers aligned on one day, and arranging that routinely takes longer than the interviews themselves. A gap is evidence about schedules, not about her candidacy in either direction. If the delay is affecting a real decision she has to make, such as a competing offer deadline, the useful action is to ask the recruiter directly for the current timeline rather than to infer one from the silence.
:::

::: check
Why does this lesson refuse to say how long the gap between the onsite and the decision usually is?
:::

::: answer
Because that specific interval varies by team, program, time of year and the availability of the people involved, and no honest general statement covers it. The curriculum knows the overall window; it does not know your requisition's internals. The information exists, but it lives with the recruiter who owns your process, and asking for the stage sequence and expected timeline is a normal use of the recruiter screen.
:::

::: check
Explain the forced-decision problem and why running parallel applications does not eliminate it.
:::

::: answer
The forced-decision problem is that another company's offer deadline can land before a five-to-eight-week process has concluded, requiring a choice between a real offer and an unfinished process. Running parallel applications does not eliminate it — in fact parallel applications are what generate it, since the competing offer has to come from somewhere. What parallel applications do is make the outcome survivable: a rejection at week six leaves you mid-pipeline elsewhere rather than starting from zero, and having a real alternative removes the pressure that distorts how you behave inside the long process. The forced decision itself is handled by telling the recruiter the date plainly and early, and then deciding on the information you actually have.
:::

::: check
A candidate counts her touchpoints and gets seven, not nine, and worries that she is somehow in a truncated or less serious version of the process. What is wrong with that inference?
:::

::: answer
The figure is "up to roughly nine", and the unit is not precisely defined — whether each onsite interview counts separately, whether scheduling exchanges count, and whether the initial contact counts all shift the total. The number exists to convey that this is a long process with many distinct occasions to prepare for, not to serve as a checklist against which a real process can be audited. Seven contacts is entirely consistent with the same process; it says nothing about seriousness.
:::

## Summary

| Fact | Value | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total length | Roughly 5 to 8 weeks, first contact to decision | You cannot run it as your only process |
| Touchpoints | Up to roughly nine | Many separate occasions to prepare for, not two interviews |
| Live evaluation time | Under an hour of calls, plus one long onsite day | The rest of the calendar is scheduling, not deliberation |
| A quiet gap | Evidence about calendars | Not evidence about your candidacy, either way |
| Where in the range you land | Not knowable from a curriculum | Ask the recruiter for the sequence and timeline |
| Parallel applications | Start early, do not hold in reserve | Makes a rejection survivable and removes distorting pressure |
