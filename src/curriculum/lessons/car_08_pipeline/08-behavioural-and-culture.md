The fifth stage is the one with no equations in it. You will be asked about things that have already happened to you — a failure, a disagreement, a decision made without enough information — and you will be expected to answer in a particular shape.

Candidates who are strong technically often treat this round as a formality to be survived on the way back to the real interviews, and that is a misreading with a concrete cost. The technical rounds establish whether you can do the work. This round is about what you are like to do the work *with*, over years, on a team that has to trust your account of your own results. Those are different questions and the second one is not softer than the first; it is simply evaluated from different evidence.

This lesson covers the structure the round expects, the themes it draws from, and the one question in it that deserves a genuinely honest answer rather than a prepared one. The thirteenth module of this track builds the story bank and drills the delivery; this is the map of what the round is.

## Where it sits

The module numbers this stage fifth, after the onsite. Whether it reaches you as a separate conversation or as one of the onsite day's rounds is one of the things that varies, and it is worth asking the coordinator when you ask about the rest of the day's shape. Nothing about how you prepare changes either way, but knowing where it falls matters for a reason the tenth lesson of this module will develop: a behavioural round late in a long day asks for sustained narrative fluency at exactly the hour it is hardest to produce.

## STAR, and the failure that defines it

Answers are expected in STAR structure: **Situation, Task, Action, Result**.

The Situation is the context, compressed to what the listener needs. The Task is what you specifically were responsible for. The Action is what you did — the decisions, the trade-offs, the steps. The Result is what happened, stated in terms that can be checked.

The failure mode is so common that it is worth treating as the definition of doing this badly: **most of the answer goes on the Situation**. This is not carelessness; it is what happens naturally. The situation is the part you remember most vividly, it requires no self-assessment to narrate, and it feels like it needs explaining before anything else can make sense. So a candidate spends sixty seconds setting up a project, twenty on what she did, and trails off without a result.

Consider the arithmetic of a ninety-second story. Something like fifteen seconds of Situation and ten of Task is enough setup for almost any story an interviewer will ask for. That leaves around forty-five seconds for Action — the part the question was actually about — and twenty for a clean Result. Invert that allocation and you have answered a question nobody asked, using the time that was meant for the answer.

::: key
STAR is Situation, Task, Action, Result. The defining failure is spending most of the answer on Situation. In a ninety-second story, setup is roughly the first twenty-five seconds; the Action is the bulk of it, and the Result closes it in a form that can be checked.
:::

## A bank of stories, not an improvisation

Prepare ten to twelve stories, each of about ninety seconds. That is a specific number and it is worth taking literally.

The case for a bank rather than improvising per question rests on two observations. The first is that the themes are largely known in advance, so improvisation is solving a problem you could have solved at a desk. The second is that under fatigue — and this round may well fall late in a long day — recall of a rehearsed narrative holds up considerably better than the construction of a new one.

The themes actually probed in rounds like this include: a failure and what changed afterwards; conflict with a colleague; a decision made with incomplete information; a missed deadline; a time you were wrong; ownership of something beyond your formal scope; teaching or bringing along someone else; and working extended hours.

Ten to twelve stories against eight themes is not an accident of arithmetic. One good story usually serves more than one theme — a project where you were wrong about an approach, admitted it late, and missed a deadline as a result can be told as a failure story, a being-wrong story or a deadline story depending on which part you foreground. Build the bank around real episodes and then map the themes onto them, rather than trying to manufacture one episode per theme.

**Quantify the Result.** A story that ends "and it went well" is not checkable and therefore not evidence. A story that ends "the landing dispersion came down from one and a half kilometres to under four hundred metres, and we shipped a week late instead of three" gives a listener something with edges on it. This is the same discipline the resume module of this track applied to bullets, for the same reason: a measured result invites a follow-up question, and the follow-up question is where a good story does its work.

::: warning Do not build a story bank out of stories that did not happen
A fabricated episode fails under the follow-up, which in this round is guaranteed rather than likely: the interviewer will ask what the other person said, what you would do differently, what the number was before. Real episodes have that detail available without effort because you were there. Invented ones run out in two questions, and what the interviewer learns at that point is not about the story.
:::

## The extended-hours question, answered honestly

Postings in this family state an expectation directly: working extended hours and weekends when needed to meet critical deadlines. Because it is written into the posting, it can reasonably be raised in this round, and a candidate should have thought about it before it is.

This curriculum is not going to tell you what to say, and the reason is worth being explicit about. What a week actually looks like on a specific team, in a specific program phase, is not something a curriculum knows. It varies by team, by where a vehicle is in its cycle, by whether a launch campaign is running, and by the manager. Anyone who tells you a number for it in general is guessing. What is stated is the expectation in the posting; what is not stated anywhere reliable is the lived reality of any particular team, and that gap is real.

Two things follow. The first is that the honest answer is the one you could still live with in the second year. An enthusiastic yes given to clear a round becomes a commitment you made on the record, to people who will remember it, about a pace you may not want. The second is that this round is the right place to gather evidence for your own decision: the topic is open, you are talking to people who actually live the schedule, and asking what a heavy week looks like on this team, and how often those weeks come, is a fair question inside a conversation about extended hours.

Intensity and pace are a genuine trade-off to evaluate **before** you accept a role, not after. That framing is the useful one, because it turns an uncomfortable question into a piece of due diligence you were going to have to do anyway.

::: example One story, told twice
A candidate is asked to describe a time she was wrong about something technical.

Her first version, timed at two minutes ten: she spends the first seventy seconds explaining a simulation project — what the vehicle model was, how the aerodynamic tables were built, which integrator she used, who else was involved and what they each did. Then twenty seconds on having chosen a fixed step that was too coarse. Then she says that she fixed it, and that it was a good lesson, and stops.

The interviewer has heard a great deal about a simulation and almost nothing about the candidate. There is no result, no account of how she discovered the error, and no evidence of what changed in how she works.

Her second version, timed at ninety-five seconds, keeps the same episode. Situation, fifteen seconds: she was building a 6-DOF ascent simulation and validating it before a dispersion campaign. Task, ten seconds: she owned the integration scheme and the verification of it. Action, fifty seconds: she had chosen a fixed step on the basis that the results looked smooth, which she now recognises as no evidence at all; the error surfaced when a conservation check on a torque-free case drifted more than it should have; she ran a step-halving study, found the error was not falling at the order it should, traced it to the step being too coarse for the fastest mode in the vehicle's rotational dynamics, and re-ran the verification suite from the analytic cases up. Result, twenty seconds: the dispersion campaign was delayed four days; the convergence study is now a standing test in the repository rather than something she runs when suspicious, and she has not shipped a fixed-step choice without one since.

Same episode. The second version is shorter, and it answers the question that was asked.
:::

::: example The extended-hours question, three ways
Three candidates are asked, in a behavioural round, how they feel about the expectation of extended hours and weekends when needed to meet critical deadlines.

The first says yes without hesitation: hours are not a concern, she will do whatever it takes, she has always worked that way. She has not thought about it. She has also made a statement on the record to people who will remember it, and if the reality turns out to be one she does not want, the conversation in eighteen months starts from what she said today.

The second treats the question as a trap and hedges: she talks about work-life balance in general terms and about the importance of sustainability, without ever addressing the expectation the posting states. She has neither answered the question nor learned anything, and the interviewer is left unsure what she actually meant.

The third answers, and then uses the question. She says she has worked stretches like that before — naming a specific one, a two-week period before a test deadline — that she can do it when a deadline is real, and that what she cares about is whether the intensity is tied to something that matters rather than being the default state. That is an honest answer with evidence attached, and it is not a promise she will regret. Then she asks: what does a heavy week actually look like on this team, and how often do they come? The answer she gets is specific and it is about this team rather than about the company in the abstract, and it is one of the most useful things she learns all day — because she is deciding too.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
Name the four parts of STAR and the failure that most often ruins an answer, and explain why that failure happens so reliably.
:::

::: answer
Situation, Task, Action, Result. The failure is spending most of the answer on Situation. It happens reliably because the situation is the most vividly remembered part, it requires no self-assessment to narrate, and it feels like necessary setup — so a candidate drifts into it naturally and arrives at the Action with little time left. The question was about the Action, so the time has been spent on the part nobody asked about.
:::

::: check
Why prepare a bank of ten to twelve ninety-second stories rather than answering each question as it comes?
:::

::: answer
Two reasons. The themes are largely known in advance — failure, conflict, deciding with incomplete information, a missed deadline, being wrong, ownership beyond scope, teaching someone, extended hours — so improvising is solving at speed a problem that could have been solved at a desk. And this round may fall late in a long day, where recalling a rehearsed narrative holds up much better under fatigue than constructing a new one. Ten to twelve covers eight themes because good stories serve several themes depending on which part is foregrounded.
:::

::: check
Why does quantifying the Result matter, and what does a quantified result invite?
:::

::: answer
An unquantified result is not checkable and therefore is not evidence — "it went well" could describe anything. A number gives the claim edges. It also invites the follow-up question, which is where the story does its real work: an interviewer who asks what the figure was before, or how it was measured, is engaging with the substance rather than listening politely. This is the same discipline the resume module applied to bullets, for the same reason.
:::

::: check
Why does this lesson refuse to tell you what the working hours are actually like, and what does it tell you to do instead?
:::

::: answer
Because the lived reality varies by team, by program phase, by whether a campaign is running, and by the manager, and no general figure would be honest. What is stated is the expectation written into the posting; what is not reliably stated anywhere is what a given team's week looks like. Instead, the lesson says to give an answer you could still live with in the second year rather than one that clears a round, and to use the round to gather your own evidence — asking people who live the schedule what a heavy week looks like and how often those weeks come.
:::

::: check
A candidate has no story for "conflict with a colleague" and is tempted to adapt one that did not really happen. What will go wrong, and what should she do instead?
:::

::: answer
It will fail under the follow-up, which in this round is effectively guaranteed: she will be asked what the other person actually said, what she would do differently, what the outcome was. Real episodes carry that detail without effort; invented ones run out within a question or two, and what the interviewer learns at that point is about her account of herself rather than about the conflict. Instead she should go back through real episodes and find the one that contains a disagreement — most projects with more than one person on them do — and foreground that part, which is how a bank of ten to twelve real stories covers eight themes in the first place.
:::

## Summary

| Element | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| Structure | Situation, Task, Action, Result |
| Defining failure | Most of the answer spent on Situation |
| Allocation in 90 seconds | About 25 seconds of setup, the bulk on Action, a clean quantified Result |
| The bank | Ten to twelve stories, roughly 90 seconds each, from real episodes |
| Themes | Failure, conflict, deciding with incomplete information, a missed deadline, being wrong, ownership beyond scope, teaching, extended hours |
| Result | Quantified, so it is checkable and invites a follow-up |
| Extended hours | Expectation stated in the posting; the lived reality varies and is not knowable in general |
| The right answer | One you could still live with in year two — and a question back |
