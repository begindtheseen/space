Sailors have known the fix for two centuries: if another vessel holds a constant bearing while its range closes, you are on a collision course, and the only way off it is to change something. Proportional navigation turns that observation into a guidance law by inverting it — instead of watching the bearing for a collision you want to avoid, force the bearing to stop changing on purpose, and you have steered onto a collision course deliberately. Everything in this lesson is that one idea, made precise: what "bearing rate" means exactly, what acceleration command drives it to zero, and why the command actually does what it claims rather than merely reacting to symptoms.

This is the law both pursuit and line-of-sight guidance were reaching for without quite arriving. Pursuit's command, $a_{lat} = V_p\dot\lambda$, already had the right *shape* — proportional to the line-of-sight rate — but the wrong multiplier and no free gain. Proportional navigation (PN) fixes both: it replaces the pursuer's own speed with the closing velocity, and it introduces a tunable constant $N$. That one substitution and one addition are the difference between a law that turns harder and harder as it fails, and a law that is provably self-correcting.

## The collision-course principle, made precise

Let $\mathbf{r}_{rel} = \mathbf{r}_T - \mathbf{r}_M$ be the position of the target relative to the pursuer (missile, chaser — the vehicle doing the steering) and $\mathbf{v}_{rel} = \dot{\mathbf{r}}_{rel}$ its rate of change, with range $R = \lVert\mathbf{r}_{rel}\rVert$. Decompose $\mathbf{v}_{rel}$ into a component along the line of sight and a component perpendicular to it. The along-LOS component is $\dot R$, and closing velocity is defined as $V_c \equiv -\dot R$ (positive when range is shrinking). If the perpendicular component is exactly zero, $\mathbf{v}_{rel}$ is purely radial: the target is moving directly away from or toward the pursuer along the current line of sight, the line of sight itself never rotates, and if $V_c > 0$ the two will occupy the same point at $t = R/V_c$ if neither changes course. That is the entire content of "constant bearing, decreasing range is a collision course," stated in coordinates instead of on a ship's bridge: **zero line-of-sight rate together with positive closing velocity is a collision course**, for any two bodies whatsoever, independent of what either one is individually doing.

## The line-of-sight rate and closing velocity, exactly

To use this, "line-of-sight rate" needs a precise, general definition — not a planar small-angle approximation, but an exact one that works in three dimensions for any relative motion. Write $\mathbf{v}_{rel} = \dot R\,\hat{\mathbf{r}} + R\,\dot{\boldsymbol\lambda}\times\hat{\mathbf{r}}$, decomposing the relative velocity into its radial part and a transverse part generated by the line of sight's own rotation $\dot{\boldsymbol\lambda}$ (an angular velocity vector, perpendicular to $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ by construction — rotation of a direction has no component along that direction). Cross both sides with $\mathbf{r}_{rel} = R\hat{\mathbf{r}}$:

$$
\mathbf{r}_{rel}\times\mathbf{v}_{rel} = R\hat{\mathbf{r}}\times\dot R\hat{\mathbf{r}} + R\hat{\mathbf{r}}\times\big(R\,\dot{\boldsymbol\lambda}\times\hat{\mathbf{r}}\big) = 0 + R^2\Big[\dot{\boldsymbol\lambda}(\hat{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}) - \hat{\mathbf{r}}(\hat{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol\lambda})\Big] = R^2\dot{\boldsymbol\lambda},
$$

using the vector triple product and $\hat{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol\lambda} = 0$. So, exactly, with no small-angle assumption anywhere:

$$
\dot{\boldsymbol\lambda} = \frac{\mathbf{r}_{rel}\times\mathbf{v}_{rel}}{\mathbf{r}_{rel}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{rel}}, \qquad V_c = -\dot R = -\frac{\mathbf{r}_{rel}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{rel}}{\lVert\mathbf{r}_{rel}\rVert},
$$

the second from differentiating $R^2 = \mathbf{r}_{rel}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{rel}$ directly. Both are computed from nothing but the instantaneous relative position and velocity — exactly what a seeker, radar, or relative-navigation solution provides.

## The proportional navigation law

PN commands an acceleration perpendicular to the line of sight, proportional to the closing velocity and the line-of-sight rate:

$$
\mathbf{a}_c = N\,V_c\,\big(\dot{\boldsymbol\lambda}\times\hat{\mathbf{r}}\big),
$$

with $N$ the **navigation constant**, typically $3$ to $5$ (the next lesson derives why). The cross product with $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ guarantees $\mathbf{a}_c\cdot\mathbf{r}_{rel} = 0$ — the command is always exactly perpendicular to the current line of sight, by construction, whatever the engagement geometry — and its magnitude is $N V_c\lvert\dot{\boldsymbol\lambda}\rvert$, vanishing exactly when $\dot{\boldsymbol\lambda}$ does. On a true collision course PN commands nothing at all, which is correct: nothing needs correcting.

::: key Proportional navigation
$$
\mathbf{a}_c = N\,V_c\,\big(\dot{\boldsymbol\lambda}\times\hat{\mathbf{r}}\big), \qquad \dot{\boldsymbol\lambda} = \frac{\mathbf{r}_{rel}\times\mathbf{v}_{rel}}{\mathbf{r}_{rel}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{rel}}, \qquad V_c = -\frac{\mathbf{r}_{rel}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{rel}}{\lVert\mathbf{r}_{rel}\rVert}.
$$
Scalar form: $a_c = NV_c\dot\lambda$, perpendicular to the line of sight. $\dot\lambda$ is the LOS rate, $V_c$ the closing velocity, $N$ the navigation constant (3 to 5). PN needs no knowledge of the target's own trajectory — only the instantaneous relative state.
:::

::: example Computing a PN command from the relative state
A chaser closing on a non-cooperative target: chaser velocity $\mathbf{v}_M = (100,\ -5,\ 0)\,\mathrm{m/s}$, target relative to chaser at $\mathbf{r}_{rel} = (3000,\ 800,\ 0)\,\mathrm{m}$ with $\mathbf{v}_{rel} = (-120,\ 15,\ 0)\,\mathrm{m/s}$ (so the target's own velocity is $\mathbf{v}_{rel} + \mathbf{v}_M = (-20,\ 10,\ 0)\,\mathrm{m/s}$ — a modest drift, not a deliberate maneuver).

```python
import numpy as np
r_rel = np.array([3000.0, 800.0, 0.0]); v_rel = np.array([-120.0, 15.0, 0.0])
R = np.linalg.norm(r_rel)
lam_dot = np.cross(r_rel, v_rel) / R**2
Vc = -np.dot(r_rel, v_rel) / R
N = 4.0
a_c = N * Vc * np.cross(lam_dot, r_rel / R)
print(R, Vc, lam_dot[2], a_c, np.dot(a_c, r_rel))
# 3104.834... 112.0833... 0.01462656... [-1.6896 6.3362 0.] 3.979e-13
```

$R = 3104.8\,\mathrm{m}$, $V_c = 112.08\,\mathrm{m/s}$, $\dot\lambda = 0.014627\,\mathrm{rad/s}$, and the $N=4$ command is $\mathbf{a}_c = (-1.690,\ 6.336,\ 0)\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, magnitude $6.558\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ — perpendicular to $\mathbf{r}_{rel}$ to within $4\times10^{-13}$, floating-point roundoff. Compare this to what pursuit (the last lesson) would have commanded from the chaser's own speed at this same instant: $V_p = \lVert\mathbf{v}_M\rVert = 100.12\,\mathrm{m/s}$, so $a_{lat} = V_p\dot\lambda = 1.464\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ — PN's command is $4.478$ times larger here, which is exactly $NV_c/V_p$, not a coincidence but the direct algebraic consequence of the two laws sharing the same $\dot\lambda$ and differing only in which speed multiplies it and by how much. Which of the two numbers is "right" is not decided by which is bigger; it is decided by what happens next, which the next section derives.
:::

## Why the command actually nulls the LOS rate

Posting a formula that vanishes on a collision course is not the same as showing that formula *drives you toward* one. This is where the "nulling principle" in this lesson's topic earns its name: substitute the PN command into the relative dynamics and solve for what $\dot\lambda$ actually does over time.

In a plane, with $R,\lambda$ as polar coordinates for the relative position, the standard decomposition of acceleration into radial and transverse parts — the same one the classical mechanics module uses for motion in polar coordinates — gives the transverse component of relative acceleration as $a_{rel,\perp} = R\ddot\lambda + 2\dot R\dot\lambda$. The second term is not optional or small; it is the exact consequence of differentiating $R\dot\lambda$ (the transverse relative velocity) while $R$ itself is changing.

Against a non-maneuvering target ($\mathbf{a}_T = 0$), the relative acceleration is entirely due to the pursuer's own command, $\mathbf{a}_{rel} = -\mathbf{a}_c$, and its transverse component is $a_{rel,\perp} = -NV_c\dot\lambda$ by construction of the PN law itself. Equating the two expressions, with $\dot R = -V_c$:

$$
R\ddot\lambda - 2V_c\dot\lambda = -NV_c\dot\lambda \quad\Longrightarrow\quad R\ddot\lambda = -(N-2)V_c\dot\lambda \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ddot\lambda = -(N-2)\frac{\dot\lambda}{t_{go}},
$$

using $R = V_c t_{go}$ in the last step. This is a first-order linear ODE in $\dot\lambda$ once written in terms of $t_{go}$ (which counts down to zero as $t$ runs forward, so $dt_{go} = -dt$): separating variables and integrating gives the closed form

$$
\dot\lambda(t_{go}) = \dot\lambda_0\left(\frac{t_{go}}{t_{go,0}}\right)^{N-2}.
$$

For $N > 2$, the exponent is positive and $\dot\lambda \to 0$ as $t_{go}\to 0$ — the line-of-sight rate genuinely collapses toward zero as intercept approaches, which is precisely the collision-course condition being reached by feedback. At exactly $N=2$, the exponent is zero and $\dot\lambda$ stays at its initial value, converging on nothing. Below $N=2$, the exponent is negative and $\dot\lambda$ *grows* without bound as $t_{go}\to0$ — proportional navigation with too small a navigation constant reproduces pursuit's own pathology.

::: example The LOS rate collapsing in practice
Simulate a full nonlinear closed-loop PN engagement — target at $(4000,\ 1200,\ 0)\,\mathrm{m}$ moving at $(-40,\ 0,\ 0)\,\mathrm{m/s}$, pursuer starting at the origin at $(60,\ 25)\,\mathrm{m/s}$, PN recomputed from the exact instantaneous relative state every step — and sample $\dot\lambda$ against the closed-form prediction:

```python
# lambda_dot0 = 1.146789e-03 rad/s, tgo0 = 40.558 s (from the initial relative state)
for N in (3.0, 4.0, 5.0):
    # ... integrate the closed-loop engagement (as in the example above) ...
    # at t_go = 4.08 s: measured lambda_dot vs lambda_dot0*(t_go/t_go0)**(N-2)
    pass
# N=3: measured 1.154079e-04, predicted 1.154704e-04 (ratio 0.99946)
# N=4: measured 1.161547e-05, predicted 1.162385e-05 (ratio 0.99928)
# N=5: measured 1.169071e-06, predicted 1.170021e-06 (ratio 0.99919)
```

The closed form matches the full nonlinear simulation to within $0.1\%$ across the whole engagement for every $N$ tested — the mild residual is exactly the constant-$V_c$ approximation used to write $R = V_c t_{go}$, which holds extremely well whenever the engagement stays close to a collision triangle, as a converging PN engagement does by design. Run the same simulation at $N=2$ and $\dot\lambda$ stays at $1.146789\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ to the last displayed digit for the entire engagement — the marginal case, exactly as the closed form predicts.
:::

::: warning $N > 2$ is necessary, not sufficient, and not the whole story
This lesson's derivation shows $N > 2$ is what makes proportional navigation *converge* at all — below it, PN inherits pursuit's own divergence, just scaled by how far below $2$ the constant sits. It does not yet say why real systems use $3$ to $5$ rather than, say, $2.1$: convergence is a much weaker requirement than good performance under lag, noise, and a target that maneuvers, and the next lesson is entirely about that gap.
:::

::: note This derivation assumed a non-maneuvering target
Setting $\mathbf{a}_T = 0$ was essential to writing $a_{rel,\perp} = -NV_c\dot\lambda$ directly from the command. A maneuvering target adds its own transverse acceleration into the relative dynamics, which the plain PN law has no term to cancel — the LOS rate no longer collapses to zero but to a nonzero residual driven by the target's maneuver. Augmented proportional navigation, in the next lesson, adds exactly the term that cancels it.
:::

## Check yourself

::: check
A pursuer measures $\mathbf{r}_{rel} = (500,\ 0,\ 0)\,\mathrm{m}$ and $\mathbf{v}_{rel} = (-80,\ 10,\ 0)\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Find $\dot\lambda$ and $V_c$.
:::

::: answer
$\dot\lambda = (\mathbf{r}_{rel}\times\mathbf{v}_{rel})/R^2$; the cross product's $z$-component is $r_x v_y - r_y v_x = 500(10) - 0(-80) = 5000$, and $R^2 = 500^2 = 250\,000$, so $\dot\lambda = 5000/250\,000 = 0.02\,\mathrm{rad/s}$. $V_c = -\mathbf{r}_{rel}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{rel}/R = -\big(500(-80)+0(10)\big)/500 = -(-40\,000)/500 = 80.0\,\mathrm{m/s}$.
:::

::: check
Why does proportional navigation use the closing velocity $V_c$ rather than the pursuer's own speed, when pursuit guidance's formula looked almost identical?
:::

::: answer
Because $V_c$ is what actually appears in the exact kinematics linking the commanded transverse acceleration to how fast the line-of-sight rate responds — the derivation of the nulling principle used $R = V_c t_{go}$ and $\dot R = -V_c$ directly, and those relations are about the *engagement geometry*, not about the pursuer's airspeed. Pursuit's $V_p$ was never derived from anything; it appeared only because pursuit's heading happens to equal $\lambda$ by definition, which fixes the pursuer's own turn rate to $\dot\lambda$ regardless of whether that is the right quantity for the engagement. PN starts from the question "what makes $\dot\lambda \to 0$" and $V_c$ is what the answer to that question actually contains.
:::

::: check
Explain, without further algebra, why "$\dot\lambda = 0$ and $V_c > 0$" is a complete description of a collision course, for any two bodies at all.
:::

::: answer
$\dot\lambda = 0$ means the line of sight is not rotating, so the relative velocity has no component perpendicular to it — the relative motion is purely along the line connecting the two bodies. $V_c > 0$ means that line is shrinking. A purely radial relative velocity that is shrinking the range means the two bodies are moving directly toward each other along a fixed line in space, with nothing left to change their relative bearing; if both continue exactly as they are, they occupy the same point at $t = R/V_c$. Nothing about either body's individual trajectory — straight, curved, powered, unpowered — enters this argument; it is purely a statement about the *relative* motion, which is why the principle transfers unchanged from ships at sea to spacecraft in orbit.
:::

::: check
At some point in an engagement, $t_{go,0} = 30\,\mathrm{s}$ and $\dot\lambda_0 = 0.002\,\mathrm{rad/s}$, flown with $N = 3.5$. What is $\dot\lambda$ when $t_{go} = 10\,\mathrm{s}$?
:::

::: answer
$\dot\lambda(t_{go}) = \dot\lambda_0(t_{go}/t_{go,0})^{N-2} = 0.002\times(10/30)^{1.5} = 0.002\times0.19245 = 3.849\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ — the LOS rate has collapsed to about $19\%$ of its earlier value as two-thirds of the remaining time has elapsed, consistent with a positive exponent driving it toward zero.
:::

::: check
A simulated engagement flown at $N = 1.5$ shows $\dot\lambda$ rising from $1.37\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ to $6.55\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ as range collapses from about $630\,\mathrm{m}$ to $130\,\mathrm{m}$. Is this a bug in the simulation?
:::

::: answer
No — it is exactly what the closed form predicts for $N < 2$. With $N=1.5$, the exponent $N-2=-0.5$ is negative, so $\dot\lambda(t_{go}) = \dot\lambda_0(t_{go}/t_{go,0})^{-0.5}$ grows as $t_{go}$ shrinks, unboundedly as $t_{go}\to0$. $N=1.5$ still nulls the range to the stopping tolerance in this particular run because the simulation was stopped at a few metres rather than run to an exact geometric zero, but the LOS rate — and with it the commanded acceleration $NV_c\dot\lambda$ — is diverging exactly the way pursuit's did in the previous lesson. This is the practical reason $N > 2$ is a hard floor, not a suggestion.
:::

## Summary

| Quantity | Statement |
| --- | --- |
| Collision-course principle | $\dot\lambda = 0$ and $V_c > 0$ together mean the two bodies meet if neither changes course |
| LOS rate (exact) | $\dot{\boldsymbol\lambda} = (\mathbf{r}_{rel}\times\mathbf{v}_{rel})/(\mathbf{r}_{rel}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{rel})$ |
| Closing velocity (exact) | $V_c = -(\mathbf{r}_{rel}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{rel})/\lVert\mathbf{r}_{rel}\rVert$ |
| PN command | $\mathbf{a}_c = NV_c(\dot{\boldsymbol\lambda}\times\hat{\mathbf{r}})$; scalar form $a_c = NV_c\dot\lambda$, perpendicular to the LOS |
| LOS-rate nulling principle | $\dot\lambda(t_{go}) = \dot\lambda_0(t_{go}/t_{go,0})^{N-2}$ against a non-maneuvering target |
| Convergence condition | $N>2$: $\dot\lambda\to0$ as $t_{go}\to0$. $N=2$: $\dot\lambda$ constant. $N<2$: $\dot\lambda$ diverges, like pursuit |

$N>2$ guarantees convergence; it says nothing yet about which value in the $3$-to-$5$ range real systems actually fly, or why that range is exactly where lag, noise and target maneuver push the design. That is the next lesson.
