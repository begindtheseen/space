=== cad03-l01 | Practice: The command line and exact points
--- for
l01-the-autocad-command-line

+++ question | Three corners of a cable tray
--- ask
Dynamic Input is off. You draw part of a cable tray outline:

```text
Command: L
LINE
Specify first point: 20,10
Specify next point or [Undo]: @45,0
Specify next point or [Undo]: @30<90
```

Where is the third point? Type it as x,y.
--- answer
65,40
(65,40)
65, 40
(65, 40)
--- why
The first point is absolute: $(20, 10)$. `@45,0` is relative, so add 45 to $x$: $(65, 10)$. `@30<90` is polar from that point: 30 units at 90°, which is straight up, so $\Delta x = 30\cos 90^\circ = 0$ and $\Delta y = 30\sin 90^\circ = 30$. The third point is $(65, 40)$.

+++ question | A diagonal with ortho on
--- ask
Ortho mode (F8) is on. The last point is $(40, 40)$. You need the next line to be exactly 50 long, pointing down and to the left at 225°. Which entry draws it?
--- choice
Point the cursor down and to the left and type `50`. Ortho allows any direction as long as the distance is typed.
--- choice correct
Type `@50<225`. A typed coordinate goes exactly where it says, whatever ortho is doing with the cursor.
--- choice
Type `@50<-225`. Negative angles are measured the other way, so this is the same as 225°.
--- choice
Type `50<225`. The `<` already says the point is measured from the last point.
--- why
Ortho only holds the cursor to horizontal and vertical, so direct distance entry with ortho on can only go along 0°, 90°, 180° or 270°. A typed coordinate is not affected. $-225^\circ$ means turning 225° clockwise, which lands on 135°, up and to the left. And without the `@`, `50<225` is measured from the origin $(0,0)$, not from $(40, 40)$. For many lines at 45° steps, switch on polar tracking (F10) at 45°, which switches ortho off, and type distances along its guide lines.

+++ question | Starting from a snapped quadrant
--- ask
A circle is centered at $(50, 50)$ and is 40 across. You start a line like this:

```text
Command: L
LINE
Specify first point: QUA
of  (click near the rightmost point of the circle)
Specify next point or [Undo]: @30<120
```

Where does the line end? Type it as x,y, rounded to two decimals.
--- answer
55,75.98
(55,75.98)
55, 75.98
(55, 75.98)
55.00,75.98
--- why
40 across means a radius of 20. The quadrant snap at the right of the circle is the point at 0°: $(50 + 20, 50) = (70, 50)$. The polar move gives $\Delta x = 30\cos 120^\circ = 30 \times (-0.5) = -15$ and $\Delta y = 30\sin 120^\circ = 30 \times 0.8660 = 25.98$. The end is $(70 - 15,\; 50 + 25.98) = (55, 75.98)$. The minus sign on $\Delta x$ is right: 120° is past 90°, so the line leans left.

+++ question | The same text, two answers
--- ask
You start a line at $(100, 100)$ and type `20,0` for the second point. Where does the line end with Dynamic Input on, and where with Dynamic Input off?
--- choice
At $(20, 0)$ both times: a point without `@` is always absolute.
--- choice
At $(120, 100)$ both times: the second point of a line is always relative.
--- choice correct
At $(120, 100)$ with Dynamic Input on, and at $(20, 0)$ with it off.
--- choice
At $(20, 0)$ with Dynamic Input on, and at $(120, 100)$ with it off.
--- why
With Dynamic Input on, AutoCAD treats a typed second point as relative even without `@`, so `20,0` means 20 to the right: $(120, 100)$. On the plain command line with Dynamic Input off, `20,0` is the absolute point $(20, 0)$. Typing `@` forces relative and `#` forces absolute in either mode, which is why careful drafters always type the prefix.

+++ question | A frame that shoots off the sheet
--- ask
A drafter meant to draw a 150 by 90 frame with its first corner at $(500, 300)$. Dynamic Input is off.

```text
Command: L
LINE
Specify first point: 500,300
Specify next point or [Undo]: @150,0
Specify next point or [Undo]: 0,90
Specify next point or [Close/Undo]: @-150,0
Specify next point or [Close/Undo]: C
```

What went wrong?
--- choice
`@-150,0` is wrong: negative values are not allowed after `@`.
--- choice correct
The third line, `0,90`, has no `@`. It sends the line to the absolute point $(0, 90)$ near the origin instead of 90 up from $(650, 300)$. It should be `@0,90`.
--- choice
The first point should be `@500,300`, because every point in a command must be relative.
--- choice
`C` closes back to the origin instead of to the first point, so the last side is wrong.
--- why
Without `@`, a typed point is measured from the origin. So the second side runs from $(650, 300)$ all the way to $(0, 90)$, the next point is $(0 - 150, 90) = (-150, 90)$, and Close draws back to the true first point, $(500, 300)$. Negative relative values are normal: `@-150,0` means 150 to the left. The first point of a shape is often absolute, and Close always returns to the first point of the command.

+++ question | From two corners to one polar entry
--- ask
The last point is $(40, 30)$. The next corner must be at $(10, 70)$, and you want to type it as a polar point, `@d<angle`. What do you type? Round the angle to two decimals.
--- answer
@50<126.87
@50<126.9
--- why
First the relative move: $\Delta x = 10 - 40 = -30$ and $\Delta y = 70 - 30 = 40$. The distance is $\sqrt{30^2 + 40^2} = \sqrt{2500} = 50$. For the angle, $\arctan(40 / 30) = 53.13^\circ$ is only the size of the angle to the $x$ axis. The move goes left and up, so it lies in the second quarter of the circle, between 90° and 180°: $180 - 53.13 = 126.87^\circ$. A calculator's plain $\arctan(40 / -30)$ gives $-53.13^\circ$, which points right and down, the wrong way. Here `@-30,40` would be the simpler and exact entry.

=== cad03-l02 | Practice: Drawing and editing shapes
--- for
l02-draw-and-modify

+++ question | Two offsets of one hole
--- ask
A circle is 30 across. You run OFFSET with a distance of 5, pick the circle and click outside it. Then you pick the original circle again and click inside it. What are the diameters of the two new circles?
--- answer
40 and 20
40, 20
40 20
20 and 40
20, 40
20 40
--- why
Offsetting a circle changes its radius by the offset distance. The original radius is 15. Outside: $15 + 5 = 20$, a diameter of 40. Inside: $15 - 5 = 10$, a diameter of 20. A common slip is to change the diameter by 5 instead of the radius, which would give 35 and 25.

+++ question | Five holes on a flange
--- ask
ARRAYPOLAR puts 5 holes evenly around a full circle, on a pitch circle 80 mm across. How far apart are two neighboring hole centers, in a straight line? Round to two decimals, in millimeters.
--- answer
47.02
47.02 mm
47.0
--- why
Five holes over 360° are $360 / 5 = 72^\circ$ apart. The pitch circle radius is $80 / 2 = 40$. The chord between two points $\alpha$ apart on a circle of radius $r$ is $2r\sin(\alpha / 2) = 2 \times 40 \times \sin 36^\circ = 80 \times 0.5878 = 47.02$ mm. It is not the radius, as it was for six holes: only a hexagon has sides equal to its radius.

+++ question | A stretch that breaks the spacing
--- ask
A plate is 150 by 90, with its bottom-left corner at $(0, 0)$. It has six separate hole circles at $x = 15, 75, 135$ and $y = 15, 75$. You run STRETCH with a crossing box from $(160, 100)$ to $(100, -10)$ and move it by `@30,0`. After the stretch, how far is the middle column of holes from the new middle of the plate, in millimeters?
--- answer
15
15 mm
--- why
The box spans $x = 100$ to $160$. The right edge and the right-hand corners are inside, so they move 30 and the plate becomes $150 + 30 = 180$ long. The holes at $x = 135$ have their centers inside, so they move to $165$, still 15 from the new edge. The middle holes at $x = 75$ are outside the box and stay put. The new middle of the plate is at $180 / 2 = 90$, so the middle holes are $90 - 75 = 15$ mm off center. STRETCH moves what is inside the box; it knows nothing about "evenly spaced". Move the middle column by `@15,0` as well.

+++ question | Fillet on a parallel pair
--- ask
The FILLET radius is set to 5. You pick two parallel lines that are 20 apart, near their bottom ends. What happens?
--- choice
AutoCAD reports an error, because parallel lines have no corner to round.
--- choice
It draws a quarter arc of radius 5 at the end of each line.
--- choice correct
It joins the ends with a half circle of radius 10, ignoring the radius setting.
--- choice
It makes a sharp corner, as if the radius were 0.
--- why
Two parallel lines have no corner, so no arc of any chosen radius can turn one into the other. The only arc that touches both is a half circle whose diameter is the gap: 20 across, radius 10. AutoCAD draws that and ignores the radius setting. It is a quick way to round the end of a slot.

+++ question | A plate that would not stretch
--- ask
A plate outline is one closed polyline, 100 by 60, with a hole near each right-hand corner. A drafter runs STRETCH, drags a box from $(70, -5)$ to $(110, 65)$, types `@40,0`, and gets this: the two right-hand holes move 40 to the right, and the plate outline does not change at all. What went wrong?
--- choice
STRETCH cannot change a polyline; the outline should have been drawn with LINE.
--- choice correct
The box was dragged from left to right, which makes a window selection. It picked only objects wholly inside it: the two holes. The polyline was not wholly inside, so it was not picked. Drag from right to left to get a crossing box.
--- choice
The displacement should have been typed as `40,0`, without the `@`.
--- choice
The holes were an associative array, which blocked the stretch.
--- why
The direction of the drag decides the kind of box. Left to right is a window: only whole objects inside are picked, and STRETCH simply moves them. Right to left is a crossing: anything inside or touched is picked, vertices inside move and lines crossing the edge stretch. Here the box started at $x = 70$ and ended at $x = 110$, so it went left to right. STRETCH works fine on polylines; it moves the vertices that are inside a crossing box.

+++ question | Working back from the slot depth
--- ask
The top edge of a plate is at $y = 100$. You need a slot 16 wide, centered at $x = 50$, whose round bottom's lowest point is at $y = 60$. You will draw the left side with LINE up to the top edge, offset it to the right by 16, and fillet the two lines near their bottom ends. Where must the left side's bottom end be? Type it as x,y.
--- answer
42,68
(42,68)
42, 68
(42, 68)
--- why
The slot is centered at $x = 50$ and is 16 wide, so its sides are at $50 - 8 = 42$ and $50 + 8 = 58$. Filleting the two parallel sides gives a half circle whose diameter is the 16 gap, so its radius is 8 and its center sits level with the line ends. The lowest point of the half circle is one radius below the line ends, so the ends must be at $60 + 8 = 68$. The left side runs from $(42, 68)$ up to $(42, 100)$.

=== cad03-l03 | Practice: Layers
--- for
l03-layers

+++ question | Half of it follows
--- ask
Layer `E-COND` is red, `HIDDEN` and 0.25 mm. A line on it has these properties: Color ByLayer, Linetype `CENTER`, Lineweight ByLayer. Someone changes `E-COND` to green, `Continuous` and 0.35 mm. How does the line look now?
--- choice
Green, continuous and 0.35 mm: it is on the layer, so it follows every change.
--- choice correct
Green, dashed in the `CENTER` pattern, and 0.35 mm.
--- choice
Red, `CENTER` and 0.25 mm: a line keeps the look it was drawn with.
--- choice
Red, continuous and 0.35 mm.
--- why
Each property is its own setting. Color and lineweight are ByLayer, so they follow the layer: green and 0.35 mm. Linetype is a per-object override, `CENTER`, so it ignores the layer's change to `Continuous`. That mixed result is exactly why overrides make a drawing hard to manage. `SETBYLAYER` would put the linetype back to ByLayer.

+++ question | Trace it, never move it
--- ask
A new propellant line is drawn over a surveyed site plan. The survey must stay visible, you must be able to snap to it, and nobody may move or erase it by accident. Which layer setting fits?
--- choice
Turn the survey layers off.
--- choice
Freeze the survey layers.
--- choice correct
Lock the survey layers.
--- choice
Set the survey layers to No Plot.
--- why
Lock keeps the objects visible and snappable but stops them being edited. Off and freeze both hide the survey, so you could not trace it. No Plot only keeps it off the printed sheet; on screen it is as editable as anything else.

+++ question | What ERASE ALL really takes
--- ask
A drawing has four layers. `SURVEY` is off and holds 40 objects. `CONTOURS` is frozen and holds 25. `GRID` is locked and holds 10. `SKETCH` is on, thawed and unlocked, and holds 100. You run `ERASE`, type `ALL` and press Enter. How many objects are erased?
--- answer
140
--- why
Select All picks up objects on layers that are off, so the 40 hidden survey objects are erased along with the 100 on `SKETCH`: $100 + 40 = 140$. Frozen layers are set aside and Select All does not pick them up. Objects on a locked layer cannot be edited, so they are not erased either. Turning a layer off is not protection.

+++ question | Hiding the layer you are on
--- ask
`S-STEL` is the current layer. You try two things in turn: freeze `S-STEL`, and then turn `S-STEL` off. What happens?
--- choice
Both work; AutoCAD makes layer 0 current by itself.
--- choice correct
The freeze is refused, because new objects go on the current layer. Turning it off works, with a warning, and anything you draw next is invisible.
--- choice
Both are refused until you make another layer current.
--- choice
The freeze works, and the off is refused because a frozen layer cannot also be off.
--- why
A frozen layer is skipped entirely, and new objects are drawn onto the current layer, so AutoCAD will not freeze it. Off is allowed with a warning, which sets a trap: you keep drawing onto a layer you cannot see. Make another layer current first, then freeze or turn off the one you want out of the way.

+++ question | Construction lines on the printout
--- ask
The layer `G-CONS` is set to No Plot, yet the gray construction lines appeared on the printed sheet. You click one. The Properties palette shows Layer: 0, Color: 8. What happened, and what is the fix?
--- choice
The No Plot setting only works on layouts, not in model space; move the lines to paper space.
--- choice
The plot style table printed color 8 as black; switch to a different table.
--- choice correct
The lines were drawn on layer 0, which plots, and colored gray with an override so they only looked like `G-CONS`. Move them to `G-CONS`, for example with MATCHPROP from a real `G-CONS` object, and reset the color with `SETBYLAYER`.
--- choice
Layer 0 can never be set to No Plot, so the drawing must be started again from the template.
--- why
Whether something prints is set by its layer's Plot setting, and these objects were never on `G-CONS`. Someone drew them with layer 0 current and forced the color to 8, so on screen they looked like construction geometry. Checking Properties, not the color, is how you catch it. Once the lines are on `G-CONS` with color ByLayer, they stop printing and they follow the layer.

+++ question | One sheet, three needs
--- ask
One sheet has two viewports of the same plan. You need all of these at once:

1. The electrical layer `E-COND` shows only in viewport 2.
2. The old survey is hidden everywhere, and a careless `ERASE ALL` must not reach it.
3. Construction geometry shows on screen but never prints.

Which set-up does it?
--- choice
Turn `E-COND` off in viewport 1; turn the survey off; set the construction layer to No Plot.
--- choice correct
Freeze `E-COND` in viewport 1 only (viewport freeze); freeze the survey; set the construction layer to No Plot.
--- choice
Freeze `E-COND` everywhere; lock the survey; turn the construction layer off.
--- choice
Freeze `E-COND` in viewport 1 only; turn the survey off; freeze the construction layer.
--- why
Only freeze can be set per viewport; off is one switch for the whole drawing, so "off in viewport 1" does not exist. Freezing the survey hides it and keeps it out of Select All, which off does not do. Locking would stop the erase but leave it visible. No Plot is the setting for "visible on screen, not on paper"; turning the layer off or freezing it would hide it on screen too.

=== cad03-l04 | Practice: Blocks, attributes and external references
--- for
l04-blocks-and-xrefs

+++ question | Counting a mixed drawing
--- ask
A harness drawing uses a connector block whose definition holds 18 objects. There are 45 references to it. Someone also exploded 6 more copies of the connector to tweak them. How many objects does the drawing hold for connectors in total?
--- answer
171
--- why
The definition is stored once: 18 objects. Each reference is 1 object: 45. Each exploded copy is 18 loose objects again: $6 \times 18 = 108$. Total $18 + 45 + 108 = 171$. The 6 exploded copies are also cut off from the definition, so a later BEDIT fix will not reach them.

+++ question | Where the rotated symbol lands
--- ask
A sensor symbol is defined as a block whose base point is its bottom-left corner. The symbol's center sits 10 to the right of and 5 above that corner. You insert it at `300,200` with a scale of 1 and a rotation of 90. Where does the symbol's center land? Type it as x,y.
--- answer
295,210
(295,210)
295, 210
(295, 210)
--- why
The base point lands on the insertion point, $(300, 200)$, and rotation turns the block about that point. Turning the offset $(10, 5)$ by 90° counterclockwise gives $(-5, 10)$: what was "right" now points up, and what was "up" now points left. So the center is at $(300 - 5,\; 200 + 10) = (295, 210)$. The base point is the pivot for rotation and scale as well as the handle.

+++ question | Three parts, three colors
--- ask
A valve block holds three kinds of geometry:

- the body, drawn on layer 0 with color ByLayer;
- the handle, drawn on layer 0 with color ByBlock;
- the tag text, drawn on the layer `TEXT`, which is white.

A reference is inserted on the layer `VALVES-LOX`, which is blue, and the reference's own Color property is set to red. What colors show?
--- choice
All three blue, because the reference is on a blue layer.
--- choice
All three red, because the reference's own color is red.
--- choice correct
Body blue, handle red, tag text white.
--- choice
Body red, handle blue, tag text white.
--- why
Layer 0 geometry takes on the reference's layer, `VALVES-LOX`, and ByLayer then means that layer's color: blue. ByBlock means "take the property from the reference itself", and the reference is set to red, so the handle is red. The tag text sits on a named layer, `TEXT`, and keeps it wherever the block goes: white.

+++ question | A new default for an old tag
--- ask
A title block's `DRAWN_BY` attribute has the default `TBD`. Forty sheets already have the block, each with a name typed in. You open the definition in the Block Editor and change the default to `JLEE`. What happens to the forty sheets?
--- choice
Every sheet now reads `JLEE`, because the references update from the definition.
--- choice
Every sheet that still read `TBD` switches to `JLEE`; the others keep their names.
--- choice correct
Nothing changes on them. Each reference stores its own value; the default only applies to new insertions.
--- choice
The forty references lose their values and go blank until ATTSYNC is run.
--- why
An attribute's value belongs to each reference, not to the definition. The default is only the answer offered at insertion time. Changing geometry in the definition updates every reference, but it does not rewrite values people typed. To change the names you edit each reference's value, for example with the Enhanced Attribute Editor (EATTEDIT).

+++ question | The site plan that went missing
--- ask
The host drawing is `P:\Pad39\Elec\E-101.dwg`. It attaches the site plan with the relative path `..\Site\SITE-PLAN.dwg`. Two things happen:

1. The whole `Pad39` folder is copied to `Q:\Archive\Pad39\`, and `E-101.dwg` is opened there.
2. Separately, someone emails only `E-101.dwg` to a contractor.

Where does the site plan show?
--- choice
In neither: moving or sending a drawing always breaks its xrefs.
--- choice correct
It shows in the archive copy, because the relative path still finds `..\Site` next to `Elec`. The contractor sees it Not Found, because the site plan file was not sent. ETRANSMIT would have packed it.
--- choice
It shows for the contractor, because the xref is stored inside `E-101.dwg`, but not in the archive.
--- choice
It shows in both, because AutoCAD keeps a copy of every xref in the host drawing.
--- why
An xref is only a pointer: the host stores the path, not the geometry. A relative path means "go up one folder, then into Site", and that is still true after the whole project folder moves together. An email with only the host carries no site plan at all, so the pointer finds nothing. ETRANSMIT gathers the drawing, its xrefs, fonts and plot style files into one package for exactly this reason.

+++ question | Which way the site plan travels
--- ask
Four drawings: the site plan `S`, the piping plan `P`, the electrical plan `E`, and a master sheet `M`.

- `P` attaches `S`.
- `E` overlays `S` and attaches `P`.
- `M` attaches `E`.

Does `S` appear in `M`, and if so, how many times and by which route?
--- choice
No: `E` overlaid `S`, so `S` stays behind.
--- choice correct
Yes, once, through `P`: `P` attached `S`, so `S` travels inside `P`, and `P` travels inside `E`.
--- choice
Yes, twice: once through `E` and once through `P`.
--- choice
Yes, once, through `E`, because `M` attached `E`.
--- why
Follow each link. `M` attaches `E`, so `E` comes in. `E` attached `P`, so `P` travels along into `M`. `P` attached `S`, so `S` travels along with `P`. `E`'s own link to `S` is an overlay, and an overlay stays behind when its host is referenced, so it adds nothing to `M`. The site plan arrives exactly once, by the only route made of attaches.

=== cad03-l05 | Practice: Annotation, paper space and plotting
--- for
l05-annotation-and-paper-space

+++ question | A pad on the sheet
--- ask
A concrete pad is 36 m long in model space, drawn at full size in millimeters. It is shown in a viewport set to 1:200. How long is it on the printed sheet, in millimeters?
--- answer
180
180 mm
180mm
--- why
36 m is $36\,000$ mm in the model. A 1:200 viewport has a scale factor of $1/200 = 0.005$, paper over model. So the pad prints $36\,000 \times 0.005 = 180$ mm long. The layout itself is plotted at 1:1; the viewport carries the 1:200.

+++ question | Working out a viewport's scale
--- ask
A plotted sheet has no scale written on it. A frame member that is 2.4 m long in the model measures 48 mm on the paper. At what standard scale was the viewport set? Type it as 1:n.
--- answer
1:50
1/50
1 : 50
--- why
The scale factor is paper over model: $48 / 2400 = 0.02$, which is $1/50$. So the viewport is 1:50, one of the standard series. Checking one known length like this is how you confirm a sheet is really to scale, for example after someone may have zoomed an unlocked viewport.

+++ question | Two copies of one note
--- ask
A sheet has a 1:20 general view and a 1:5 detail view of the same model. Your office does not use annotative text, so each note is drawn twice in model space, once for each scale, on its own layer: `NOTES-20` and `NOTES-5`. Every note must print 2.5 mm tall. Which set-up is right?
--- choice
`NOTES-20` text is 12.5 mm tall and `NOTES-5` text is 50 mm tall. Freeze `NOTES-5` in the 1:5 viewport and `NOTES-20` in the 1:20 viewport.
--- choice correct
`NOTES-20` text is 50 mm tall and `NOTES-5` text is 12.5 mm tall. Freeze `NOTES-5` in the 1:20 viewport and `NOTES-20` in the 1:5 viewport.
--- choice
Both layers use 2.5 mm text, because the viewport scales text to the right size by itself.
--- choice
`NOTES-20` text is 50 mm tall and `NOTES-5` text is 12.5 mm tall. Turn `NOTES-5` off in the 1:20 viewport and `NOTES-20` off in the 1:5 viewport.
--- why
Non-annotative model text must be drawn at printed height times the scale's second number: $2.5 \times 20 = 50$ mm for the 1:20 view and $2.5 \times 5 = 12.5$ mm for the 1:5 view. Each viewport must hide the copy made for the other scale, and hiding in one viewport only is viewport freeze; off has no per-viewport form. This bookkeeping is what annotative objects replace: one note, a paper height of 2.5 mm, and both scales on its list.

+++ question | Gray lines that printed anyway
--- ask
Construction lines sit on the layer `G-CONS`, color 8 (gray). The layout plots with `monochrome.ctb`. The team expected the plot style table to leave the gray lines off the sheet, but they printed in black. Why, and what is the fix?
--- choice
`monochrome.ctb` prints every color as black, so no layer can ever be kept off the sheet with it. Switch to an STB table.
--- choice correct
A plot style table decides how an object prints, such as its color and lineweight, not whether its layer prints. `G-CONS` still has Plot switched on. Set `G-CONS` to No Plot.
--- choice
Color 8 is not in a CTB table, so AutoCAD printed it with the default settings. Change the lines to color 7.
--- choice
The layout should be plotted at 1:20 so the construction lines become too thin to print.
--- why
A CTB table maps each color to print settings: `monochrome.ctb` turns every color black. Whether a layer reaches the paper at all is the layer's own Plot or No Plot setting, which is still on. The fix is one click in the Layer Properties Manager. Plotting at another scale would not help either: a layout normally plots at 1:1, and its viewports carry the scale.

+++ question | Dimension text five times too big
--- ask
A non-annotative dimension style has a text height of 2.5 mm and an overall scale, DIMSCALE, of 100. Its dimensions are shown in a 1:20 viewport, and the text prints 12.5 mm tall. What is wrong, and what should DIMSCALE be?
--- choice
The text height should be 0.5 mm; DIMSCALE is right.
--- choice
DIMSCALE should be 1, because the viewport already scales everything.
--- choice correct
DIMSCALE was set for a 1:100 view. The text is $2.5 \times 100 = 250$ mm in the model and prints $250 \times 0.05 = 12.5$ mm. For a 1:20 viewport, DIMSCALE must be 20.
--- choice
DIMSCALE should be 0.05, the viewport's scale factor.
--- why
For non-annotative dimensions, DIMSCALE multiplies every size in the style, and it must equal the viewport scale's second number. With 20, the model text is $2.5 \times 20 = 50$ mm, and the 1:20 viewport shrinks it by $0.05$ to $2.5$ mm. With DIMSCALE 1, the text would print $2.5 \times 0.05 = 0.125$ mm, a speck. Setting DIMSCALE to the scale factor, 0.05, would be smaller still.

+++ question | A cart and its wheel bracket on one A3
--- ask
An A3 layout leaves a space about 380 mm by 250 mm for the main view, and a space 120 mm by 90 mm for a detail. The main view shows a ground support cart 3200 mm long and 1900 mm tall. The detail shows a wheel bracket 180 mm by 120 mm. Using the largest standard scale that fits each space, and non-annotative notes that must print 2.5 mm tall, which is right?
--- choice correct
Main view 1:10, detail 1:2. Notes in the main view are drawn 25 mm tall, in the detail 5 mm tall.
--- choice
Main view 1:5, detail 1:1. Notes 12.5 mm and 2.5 mm.
--- choice
Main view 1:10, detail 1:2. Notes 0.25 mm and 1.25 mm.
--- choice
Main view 1:20, detail 1:2. Notes 50 mm and 5 mm.
--- why

=== cad03-l07 | Practice: Parametric sketches and constraints
--- for

+++ question | Freedom before any rule
--- ask
A sketch holds two arcs, four line segments and one point, with no constraints yet. How many degrees of freedom does it have?
--- answer
28
--- why
An arc has 5 (center $x$ and $y$, radius, start angle, end angle), a line segment 4 (two endpoints of 2 each), and a point 2. So $2 \times 5 + 4 \times 4 + 2 = 10 + 16 + 2 = 28$.

+++ question | A slot, nearly there
--- ask
You sketch a slot: two straight lines and two arcs, joined end to end. You add:

- four point-on-point coincidents where each line meets an arc;
- four tangents, one at each of those joins;
- an equal constraint between the two arcs;
- a horizontal constraint on one line.

How many degrees of freedom are left?
--- answer
4
--- why
Start: $2 \times 4 + 2 \times 5 = 18$. The four coincidents remove $4 \times 2 = 8$, leaving 10. The four tangents remove 4, leaving 6: two arc centers (4) and two radii (2), because lines tangent to both arcs are then set. Equal removes 1 and horizontal removes 1, leaving 4. They are the slot's position ($x$ and $y$), the distance between the arc centers, and the radius. Two dimensions and one coincident of an arc center on the origin would finish it.

+++ question | The slot that stays centered
--- ask
A plate sketch is 100 mm wide and centered on the origin, so the vertical axis through the origin runs down the plate's middle. A slot in it must stay centered left to right when the plate width changes, and its width must stay 12 mm. Which set of rules records that intent?
--- choice
Dimension the slot's left side 44 mm from the plate's left edge, and its right side 44 mm from the plate's right edge.
--- choice
Fix the slot where it is, so nothing can move it.
--- choice correct
Make the slot's two sides symmetric about the vertical axis through the origin, and give one 12 mm dimension between them.
--- choice
Dimension the slot's left side 44 mm from the plate's left edge, and give one 12 mm dimension between the sides.
--- why
A symmetric constraint writes "centered" into the model, and one 12 mm dimension writes the width. The first choice fixes the edge distances instead: widen the plate and the slot itself grows, because both 44 mm distances hold. The fourth is centered today only because $44 + 6 = 50$ is half of 100; widen the plate to 120 and the slot follows the left edge, 10 mm off center. Fix records no reason at all, so nothing follows a change. In AutoCAD you would have redrawn the slot; here the rules move it.

+++ question | Zero on paper, two in truth
--- ask
A rectangle sketch has four point-on-point corner coincidents, horizontal on top and bottom, vertical on both sides, a width, a height, and an equal constraint between the top and bottom edges. It is not tied to the origin. Subtracting every constraint from the start gives $16 - 8 - 4 - 2 - 1 = 1$. How many degrees of freedom does the sketch really have?
--- answer
2
--- why
Horizontal and vertical sides already force the top and bottom to be equal, so the equal constraint is redundant and removes nothing. Without it: $16 - 8 - 4 - 2 = 2$, the rectangle's $x$ and $y$ position. The arithmetic only counts independent constraints; a redundant one makes the count look better than the sketch is. The software flags the equal constraint, and dragging the rectangle shows it still slides.

+++ question | Fifteen, forty-five, sixty
--- ask
A plate sketch is 60 mm wide. The hole is dimensioned 15 mm from the left edge and 45 mm from the right edge. Everything else is fully defined. What is the state of the sketch, and what happens when the width is changed to 80 mm?
--- choice
It is fully defined; at 80 mm the hole moves to stay in the middle.
--- choice correct
It is over-defined: the three numbers repeat one fact, since $15 + 45 = 60$. At 80 mm they conflict, because no position is both 15 from the left and 45 from the right. Delete the hole dimension that carries no intent.
--- choice
It is under-defined, because a hole needs three dimensions.
--- choice
It is fully defined; at 80 mm the solver keeps 15 and 45 and quietly ignores the width.
--- why
The width fixes the right edge, and the 15 mm dimension then fixes the hole's $x$, so the 45 mm dimension has no freedom left to remove: it is redundant. While the numbers agree it may only be flagged; once they disagree, no shape satisfies all three, and the solver reports a conflict. Keep the dimension that matches the design's reason, for example the 15 mm from the edge the part locates on, and delete the other, or turn it into a reference dimension.

+++ question | Which set over-defines the gusset
--- ask
A right-triangle gusset sketch has three lines with coincident corners, a horizontal base, a vertical upright, and the right-angle corner on the origin. That leaves 2 degrees of freedom. Which set of dimensions makes the sketch over-defined?
--- choice
The base, 40 mm, and the height, 30 mm.
--- choice
The base, 40 mm, and the hypotenuse, 50 mm.
--- choice
The hypotenuse, 50 mm, and the angle between the base and the hypotenuse, 36.87°.
--- choice correct
The base, 40 mm, the height, 30 mm, and the angle between the base and the hypotenuse, 36.87°.
--- why
Two degrees of freedom are left: the two leg lengths. Any two independent dimensions settle them, so the first three choices each fully define the sketch. The last adds a third number when base and height already fix the angle, $\arctan(30 / 40) = 36.87^\circ$. +++ question | One wrong pairing

+++ question | The most draft that still fits
--- ask
A molded cover has side walls 30 mm tall. A board inside means the top of each wall may be at most 0.5 mm further in than the bottom. What is the largest draft angle you can use, in degrees, to two decimals?
--- answer
0.95
0.955
--- why
The top edge moves in by $h\tan\theta$. Set $30\tan\theta = 0.5$, so $\tan\theta = 0.5 / 30 = 0.01667$ and $\theta = \arctan(0.01667) \approx 0.95^\circ$. +++ question | Two more holes in the pattern

+++ question | An avionics box the weight of a ship
--- ask
A spreadsheet takes a shelled housing's volume from CAD, $42\,752\ \mathrm{mm^3}$, multiplies by aluminum's density, $2700\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, and reports a mass of $115\,430\,400$ kg. What went wrong?

--- choice correct

--- choice

--- choice

$m = \rho V$ only works when the units match. +++ question | The bolt that will not go through
--- ask
A bracket must bolt to a panel with an M6 bolt that passes through the bracket and screws into the panel. The modeler used the hole wizard on the bracket and picked "Tapped, M6". What is wrong?

--- choice correct

+++ question | A titanium flange ring
--- ask
A flange ring is made by revolving a rectangle 10 mm wide in the radial direction and 8 mm tall, whose center sits 45 mm from the axis, through 360°. It is titanium alloy, $4430\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$. What is its mass, in grams, to one decimal?

100.2
100.2 g

+++ question | Whose child is Sketch 4
--- ask
In a bracket's tree, Sketch 4 is drawn on the top face of Extrude 2 and has one dimension to an edge made by Fillet 1. Fillet 1 rounds the edge where Extrude 2 meets Extrude 1. Which features must Sketch 4 wait for, directly or through a chain, before it can rebuild?

--- choice
Only Fillet 1, because that is the feature just above it.
--- choice correct
Extrude 2 and Fillet 1 directly, and Extrude 1 through Fillet 1.
--- choice

--- why

+++ question | A reorder the software allows

A plate carries a row of holes that must match a star tracker's bolt pattern, 70 mm apart, centered on the plate. Today the plate is 100 mm wide. Which way of modeling the holes captures the intent?

+++ question | Thinner, then thicker
--- ask
Through-holes in a plate were cut Blind 10 mm, because the plate was 10 mm thick. Later the plate is changed to 7 mm, and later still to 12 mm. What does each change do to the holes?

+++ question | The sketch that jumped

=== cad03-l10 | Practice: Assemblies, mates and clash checks
--- for

+++ question | Flat on the panel, on a pin
--- ask
A sensor block is mated face to face with a panel (coincident), then a round pin on the block, whose axis is at right angles to that face, is made concentric with a hole in the panel. How many degrees of freedom does the block have left?
--- answer
1
--- why
Start at 6. The coincident removes 3: movement off the panel and the two tilts. Left: sliding two ways on the panel and spinning about the panel's normal. The pin's axis is that normal, so the concentric mate's tilts are already gone and it only removes the two slides. Left: $3 - 2 = 1$, spinning about the pin. A second pin, or an angle mate, would lock it.

+++ question | A carriage on a rail
--- ask
A carriage sits on a straight rail. Its bottom face is coincident with the rail's top face, and its side face is coincident with the rail's side face, which is at right angles to the top. How many degrees of freedom does the carriage have left?
--- answer
1
--- why
The first coincident removes 3, leaving the two slides across the top and the spin about the vertical. The second face is vertical, so of the three things a coincident would normally remove, only two are still free: the slide towards the side face and the spin. Left: $3 - 2 = 1$, sliding along the rail, which is what a carriage should do. A distance mate from the rail's end would take that last one if a fixed position is wanted.

+++ question | Four parts, counted
--- ask
An assembly has four parts. The panel is grounded. The bracket is coincident with the panel and concentric with it at two holes. The reaction wheel is concentric with the bracket's bore and coincident with its shoulder. A cover is coincident with a face of the bracket and concentric with one bolt hole in it. How many free degrees of freedom does the assembly have?
--- answer
2
--- why
Panel: 0. Bracket: $6 - 3 - 2 - 1 = 0$. Wheel: $6 - 4 - 1 = 1$, the spin about its shaft, which is intended. Cover: the coincident removes 3, and the concentric on a bolt hole at right angles to that face removes only the 2 slides, leaving the spin about that bolt: 1. Total $0 + 0 + 1 + 1 = 2$. The cover's spin is not intended: it needs a second concentric on another hole. Counting is how you find loose parts like that.

+++ question | A third hole
--- ask
The bracket above is already at 0 degrees of freedom: coincident with the panel and concentric at holes A and B. Someone adds a third concentric mate at hole C. What happens in each case?

- Case 2: hole C is 0.1 mm off.
--- choice
Case 1: the bracket loses 4 more degrees of freedom. Case 2: the solver moves hole C to fit.
--- choice correct
Case 1: the mate is redundant and removes nothing; many tools flag it. Case 2: the mates conflict, the assembly is over-defined, and the offset is a real message that the parts do not fit as drawn.
--- choice
Both cases solve silently, because concentric mates only matter for the first hole.
--- choice
Case 1: the assembly is over-defined and cannot be saved. Case 2: the extra mate is ignored.
--- why
With every degree of freedom already gone, a new mate can only repeat what other mates say. If the geometry agrees, it is redundant. If it does not, no position satisfies all the mates, and the solver reports a conflict. Delete the extra mate, or find the wrong hole position; do not suppress mates until the red goes away.

+++ question | Three parts in a loop
--- ask
Three parts were all designed in context. The cover's cut-out was sketched on the bracket's edges. The bracket's holes were snapped to the panel's holes. --- choice correct

--- choice

A skeleton model holds only the shared interface, so every part points one way, at it, and never at each other. In-context design is useful; loops and unmanaged references are the danger.

+++ question | The harness near the hot line

A harness 12 mm across runs past a hot line 16 mm across. In the model their center lines are 30 mm apart. The rule says the gap between their surfaces must be at least 10 mm. Total mass $2 + 3 + 5 = 10$ kg. In $x$: $(2 \times 0 + 3 \times 0.4 + 5 \times 0.2) / 10 = (1.2 + 1.0) / 10 = 0.22$ m. In $y$: $(2 \times 0 + 3 \times 0 + 5 \times 0.3) / 10 = 1.5 / 10 = 0.15$ m. Each direction is its own weighted average.

+++ question | Which way a plate is hardest to spin
--- ask
A steel plate ($7850\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$) is 0.4 m along $x$, 0.2 m along $y$ and 0.01 m thick along $z$. About its own center, which is right?
--- choice
$I_{xx}$ is largest, because the plate is longest along $x$.
--- choice correct
$I_{zz} \approx 0.105\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$ is largest, $I_{yy} \approx 0.084$, and $I_{xx} \approx 0.021$ is smallest.
--- choice
All three are equal, because the plate is one solid piece of one material.
--- choice
$I_{zz} \approx 0.0010\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$ is smallest, because the plate is thinnest along $z$.
--- why
Mass: $7850 \times 0.4 \times 0.2 \times 0.01 = 6.28$ kg. Each moment uses the two sides at right angles to its axis: $I_{zz} = \frac{6.28}{12}(0.4^2 + 0.2^2) = 0.105$, $I_{yy} = \frac{6.28}{12}(0.4^2 + 0.01^2) = 0.084$, $I_{xx} = \frac{6.28}{12}(0.2^2 + 0.01^2) = 0.021$. Spinning about $z$, the axis through the thin direction, swings all the mass far out, so it is hardest. The long side makes spin about $x$ easy, not hard.

+++ question | The harness nobody modeled

A vehicle's CAD model gives 400 kg with the CG at $x = 1.20$ m. A 12 kg harness was never modeled; its own CG is at $x = 2.10$ m. With it added, where is the CG along $x$, in meters to three decimals?

+++ question | A product of inertia from two masses
--- ask
Two 1 kg point masses sit at $(1, 1, 0)$ and $(-1, -1, 0)$ in meters. Using the tensor convention, $I_{xy} = -\sum m\,x\,y$, what is $I_{xy}$ in $\mathrm{kg\,m^2}$?
--- answer
-2
−2
-2 kg m^2
-2.0
--- why
Each mass has $x\,y = 1 \times 1 = 1$ or $(-1) \times (-1) = 1$, so $\sum m\,x\,y = 2$ and $I_{xy} = -2$. The mass lies along the line where $x$ and $y$ have the same sign, so the product is not zero. Add two more 1 kg masses at $(-1, 1, 0)$ and $(1, -1, 0)$ and their $x\,y$ values are $-1$ each, so the sum becomes 0: balanced across the axes. A tool that lists $+\sum m\,x\,y$ would print $+2$ for the same part.

+++ question | A tensor about the wrong point
--- ask
This function should return the inertia tensor about the assembly CG. One line is wrong.

```python
import numpy as np

def inertia_about_cg(m, centres, own):
    # m: masses; centres: part centres (x, y, z); own: each part's tensor about its own centre
    M = m.sum()
    cg = (m[:, None] * centres).sum(axis=0) / M
    I = np.zeros((3, 3))
    for mi, ci, Ii in zip(m, centres, own):
        d = ci
        I += Ii + mi * ((d @ d) * np.eye(3) - np.outer(d, d))
    return cg, I
```

What does it compute instead, and what is the fix?
--- choice
It computes the right tensor; the CG is only returned for reference.
--- choice correct
It shifts every part to the model origin, so it returns the tensor about the origin, which is larger than the one about the CG unless the CG is at the origin. The line should be `d = ci - cg`.
--- choice
It adds tensors about different points, so NumPy raises an error.
--- choice
It computes the principal moments instead of the tensor. It should call `np.linalg.eigvalsh`.
--- why
The parallel-axis shift needs the vector from the new reference point to each part's center. With `d = ci`, that point is the origin. The result is a valid tensor, but about the wrong point, and nothing fails. The tensor about the CG is the smallest one, so a simulator given this one would turn the vehicle too slowly for a given torque.

+++ question | Which export cannot be real
--- ask
Four CAD reports give the principal moments of a part at its CG, in $\mathrm{kg\,mm^2}$. Which one must contain an error?
--- choice
$48\,000$, $61\,000$, $77\,000$
--- choice
$20\,000$, $95\,000$, $105\,000$
--- choice correct
$20\,000$, $61\,000$, $104\,000$
--- choice
$61\,000$, $61\,000$, $61\,000$
--- why
No principal moment can be bigger than the other two added together. In the third report, $20\,000 + 61\,000 = 81\,000$, which is less than $104\,000$, so it is impossible. The second is a thin, flat part: $20\,000 + 95\,000 = 115\,000 \ge 105\,000$. Three equal moments are what a cube or a sphere gives. In the simulator's units, $1\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2} = 10^6\ \mathrm{kg\,mm^2}$, so the first report is $0.048$, $0.061$ and $0.077\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$.

+++ question | A thin bracket's bend
--- ask
A 90° bend is made in 1.6 mm aluminum sheet with an inside radius of 1.6 mm. The shop's K-factor is 0.42. What is the bend allowance, in millimeters, to two decimals?
--- answer

3.57 mm
--- why
The angle in radians is $\pi / 2 = 1.5708$. The neutral radius is $R + KT = 1.6 + 0.42 \times 1.6 = 1.6 + 0.672 = 2.272$ mm. $BA = 1.5708 \times 2.272 = 3.57$ mm. +++ question | The K-factor from a test piece
--- ask
The shop bends a test L-bracket from 2 mm sheet with an inside radius of 2 mm and one 90° bend. Its outside legs are 50 mm and 30 mm. The flat blank they cut to get it was 76.52 mm long. What K-factor does that imply, to two decimals?
--- answer
0.44
--- why
Each straight part of a leg ends $R + T = 4$ mm short of the outside corner, so the flat flanges are $50 - 4 = 46$ and $30 - 4 = 26$ mm, together 72 mm. The bend used the rest: $BA = 76.52 - 72 = 4.52$ mm. Then $R + KT = 4.52 / 1.5708 = 2.8775$, so $KT = 0.8775$ and $K = 0.8775 / 2 = 0.44$. +++ question | What moved on the drawing

A bracket's drawing was made from its model. Two things change in the model: its material goes from aluminum to titanium, and a sharp corner is replaced by a fillet. On the sheet, which is right?

+++ question | A crease in the reflection
--- ask
A surfaced nose fairing shows a faint line in its reflections along one seam. The two surfaces there meet with no gap and with the same slope. What is going on?
--- choice
The surfaces do not touch; the seam is G0 at best.
--- choice
The model is a solid, so reflections are drawn wrongly on it.
--- choice correct
The seam is G1 but not G2: slope matches but curvature jumps, and reflections show that as a crease. Aero skins often need G2.
--- choice
The surfaces overlap, so the software is drawing both of them.

G0 means the surfaces touch, G1 means they also share a slope, and G2 means they also share curvature. Light reflects according to curvature, so a jump in it shows as a crease even when the slopes match. An aerodynamic skin that becomes part of the OML usually wants at least G1 and often G2.

+++ question | Tolerances the inspection program cannot read

+++ question | Reviewing the whole vehicle on a laptop
--- ask
A program manager wants to walk through a 20,000-part vehicle assembly on a laptop during a review, with the assembly tree visible. Nobody will edit anything. Which format fits best?
--- choice
IGES, because it is the oldest and so the most widely read.

+++ question | What each file carries
--- ask
Which one statement is true?
--- choice
A STEP AP214 file carries semantic PMI, so a CMM program can read its tolerances.
--- choice
A Parasolid `.x_t` file from NX carries the feature tree into SolidWorks, because both use the same kernel.

+++ question | Centimeters read as millimeters
--- ask
A model saved in centimeters travels in a format with no units, and the receiver's software reads the numbers as millimeters. By what factor are the imported part's moments of inertia too small?
--- answer
100000
100 000
1e5
10^5
--- why
Every length is read 10 times too small, because 1 cm is 10 mm. Volume, and so mass, goes with length cubed: $10^3$ too small. A moment of inertia is mass times distance squared: $10^3 \times 10^2 = 10^5$ too small. A simulated vehicle with inertia a hundred thousand times too small would spin up absurdly fast on the first thruster pulse.

+++ question | Counting open edges
--- ask
A bracket comes from a program whose modeling tolerance is $0.005$ mm. Five of its edges have tiny gaps: $0.0003$, $0.001$, $0.0045$, $0.0002$ and $0.0049$ mm. It is imported into a program whose tolerance is $0.0005$ mm. How many open edges does the receiver see?
--- answer
3
--- why
At home every gap is smaller than $0.005$, so the bracket is a valid solid. The receiver only counts a gap as closed if it is smaller than $0.0005$: $0.0003$ and $0.0002$ pass, while $0.001$, $0.0045$ and $0.0049$ do not. That is 3 open edges, so the bracket arrives as an open shell with no volume. Healing with the sender's tolerance, $0.005$ mm, would close all five; then compare the volume and CG with the sender's numbers.

+++ question | A face that moved alone
--- ask
A supplier received a housing as a STEP file. To enlarge a boss, they used a direct editing tool to push the boss's outer face out 2 mm. The hole in the middle of the boss did not move, and the wall on one side is now 2 mm thicker than on the other. What went wrong?
--- choice
The STEP file was corrupt; a clean STEP file would have kept the hole centered.
--- choice correct
STEP carries a dumb solid: the faces, with no rule that the hole is concentric with the boss. Direct editing moved one face and nothing followed it. The originator should make the change in the native model, where the concentric rule lives.
--- choice

--- choice

A neutral file has the shape without the reasons. === cad03-l14 | Practice: Part numbers, revisions and which version is real

+++ question | Saves and releases
--- ask
A designer saves a bracket model 12 times and it is released as revision B. Later an approved change calls for a fix; the designer saves 5 more times, and the fix is released as revision C. Which is right?
--- choice
17 revisions exist, one per save; the shop builds from the newest one.
--- choice correct
There are 17 versions and 2 revisions, B and C. New hardware is built to C once it is effective; nobody builds from the in-between versions.
--- choice
There are 2 versions and 17 revisions.
--- choice
There are 17 versions and 1 revision, because B was overwritten by C.
--- why
A version is any saved state; a revision is a released, controlled configuration with a letter. B stays in the record, frozen: hardware built to it is still correctly described by it. Versions are the designer's working history. Which revision applies to which unit is set by effectivity, not by which revision is newest.

--- ask
A build of 20 spacecraft, serials 001 to 020, uses bracket `40221`. Revision B is effective for serials 001 to 007, revision C from serial 008, and revision D from serial 015. Nothing is swapped. How many spacecraft carry revision C?

7
--- why
Revision C runs from serial 008 up to the last serial before D takes over, 014. That is serials 008 to 014: $14 - 8 + 1 = 7$ units. - two point-on-point coincidents where the arc meets each line;
- two tangents, one at each of those joins;
- horizontal on L1 and vertical on L2;
- the arc's radius, L1's length and L2's length;
- the free end of L1 on the $x$ axis (point on a line).

Then you add a perpendicular constraint between L1 and L2. What is the state of the sketch?
--- choice
Fully defined: the perpendicular removed the last degree of freedom.
--- choice correct
Still under-defined by 1, the slide along the $x$ axis, and the perpendicular is flagged as redundant.
--- choice
Over-defined with a conflict: a perpendicular cannot be added to lines joined by an arc.
--- choice
Under-defined by 2, because a point on a line removes nothing.
--- why
Start: $4 + 5 + 4 = 13$. Coincidents $-4$ leave 9; tangents $-2$ leave 7; horizontal and vertical $-2$ leave 5: position (2), radius, and the two lengths. Three dimensions leave 2, and the point on the axis leaves 1: sliding along $x$. The perpendicular adds nothing, because a horizontal L1 and a vertical L2 are already at right angles. Plain subtraction reaches 0 and hides the free slide. A coincident of L1's end with the origin, instead of the perpendicular, would finish the sketch.

+++ question | Which choices break silently
--- ask
A bracket plate is 8 mm thick and 100 mm wide. A reaction wheel bolts to two holes 70 mm apart. The model was built with these four choices:

1. The holes are symmetric about the Right plane, with one 70 mm dimension between them.
2. The holes are cut Blind 8 mm.
3. A row of cable-tie holes is spaced by the equation W − 30, where W is the plate width, because the harness clamp holes are 70 mm apart.
4. The upright is extruded up to a datum plane 40 mm above the Top plane.

The plate is changed to 10 mm thick and 120 mm wide. Which choices rebuild with no error into a part that no longer does its job?
--- choice
1 and 4
--- choice
2 only
--- choice correct
2 and 3
--- choice
3 and 4
--- why
Choice 2: the holes stay 8 mm deep in a 10 mm plate, so 2 mm of metal is left under them. Choice 3: $120 - 30 = 90$ mm, so the cable-tie holes no longer match the clamp's 70 mm. Neither turns red. Choice 1 keeps the wheel holes 70 mm apart and centered, and choice 4 keeps the upright's top at 40 mm whatever the plate does: both say what the design needs. "Through all" and a plain 70 mm dimension would have fixed 2 and 3.

+++ question | A gimbal, rigid and flexible
--- ask
An engine gimbal is built as a sub-assembly. Its frame is grounded. The gimbal ring is concentric with the frame's pivot axis and coincident with a thrust face on the frame, so it can only swing about that axis. The engine is concentric with the ring's second pivot axis, at right angles to the first, and coincident with a thrust face on the ring. The sub-assembly is then placed in the vehicle assembly with its frame fully mated. How many free degrees of freedom does the vehicle assembly show from the gimbal while the sub-assembly is rigid, and how many when it is set to flexible? Type the two numbers.
--- answer
0 and 2
0, 2
0 2
0,2
0 then 2
rigid 0, flexible 2
--- why
Inside: the ring has $6 - 4 - 1 = 1$, its swing, and the engine has $6 - 4 - 1 = 1$, its swing about the second axis, so the sub-assembly has 2, pitch and yaw. +++ question | Two parts about their CG
--- ask
Part A is 4 kg, with $I_{zz} = 0.02\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$ about its own center, which is at $x = 0$. Part B is 2 kg, with $I_{zz} = 0.01\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$ about its own center, which is at $x = 0.6$ m. Both centers lie on the $x$ axis. What is $I_{zz}$ of the pair about their combined CG?
--- choice
$0.75\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$
--- choice correct
$0.51\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$
--- choice
$0.48\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$
--- choice
$0.57\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$
--- why
CG: $(4 \times 0 + 2 \times 0.6) / 6 = 0.2$ m. Shift each part: A is 0.2 m away, adding $4 \times 0.2^2 = 0.16$; B is 0.4 m away, adding $2 \times 0.4^2 = 0.32$. Total $0.02 + 0.16 + 0.01 + 0.32 = 0.51$. The value about A's center is $0.03 + 2 \times 0.6^2 = 0.75$, and subtracting $6 \times 0.2^2 = 0.24$ gives 0.51 again. Leaving out the parts' own moments gives 0.48, and using the halfway point 0.3 m instead of the CG gives 0.57.

--- ask
A CAD tool reports, about the CG in body axes, in $\mathrm{kg\,mm^2}$: $I_{xx} = 2\,400\,000$, $I_{yy} = 3\,100\,000$, $I_{zz} = 3\,600\,000$, and products listed as $P_{xy} = 120\,000$, $P_{xz} = -40\,000$, $P_{yz} = 0$. --- choice correct
$I_{xx} = 2.4$, $I_{yy} = 3.1$, $I_{zz} = 3.6$, $I_{xy} = -0.12$, $I_{xz} = 0.04$, $I_{yz} = 0$.
--- choice
$I_{xx} = 2.4$, $I_{yy} = 3.1$, $I_{zz} = 3.6$, $I_{xy} = 0.12$, $I_{xz} = -0.04$, $I_{yz} = 0$.

+++ question | A U-channel blank
--- ask
A U-channel is bent from 2 mm sheet with two 90° bends. The inside radius is 3 mm and the shop's K-factor is 0.40. Measured to the sharp outside corners, the two side flanges are each 20 mm and the web between them is 40 mm. How long is the flat blank?
--- choice
80.00 mm
--- choice correct
71.94 mm
--- choice
69.42 mm
--- choice
76.94 mm
--- why
For a 90° bend, each straight part ends $R + T = 5$ mm short of the outside corner. Side flanges: $20 - 5 = 15$ mm each. The web loses 5 mm at each end: $40 - 10 = 30$ mm. Each bend: $BA = 1.5708 \times (3 + 0.40 \times 2) = 1.5708 \times 3.8 = 5.97$ mm. Blank $= 15 + 30 + 15 + 2 \times 5.97 = 71.94$ mm. Adding the outside lengths gives 80. +++ question | What to hand the aero team
--- ask
The aerodynamics team needs the stage's outer mold line to build a CFD mesh and to set the reference area. You have three files:

1. The native NX model of the propellant tank structure.
2. A JT file of the whole stage, with insulation and raceways, triangles only.
3. A STEP AP242 file of the stage's outer surfaces, including the sprayed insulation, raceways and fairing, as exact geometry.

Which should they get, and why?
--- choice
File 1, because the native model is the most accurate and carries its feature tree.
--- choice
File 2, because it includes the insulation and opens fast.
--- choice correct
File 3, because it is the true OML, protection and protrusions included, in exact geometry.
--- choice
Files 1 and 2 together, because the tank gives exact geometry and the JT adds the insulation.

+++ question | One wrong route
--- ask
Four jobs at a launch company, each with a tool and a way of sending it. Which one is wrong?

The pad's propellant-loading P&ID is drawn in AutoCAD, with valve blocks carrying tag attributes, and sent to the pad contractor as DWG.

A flight bracket modeled in NX goes to a machine shop that runs SolidWorks as a Parasolid `.x_t` file.

A build of 30 units, serials 001 to 030, uses bracket `40221`. Revision B is effective for serials 001 to 011, revision C from 012, and revision D from 024. --- answer
11

+++ question | Bigger holes in a released bracket
--- ask
Bracket `40017` revision C is released and flying on several spacecraft. A new wheel needs its two mounting holes changed from M5 clearance to M6 clearance, so M5 bolts would no longer fill them properly. Which way of making the change is right?

+++ question | The mass in the title block
--- ask
A bracket model has no material assigned, and the CAD tool quietly uses $1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$. The drawing's title block reads the model's mass property and shows 18.2 g. The bracket is really titanium alloy, $4430\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$. Once the material is assigned, what mass does the title block show, in grams, to one decimal?
--- answer
80.6
80.6 g
80.63
--- why
The volume is right; only the density was wrong. Mass is density times volume, so it scales by $4430 / 1000 = 4.43$: $18.2 \times 4.43 = 80.6$ g. The title block field is associative, so it updates by itself. The part's own CG does not move, but its moments of inertia also grow by 4.43, and so does its pull on the assembly's CG. A mass that looks precise is only as good as the material behind it.
