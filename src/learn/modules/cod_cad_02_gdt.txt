=== cad02-l01 | Practice: Why GD&T exists, and how to read a feature control frame
--- for
l01-why-gdt-exists

+++ question | A miss reported as a diameter
--- ask
A coordinate measuring machine finds a hole's center $0.07\,\mathrm{mm}$ to the right of its true position and $0.05\,\mathrm{mm}$ below it. GD&T reports the miss as the diameter of the smallest round zone, centered on true position, that still holds the center. What is that diameter, in millimeters? Round to three decimal places.
--- answer
0.172
0.172 mm
0.172mm
⌀0.172
⌀ 0.172
--- why
The straight-line miss comes from Pythagoras: $\sqrt{0.07^2 + 0.05^2} = \sqrt{0.0049 + 0.0025} = \sqrt{0.0074} = 0.0860\,\mathrm{mm}$. The zone is centered on true position, so its *radius* must reach the hole's center, and its diameter is twice that: $2 \times 0.0860 = 0.172\,\mathrm{mm}$. "Below" does not matter: the minus sign disappears when the miss is squared, because a round zone treats every direction alike. Two common slips: reporting $0.086$ (the radius, half the right number) or adding the two misses, $0.07 + 0.05 = 0.12$, which is the distance along two sides of a triangle instead of across it.

+++ question | The corners the circle trims off
--- ask
A drawing locates a hole $\pm 0.1\,\mathrm{mm}$ in $x$ and $\pm 0.1\,\mathrm{mm}$ in $y$. A new revision replaces that with position $\varnothing\,0.2$ at the same true position. Which hole, given as its miss from true position, passed the old drawing but fails the new one?
--- choice
$0.10$ to the right, $0.00$ up.
--- choice correct
$0.08$ to the right, $0.08$ up.
--- choice
$0.12$ to the right, $0.00$ up.
--- choice
$0.05$ to the right, $0.05$ up.
--- why
A round zone of diameter $0.2$ fits *inside* the old $0.2 \times 0.2$ square: it touches the middle of each side and cuts off the four corners. The hole at $(0.08, 0.08)$ sits in a corner region. It is inside the square, since $0.08 < 0.1$ both ways, but its straight-line miss is $\sqrt{0.08^2 + 0.08^2} = 0.113$, which needs a zone of $\varnothing\,0.226$. The hole at $(0.10, 0)$ needs exactly $\varnothing\,0.2$ and sits on both boundaries, so it passes both. $(0.12, 0)$ fails both. $(0.05, 0.05)$ needs only $\varnothing\,0.141$ and passes both. To keep every part the square accepted, the circle must pass through the corners: $\varnothing\,0.283$.

+++ question | A frame read box by box
--- ask
A hole carries this feature control frame:

`| ⌖ | 0.3 | A | C | B |`

Which reading is right?
--- choice correct
Position, within a zone of two parallel planes $0.3\,\mathrm{mm}$ apart ($0.15$ each side of true position), regardless of feature size, with A primary, C secondary and B tertiary.
--- choice
Position, within a round zone of diameter $0.3$ at maximum material condition, relative to A, B and C in alphabetical order.
--- choice
The letters are out of order, so the drawing has a mistake; the inspector should build the frame in the order A, B, C.
--- choice
Position, within a round zone $0.3$ across, because a position zone is always a cylinder around the true axis.
--- why
Read the second box first for its shape: there is no diameter sign ⌀, so the zone is the space between two parallel planes (or lines), and $0.3$ is their distance apart. There is no circled M or L, so the tolerance is regardless of feature size (RFS): the number stays fixed. The datum boxes are read in the order they are written, and that order is precedence: A is seated first, C second, B third. Nothing requires alphabetical order, and quietly "fixing" it would build a different datum reference frame and measure a different requirement.

+++ question | Band or circle
--- ask
A hole's center is found $0.09\,\mathrm{mm}$ off true position in $x$ and $0.15\,\mathrm{mm}$ off in $y$. Compare two ways of controlling its position.

- Drawing 1: position $\varnothing\,0.2$.
- Drawing 2: position $0.2$ with no diameter sign, drawn so that its two planes are square to $x$. It limits the $x$ direction only.

Which verdicts are right?
--- choice correct
It fails drawing 1, which needs a round zone of about $\varnothing\,0.35$, and passes drawing 2, because $0.09$ is within the $0.1$ each side of the band's middle.
--- choice
It passes both, because $0.09$ and $0.15$ are each smaller than $0.2$.
--- choice
It fails both, because $0.15$ is more than half of $0.2$.
--- choice
It passes drawing 1 and fails drawing 2, because a band is always stricter than a round zone.
--- why
With the ⌀, the zone is a circle: the center may be at most $0.1$ from true position in *any* direction. The straight-line miss is $\sqrt{0.09^2 + 0.15^2} = \sqrt{0.0306} = 0.175$, which needs $\varnothing\,0.35$, so drawing 1 fails. Without the ⌀, the zone is a band $0.2$ wide, $0.1$ each side of true position, and this band only looks at $x$. The $0.15$ in $y$ is not its business (a real drawing would control $y$ with another requirement). Read the ⌀ before you read the number: the same digits mean a different shape.

+++ question | A conversion that throws parts away
--- ask
An engineer redraws an old hole callout of $\pm 0.08\,\mathrm{mm}$ in $x$ and $\pm 0.08\,\mathrm{mm}$ in $y$. He writes position $\varnothing\,0.16$ and adds a note: "Same numbers, so every part the old drawing accepted still passes." What is wrong?
--- choice correct
$\varnothing\,0.16$ is the circle that fits inside the old square. A hole $0.08$ off in both $x$ and $y$ passed before and now needs about $\varnothing\,0.226$. Keeping every old part needs the circle through the corners, about $\varnothing\,0.226$.
--- choice
A position tolerance is a radius, so $\varnothing\,0.16$ is twice too loose; he should write $\varnothing\,0.08$.
--- choice
He should add the two bands together and write $\varnothing\,0.32$.
--- choice
Nothing: $\pm 0.08$ both ways and $\varnothing\,0.16$ describe the same zone.
--- why
The corner of the old square is $0.08$ across and $0.08$ up, a straight-line distance of $\sqrt{0.08^2 + 0.08^2} = 0.113$. The round zone that keeps every part the square accepted must reach that far: diameter $2 \times 0.113 = 0.226$. A circle of $\varnothing\,0.16$ only touches the middles of the square's sides. Choosing $\varnothing\,0.16$ can still be a sound design decision, but then the note is false: it rejects corner parts that used to pass. The number in a position frame is a diameter, not a radius, and adding the two bands has no geometric meaning.

+++ question | How much lean the fence can take
--- ask
A plate's left edge is $90\,\mathrm{mm}$ tall and leans: its top corner sits farther left than its bottom corner. A hole is dimensioned $45 \pm 0.05\,\mathrm{mm}$ from the left edge and $70\,\mathrm{mm}$ up from the bottom. It was machined exactly $45.02\,\mathrm{mm}$ from the bottom corner.

One inspector stands the plate on a table and slides the left edge against an upright fence, which touches the edge's leftmost point, the top corner. What is the largest lean of the top corner, in millimeters, for which this inspector still passes the hole?
--- answer
0.03
0.030
0.03 mm
0.03mm
--- why
The fence touches the top corner, which sits "lean" millimeters left of the bottom corner. So this inspector reads $45.02 + \text{lean}$. The upper limit is $45.05$, so the lean may be at most $45.05 - 45.02 = 0.03\,\mathrm{mm}$. An inspector measuring from the bottom corner reads $45.02$ whatever the lean, and one measuring straight across at the hole's height reads $45.02 + \frac{70}{90} \times \text{lean}$. Past a lean of $0.03$ the three honest inspectors start to disagree about the same part. A plus-or-minus dimension never said which of them was right. A datum reference frame does: it names the surface and how it is contacted.

=== cad02-l02 | Practice: Datums, six degrees of freedom and the 3-2-1 rule
--- for
l02-datums-and-the-3-2-1-rule

+++ question | Naming the three things
--- ask
A gimbal housing is set with its machined base down on a granite surface plate for inspection. The drawing tags the base with the datum feature symbol A. Which description is right?
--- choice correct
The base is datum feature A. The plate's surface is the datum feature simulator. Datum A is the perfect plane the plate stands in for, resting on the base's high points.
--- choice
The base is datum A. The plate is datum feature A. The base's high points are the simulator.
--- choice
The base is both the datum feature and the datum, because a machined face is flat enough to count as perfect.
--- choice
Datum A is found by averaging many probe readings taken all over the base; the plate only holds the part.
--- why
The **datum feature** is the real, imperfect surface: you can touch it, and under a microscope it has hills and valleys. The **datum** is the perfect plane derived from it, which nobody can touch. The **datum feature simulator**, here the surface plate, is the hardware that makes the idea real: much flatter than the part, it touches the base's highest points, and its surface stands in for the datum. No machined face is perfect, so the two words must stay apart. An average plane through probe readings runs through the middle of the bumps, which is not where the plate sits, so it is not datum A.

+++ question | Skipping the secondary
--- ask
A rectangular bracket is seated on its large face, datum A, with three points of contact. The inspector then skips datum B and pushes the bracket's short end against a single stop, touching at one point. How many degrees of freedom are still free?
--- answer
2
two
2 dof
--- why
Seated on A, the bracket has lost three degrees of freedom: moving down, and tipping about the two directions in the plate. Three are left: two slides and a spin in the plane. The single stop on the end removes one slide, the one straight into the stop. Two remain: sliding along the end face, and spinning about the contact point like a door on a hinge. That spin is exactly what the secondary datum's *two* points are for. Skip B and the part is not located, so every measurement can drift.

+++ question | What two points take away
--- ask
Primary datum A is a plane lying in the $x$ and $y$ directions; the part rests on it. Secondary datum B is a long edge running along $x$, touched by an angle plate at two points spread along the edge. Which two degrees of freedom does B remove?
--- choice correct
Translation along $y$, and rotation about $z$.
--- choice
Translation along $x$, and translation along $y$.
--- choice
Rotation about $x$, and rotation about $y$.
--- choice
Translation along $z$, and rotation about $z$.
--- why
The angle plate faces the edge across $y$, so pushing into it stops sliding along $y$. Because its two points are spread along the edge, the part can no longer turn flat on the plate, so rotation about $z$ is gone too. Sliding along $x$, along the plate, is still free: that is the tertiary datum's job. Moving along $z$ and tipping about $x$ and $y$ were already removed by the primary plane, so B cannot take them again. Each datum only takes freedoms that are still left.

+++ question | Contacts squeezed together
--- ask
Secondary datum B is touched at two points only $20\,\mathrm{mm}$ apart, because the angle plate is short. A chip $0.01\,\mathrm{mm}$ thick sits under one of the two contacts, so the part turns a little in the plane. About how far does a hole $80\,\mathrm{mm}$ from the contacts move? Answer in millimeters.
--- answer
0.04
0.040
0.04 mm
0.04mm
--- why
For a small turn, the turn is the chip's height divided by the distance between the contacts: $0.01 / 20 = 0.0005$. A point a distance $d$ away moves about $d$ times that: $80 \times 0.0005 = 0.04\,\mathrm{mm}$. With the two contacts spread $100\,\mathrm{mm}$ apart, the turn would be $0.01 / 100 = 0.0001$ and the hole would move only $0.008$, five times less. That is why the secondary's two points go far apart, along the longest suitable edge: the same speck of dirt then turns the part far less.

+++ question | Measuring from wherever the probe lands
--- ask
Datum A is a bracket's machined bottom face. To report a hole's height above datum A, an inspector touches the probe once on the bottom face right next to the hole, and subtracts. The face has a shallow dip, $0.03\,\mathrm{mm}$ deep, exactly where she touched. What is wrong with her method?
--- choice correct
Datum A is the plane a surface plate makes on the face's high points, not the spot she touched. Her single touch measures from the bottom of the dip, so her number is off by the dip's depth, and another inspector touching elsewhere would get a different number.
--- choice
Nothing: she touched the real surface of datum feature A, and that surface is the datum.
--- choice
She should have probed many points and used the average plane through them; the average plane is datum A.
--- choice
She should have measured from the top face instead, because it is farther from the dip.
--- why
"Measure from datum A" means from the perfect plane the simulator forms by resting on the face's high points. A single touch at a random spot brings back exactly the ambiguity GD&T removes: every inspector picks a different spot and gets a different number. An average plane is a real misconception too: it runs through the middle of the bumps, while a plate rests on the highest ones, so it sits in a different place. The top face is a different feature altogether and is not named as the datum.

+++ question | A datum scheme for a tracker bracket
--- ask
A star tracker bracket bolts to a panel through its $110 \times 70\,\mathrm{mm}$ base face. On the panel it is located by a dowel pin in hole H1 near one end of the base, and by a second pin in slot S at the far end, $90\,\mathrm{mm}$ away. A $6\,\mathrm{mm}$ tall side edge runs along the base. Which datum scheme best copies how the bracket really sits?
--- choice correct
A: the base face (primary). B: hole H1 (secondary). C: slot S (tertiary).
--- choice
A: the $6\,\mathrm{mm}$ side edge (primary). B: the base face. C: hole H1.
--- choice
A: hole H1 (primary). B: the base face. C: slot S.
--- choice
A: the base face. B: hole H1. C: the side edge, touched at one point near H1.
--- why
A good scheme imitates the assembly. The bracket is clamped flat on its base, so the large base face is primary: three well-spread contacts, and the same high points the panel will touch. Hole H1, square to the base, stops both slides in the plane, which is the secondary's two degrees of freedom taken by a pin instead of an edge. Slot S stops the last spin about H1, and $90\,\mathrm{mm}$ is a long lever, so it stops the spin well. A $6\,\mathrm{mm}$ edge as primary multiplies any burr by the long distance to the features. The side edge touched near H1 would stop the spin with a very short lever, and it is not how the bracket is located on the panel.

=== cad02-l03 | Practice: Datum targets and datum precedence
--- for

+++ question | A symbol with an empty top half
--- ask
A datum target symbol has an empty top half and C1 in its bottom half. Its leader points to an X on a casting's end face, and the other view of that face shows only an X as well, with no phantom line. What does it call for?
--- choice correct
A point target, the one target of datum C. The fixture touches the end face with a single round-tipped pin, placed at the target's basic location.
--- choice
An area target $1\,\mathrm{mm}$ across, because the number 1 gives its size.
--- choice
The first of three targets that together make datum C a plane.
--- choice
A line target, because an empty top half means the contact runs along a line.
--- why
The top half of the symbol gives the size of an **area** target, so an empty top half means a point or a line. An X that shows as an X in both views is a **point** target; a line target would show as a phantom line in the view that sees it lying along the surface. The bottom half names the datum and numbers its targets: C1 is the first target of datum C. A tertiary datum needs one contact in the 3-2-1 rule, so C1 alone is expected. In the fixture a point target becomes a pin with a rounded tip, placed by basic dimensions.

+++ question | A line target
--- ask
A thin sheet-metal part must be seated along its straight bottom edge, touching along a line. How does the drawing show that target, and what does the fixture use for it?
--- choice correct
An X in the view that sees the edge end-on, and a phantom line (a long dash and two short dashes) in the view that sees it lying along the surface. The fixture uses a pin with a knife edge or a round side.
--- choice
A hatched patch with its size in the top half of the symbol. The fixture uses a pin with a flat end of that size.
--- choice
A single X in every view. The fixture uses a ball-tipped pin.
--- choice
A thick solid line along the edge. The fixture uses the whole surface plate.
--- why
Each kind of target has its own drawing and its own hardware. A **point** target is an X, and a pin with a rounded tip. A **line** target is an X where the line is seen end-on and a phantom line where it is seen lying along the surface, and a pin with a knife edge or a round side, so it touches along a line. An **area** target is a hatched patch with its size in the symbol's top half, and a pin with a flat end of that size. Using the whole surface plate would bring back the problem targets solve: a floppy part touching wherever it happens to sag.

+++ question | Targets bunched in a corner
--- ask
Three targets make primary datum A on a casting. Design 1 bunches them in one corner, with the two farthest apart only $10\,\mathrm{mm}$ apart. Design 2 spreads them so the same two are $150\,\mathrm{mm}$ apart. A speck $0.05\,\mathrm{mm}$ high lifts one of those two targets. For a boss $100\,\mathrm{mm}$ away, how many times farther does the boss move with design 1 than with design 2?
--- answer
15
15 times
15x
15 x
15×
fifteen
--- why
The tilt is about the speck's height divided by the distance between the contacts. Design 1: $0.05 / 10 = 0.005$, so the boss moves about $100 \times 0.005 = 0.5\,\mathrm{mm}$. Design 2: $0.05 / 150 = 0.000333$, so it moves about $0.033\,\mathrm{mm}$. The ratio is $0.5 / 0.033 = 15$, which is also just $150 / 10$: the boss distance cancels. Datum targets fix *where* the part touches, but they must still be spread far apart, or the plane they define wobbles with every tiny bump under them. It is the short-base problem again.

+++ question | Seating B first can help
--- ask
A block's bottom face, datum feature A, is straight and $80\,\mathrm{mm}$ long. Its left face, datum feature B, is $40\,\mathrm{mm}$ tall and leans outward: its top sits $0.1\,\mathrm{mm}$ farther left than the bottom corner. A hole was drilled exactly $60.00\,\mathrm{mm}$ to the right of the bottom corner and $10.00\,\mathrm{mm}$ up. Its basic location is $60$ from B and $10$ from A, and it carries position $\varnothing\,0.15$.

Seated A primary and B secondary, the wall touches B's top corner, so the hole reads $60.10$ across and $10.00$ up: a position error of $\varnothing\,0.20$, a fail.

Now seat it B primary and A secondary. Using the small-tilt shortcut, what position error does the hole show, as a diameter in millimeters? Round to three decimal places.
--- answer
0.112
0.112 mm
⌀0.112
0.1118
--- why
Standing B upright tips the block by $0.1 / 40 = 0.0025$ about the bottom-left corner. The hole, $10\,\mathrm{mm}$ up, slides right by $10 \times 0.0025 = 0.025$, so it reads $60.025$ across. Being $60\,\mathrm{mm}$ to the right, it drops by $60 \times 0.0025 = 0.15$, to $9.85$ above the corner's height. The bottom face now slopes down to the right; its far end, $80\,\mathrm{mm}$ out, drops $80 \times 0.0025 = 0.20$, and the table rises to touch that lowest point. So the hole is $9.85 + 0.20 = 10.05$ above the table. The miss is $(0.025, 0.05)$: $2\sqrt{0.025^2 + 0.05^2} = 2 \times 0.0559 = 0.112$, which passes. The same part fails one way and passes the other. Precedence does not always make things worse; it decides *which* requirement you are measuring, which is why the order belongs to the drawing.

+++ question | The order the probe happened to take
--- ask
A bracket's holes carry `| ⌖ | ⌀0.2 | A | B | C |`. The CMM programmer probes datum feature B first, because it faces the probe, and the software builds the frame in the order the features were probed: B, then A, then C. Several brackets fail. What is the right conclusion?
--- choice correct
The program measured a different requirement. The frame must be fitted A first, then B constrained to it, then C, as the drawing says. Rerun before judging the parts.
--- choice
The parts are bad. The order in which datums are fitted does not change a position result.
--- choice
It is fine as long as all three datums were used; the letters define the frame, not their order.
--- choice
Change the drawing to B, A, C so that it matches the program.
--- why
Real datum features are never exactly square to each other, so only the first one fitted gets full contact; the later ones touch where the first allows. Fitting B first builds a different frame, and the same holes then measure differently. Those parts may be good or bad; the run cannot tell. If the drawing's order ever looks wrong, the fix is to ask the designer, and the designer chooses the order by copying how the part is held in the assembly, not by what suits a program or a fixture.

+++ question | One pin where two belong
--- ask
A casting's drawing uses targets A1, A2 and A3 on its base, B1 and B2 on its long side, and C1 on its end. The fixture builder, short of room, puts one pin on the long side and two pins on the end. The part still sits firmly and cannot move. What has gone wrong?
--- choice correct
The end face now stops the part turning, so it acts as the secondary datum and the long side as the tertiary. The fixture builds the frame A, C, B instead of A, B, C, and parts measure differently from what the drawing requires.
--- choice
Nothing. Six pins still remove all six degrees of freedom, so the frame is the same.
--- choice
The part is under-constrained and can still spin about the vertical axis.
--- choice
The part is over-constrained and will rock like a four-legged table.
--- why
Counting is not enough. Three, two and one contacts still remove six degrees of freedom, so the part is held. But the face with two contacts is the one that sets the part's rotation in the plane, and that is exactly what makes a datum secondary. With two pins on the end, the end face dominates and the long side only stops the last slide. That is a different precedence, so a different frame and different results. The target labels on the drawing (B1, B2, C1) say how many contacts each datum gets, and the fixture must follow them.

=== cad02-l04 | Practice: Form: flat, straight, round and cylindrical
--- for

+++ question | Flatness from nine readings
--- ask
A reaction wheel's mounting face is called out flat within $0.025\,\mathrm{mm}$. The inspector levels it and reads nine heights, in millimeters:

$0.006,\ -0.004,\ 0.011,\ 0.002,\ -0.009,\ 0.005,\ 0.013,\ -0.001,\ 0.000$

What flatness do these readings show, in millimeters?
--- answer
0.022
0.022 mm
0.022mm
--- why
On a leveled face, flatness from indicator readings is the highest reading minus the lowest. Highest: $0.013$. Lowest: $-0.009$. Subtracting a negative adds it: $0.013 - (-0.009) = 0.022\,\mathrm{mm}$. That is under $0.025$, so on these readings the face passes. Two cautions from the lesson: the face must be leveled first, because the zone may tip, and nine points can miss a high spot between them.

+++ question | A diameter sign beside the size
--- ask
A pin is dimensioned $\varnothing\,12.00$ to $12.05\,\mathrm{mm}$. Right next to that size dimension sits the frame `| ⏤ | ⌀0.03 |`. What does the frame control?
--- choice correct
The derived median line, the line joining the centers of every cross-section, must lie inside a cylinder $0.03\,\mathrm{mm}$ in diameter.
--- choice
Each straight line along the pin's surface must lie between two parallel lines $0.03\,\mathrm{mm}$ apart.
--- choice
The pin's diameter may change by no more than $0.03\,\mathrm{mm}$ along its length.
--- choice
The pin's axis must lie within $0.03\,\mathrm{mm}$ of a datum axis.
--- why
Straightness comes in two flavors, and the frame's placement and its ⌀ tell them apart. Pointing at a surface line, with no ⌀, it checks each surface line element between two parallel lines. Placed beside the size dimension, with ⌀, it checks the axis: the derived median line must fit in a cylinder of that diameter, so a banana-shaped pin fails. It says nothing about diameter changes, which are size. And like every form control it has no datum: a straight pin can lie at any angle.

+++ question | A form control that adds nothing yet
--- ask
A pin is dimensioned $\varnothing\,10.00$ to $10.06\,\mathrm{mm}$, and it carries straightness $0.02\,\mathrm{mm}$ on its surface line elements (no diameter sign). It is made at $10.05\,\mathrm{mm}$ all along its length, and it is slightly bowed. What is the most its axis may bow? Answer in millimeters.
--- answer
0.01
0.010
0.01 mm
0.01mm
--- why
Two limits apply, and the tighter one wins. **Rule #1**: the pin must fit inside a perfect tube at its MMC size, $10.06$. At $10.05$ the room left is $10.06 - 10.05 = 0.01$, so the bow may be at most $0.01$. **Straightness**: a pin of constant diameter bowed by $b$ has every surface line bowed by $b$, so $b \le 0.02$. The answer is $0.01$. At this size the straightness frame adds nothing; it only starts to bite for pins made at $10.04$ or thinner, where Rule #1 would allow $0.02$ or more.

+++ question | A tilted face that is perfectly flat
--- ask
An inspector forgets to level a face before reading heights on a 3 by 3 grid of points, $20\,\mathrm{mm}$ apart. The rows, in millimeters, are:

- row 1: $0.000,\ 0.010,\ 0.020$
- row 2: $0.005,\ 0.015,\ 0.025$
- row 3: $0.010,\ 0.020,\ 0.030$

Highest minus lowest is $0.030$. What flatness do these points actually show, in millimeters?
--- answer
0
0.0
0.00
0.000
zero
0 mm
--- why
Every step to the right adds exactly $0.010$, and every step down adds exactly $0.005$. All nine points lie on one tilted plane. A flatness zone may tip any way it likes, so two parallel planes tilted with the face hold every point with no gap at all: the flatness is $0$. The $0.030$ is tilt, not unflatness. Tilt would matter to parallelism or perpendicularity, which lock the zone's angle to a datum; flatness has no datum. A CMM avoids this slip by fitting the minimum-zone planes in software.

+++ question | A diameter spread against circularity
--- ask
A $\varnothing\,20$ journal carries circularity $0.01\,\mathrm{mm}$. An inspector measures one cross-section with a micrometer in six directions. The readings run from $20.004$ to $20.016$, a spread of $0.012$, and he rejects the part "because $0.012$ is more than $0.01$". What is wrong with his check?
--- choice correct
Circularity is the radial gap between two concentric circles, judged by radius about a center. A two-point spread says little: on a plain oval, $0.012$ across is only about $0.006$ of radial gap, and a micrometer cannot see odd lobes at all. The slice has to be measured by radius as it turns about a center.
--- choice
Nothing. Circularity is the largest diameter minus the smallest diameter in one slice.
--- choice
He should compare the spread with twice the tolerance, $0.02$, and pass the part; that fixes the check.
--- choice
Circularity needs a datum axis, so no measurement of this journal can judge it.
--- why
The circularity zone is two concentric circles whose *radii* differ by $t$. For an oval, the long and short radii differ by half the difference of the diameters, so a $0.012$ spread is a radial gap of about $0.006$, which would pass. Doubling the tolerance fixes that one case but not the deeper problem: on a three-lobed part every bump faces a dip, so a micrometer reads the same number in every direction while the radius swings up and down. Only a radius-based measurement about a center, as on a precision spindle or a CMM, judges circularity. And circularity takes no datum.

+++ question | Rule #1 on a plate's thickness
--- ask

0.63 mm
--- why
The worst tilt allowed inside the part runs from one side of the zone at the bottom of the thread to the other side at the surface: $0.15$ across in $10\,\mathrm{mm}$, a slope of $0.015$. The stud keeps going at that slope. === cad02-l09 | Practice: Profile and runout
--- for

+++ question | Points against a profile band
--- ask
A fairing skin carries profile of a surface $0.6$, equal-bilateral, to A, B, C. The CMM reports these deviations from the model, perpendicular to the surface, in millimeters (plus means proud):

$0.21,\ -0.28,\ 0.33,\ -0.12,\ 0.05,\ -0.31,\ 0.18$

How many of the points are outside the zone?
--- answer
2
two
--- why
An equal-bilateral zone of $0.6$ is split evenly: $0.3$ outside the model and $0.3$ inside. Every deviation must lie from $-0.3$ to $+0.3$. Two points break it: $+0.33$ and $-0.31$. Reading the $0.6$ as $\pm 0.6$ would pass all seven, doubling the tolerance. With datums A, B and C the zone is fixed on the part, so the points cannot be rescued by sliding the band.

+++ question | The largest offset a runout allows
--- ask
A gear seat carries circular runout $0.02\,\mathrm{mm}$ to the shaft's datum axis. If the seat were perfectly round, what is the largest amount its center could be off the datum axis and still pass? Answer in millimeters.
--- answer
0.01
0.010
0.01 mm
--- why
A round seat whose center is a distance $e$ off the axis swings the indicator from $+e$ on one side to $-e$ half a turn later, so the full indicator movement is $\text{FIM} = 2e$. Passing means $2e \le 0.02$, so $e \le 0.01$. Going the other way, an allowed offset turns into a runout tolerance twice as big. A real seat is never perfectly round, and out-of-round also moves the needle, so a real seat gets less offset than this.

+++ question | A flatness frame under a profile
--- ask
A flat seat, nominally parallel to datum A, carries profile of a surface $0.3$ to A, B, C. The designer considers adding one more frame to it. Which one adds a real requirement?
--- choice correct
`| ▱ | 0.05 |`
--- choice
`| ▱ | 0.4 |`
--- choice
`| ∥ | 0.3 | A |`
--- choice
`| ⌓ | 0.3 |`, the same profile with no datums
--- why
With a full datum reference frame, profile $0.3$ controls the seat's form, orientation and location at once: the seat must lie in a band $0.3$ wide locked to the part. So it is already flat within $0.3$ and parallel to A within $0.3$, which makes flatness $0.4$ and parallelism $0.3$ add nothing. The same profile with no datums controls only shape, which the located one already does. Flatness $0.05$ is a genuine refinement, tighter than the form the profile allows. Profile can stand in for several controls, and the smaller ones are only worth adding when they are tighter.

+++ question | A deviation measured straight up
--- ask
A curved skin carries profile of a surface $0.6$, equal-bilateral. At one point, where the skin slopes at $60^\circ$ from horizontal, an old program measured the gap between the real and the model surface straight up, in $z$: $0.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ proud. Is that point in the zone?
--- choice correct
Yes. The zone is measured perpendicular to the surface. On a $60^\circ$ slope, a vertical gap of $0.5$ is only about $0.5 \times \cos 60^\circ = 0.25$ perpendicular to the skin, inside $\pm 0.3$.
--- choice
No. $0.5$ is more than $0.3$.
--- choice
Yes. $0.5$ is less than the zone's total width, $0.6$.
--- choice
No. Perpendicular to a slope the gap is bigger than the vertical one, $0.5 / \cos 60^\circ = 1.0$.
--- why
The profile zone is a shell of constant thickness measured perpendicular to the true surface at every point. On a steep slope, a vertical line crosses that thin shell on a long slant, so vertical gaps exaggerate the deviation. Picture a right triangle: the vertical gap is its long side, and the perpendicular distance is the side next to the $60^\circ$ angle, $0.5 \times \cos 60^\circ = 0.25$. The total width $0.6$ is not the limit either: an equal-bilateral zone allows $0.3$ each way.

+++ question | Reading FIM as the offset
--- ask
A gear mesh analysis needs a seat's center within $0.02\,\mathrm{mm}$ of the shaft's bearing axis. The seat is known to be very round. An inspector spins the shaft on its bearing journals and reports a full indicator movement of $0.03\,\mathrm{mm}$ on the seat. The engineer writes: "Seat is $0.03$ off center, over the $0.02$ limit. Reject." What is right?
--- choice correct
FIM is twice the offset. A round seat showing $0.03$ is only $0.015$ off center, inside the limit. On the drawing, the $0.02$ offset limit should appear as a runout of $0.04$.
--- choice
The engineer is right: FIM is the offset.
--- choice
FIM is half the offset, so the seat is $0.06$ off center and fails badly.
--- choice
Runout says nothing about centering; only a CMM can tell.
--- why
As the shaft turns, the side that sticks out $e$ reads $+e$ and, half a turn later, the side that sinks $e$ reads $-e$. The needle's full swing is $2e$. So divide FIM by two to get the offset: $0.015$. And multiply an allowed offset by two to write the runout tolerance: $0.04$. Runout does see centering, together with out-of-round, which is exactly what the gear's mesh feels as the shaft spins.

+++ question | Four stations and a trend
--- ask
A gimbal journal is checked for runout at four stations along its length, with one shared zero. Each row is the lowest and highest reading in one turn, in millimeters:

- station 1: $-0.002$ to $0.007$
- station 2: $0.001$ to $0.011$
- station 3: $0.004$ to $0.013$
- station 4: $0.006$ to $0.016$

What total runout does the journal show, in millimeters?
--- answer
0.018
0.018 mm
--- why
Total runout is one number for the whole surface: the highest reading anywhere minus the lowest anywhere, $0.016 - (-0.002) = 0.018$. Circular runout judges each circle alone: $0.009$, $0.010$, $0.009$ and $0.010$, so a circular runout of $0.015$ passes while a total runout of $0.015$ fails. Look at the pattern: each station's readings sit a little higher than the one before, so the surface creeps away from the axis along the journal, as a taper or a tilted axis would. Only total runout, with the indicator traversed along the length while the part turns, sees that.

=== cad02-l10 | Practice: Free state, geometric stack-ups and the error budget
--- for

+++ question | Three zones in one column
--- ask
A sensor's side-to-side location passes through three contributors along one direction: a position $\varnothing\,0.16$, a position $\varnothing\,0.07$, and a profile of a surface $0.12$, equal-bilateral. What is the worst-case stack, in plus-or-minus millimeters?
--- answer
0.175
±0.175
0.175 mm
--- why
First turn each zone into a plus-or-minus number along the one direction. A round position zone lets the axis reach its edge, half the diameter away: $\pm 0.08$ and $\pm 0.035$. An equal-bilateral profile of $0.12$ lets the surface sit $0.06$ out or $0.06$ in: $\pm 0.06$. Worst case adds them straight: $0.08 + 0.035 + 0.06 = 0.175$. +++ question | A hole with a bonus in the stack
--- ask
A hole's MMC is $4.00\,\mathrm{mm}$ and its LMC is $4.10$. It carries position $\varnothing\,0.05$ at MMC. What is its worst-case contribution to a one-direction stack, in plus-or-minus millimeters?
--- answer
0.075
±0.075
0.075 mm
--- why
With Ⓜ, the zone a real part can reach grows with its size. At LMC the bonus is the full size tolerance, $4.10 - 4.00 = 0.10$, so the zone can be $\varnothing\,(0.05 + 0.10) = \varnothing\,0.15$. Along one direction the axis can move half of that: $\pm 0.075$. Using only the stated $0.05$, which is $\pm 0.025$, would understate the worst case by a factor of three.

+++ question | A ring that sags in its cradle
--- ask
A thin ring carries circularity $1.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ marked Ⓕ, free state. Standing on edge in its cradle, it takes a smooth oval shape: its longest diameter measures $901.2\,\mathrm{mm}$ and its shortest $898.9\,\mathrm{mm}$. What radial gap does the free-state check show, in millimeters?
--- answer
1.15
1.15 mm
1.150
--- why
For an oval, the two concentric circles that just hold the outline have radii equal to the long and short half-diameters. Their gap is half the difference of the diameters: $(901.2 - 898.9) / 2 = 2.3 / 2 = 1.15\,\mathrm{mm}$. That is more than $1.0$, so the Ⓕ tolerance fails: the ring is too floppy in the free state, even if it would be round once bolted down. Comparing the whole $2.3$ with the tolerance mixes diameter with radius, the other way round.

+++ question | Clamped harder than the note says
--- ask
A thin panel's drawing has a restraint note: datum feature A bolted to a flat fixture with 12 bolts torqued to $5\,\mathrm{N \cdot m}$, panel horizontal. To hold it steadier, the inspector uses 24 bolts at $10\,\mathrm{N \cdot m}$. The panel passes. What does the result show?
--- choice correct
Nothing reliable. Extra force can pull a warped panel into its zones, and it springs back when the bolts come out. Remeasure with exactly the restraint the note gives.
--- choice
The panel is good: more restraint only makes a measurement more repeatable.
--- choice
The tolerances now apply in the free state instead, so the panel must be remeasured unbolted.
--- choice
The panel is good, provided the Ⓕ tolerances are also checked with the 24 bolts.
--- why
The restraint note defines the condition each tolerance (without Ⓕ) applies in. It copies how the part is held in the assembly, so the measurement predicts the part's real shape there. Clamping harder or in more places can force almost any warped part into its zones, which hides the defect. The note does not switch anything to the free state; it overrides the free-state default. Tolerances marked Ⓕ apply with nothing holding the part, never under bolts.

+++ question | The wrong span in a trace
--- ask
An engineer traces a $0.04^\circ$ clocking allocation to two dowel-pin holes $50\,\mathrm{mm}$ apart, on a bracket $180\,\mathrm{mm}$ long. The pins float $0.008\,\mathrm{mm}$ in total. The work:

1. $0.04^\circ \times \pi / 180 = 0.000698\,\mathrm{rad}$.
2. Allowed opposite motion at the pins: $0.000698 \times 180 = 0.126\,\mathrm{mm}$.
3. Subtract the float: $0.126 - 0.008 = 0.118\,\mathrm{mm}$.
4. So the pin holes get position $\varnothing\,0.1$ to A, B, C.

Which line is wrong?
--- choice correct
Line 2. The span must be the pin spacing, $50\,\mathrm{mm}$: $0.000698 \times 50 = 0.035$, minus $0.008$ leaves $0.027$, so about $\varnothing\,0.025$. The $\varnothing\,0.1$ would allow about $0.12^\circ$, three times the budget.
--- choice
Line 1. Degrees become radians by multiplying by $180 / \pi$.
--- choice
Line 3. The float should be added to what is left for position, not subtracted.
--- choice
None; the trace is correct.
--- why
Clocking is the turn of the line between the two pins, so the lever is the distance between them, $50\,\mathrm{mm}$. Using the bracket's whole length credits the design with a lever arm it does not have. Check the result: $(0.1 + 0.008) / 50 = 0.00216\,\mathrm{rad}$, about $0.124^\circ$, far over $0.04^\circ$. Line 1 is right: degrees times $\pi/180$ give radians. The pin float uses up part of the allowance, so it is subtracted before position gets the rest.

+++ question | A tight budget in three frames
--- ask
An attitude-knowledge budget allows a star tracker seat $0.015^\circ$ of tilt and $0.015^\circ$ of clocking. The seat's contact points span $50\,\mathrm{mm}$ and sit at a basic $30^\circ$ to datum A. The two dowel-pin holes are $60\,\mathrm{mm}$ apart, and the pins float $0.006\,\mathrm{mm}$ in total. Which set of frames meets the budget?
--- choice correct
On the seat, `| ∠ | 0.013 | A | B |` and `| ▱ | 0.005 |`. On each pin hole, `| ⌖ | ⌀0.008 | A | B | C |`.
--- choice
On the seat, `| ∠ | 0.013 | A | B |` and `| ▱ | 0.005 |`. On each pin hole, `| ⌖ | ⌀0.008 Ⓜ | A | B | C |`.
--- choice
On the seat, `| ∠ | 0.013 | A |` and `| ▱ | 0.005 |`. On each pin hole, `| ⌖ | ⌀0.008 | A | B | C |`.
--- choice
On the seat, `| ∠ | 0.75 | A | B |` and `| ▱ | 0.005 |`. On each pin hole, `| ⌖ | ⌀0.9 | A | B | C |`.
--- why
$0.015^\circ$ is $0.015 \times \pi/180 = 0.000262\,\mathrm{rad}$. Tilt: $t \le 50 \times 0.000262 = 0.0131$, so angularity $0.013$ gives $0.00026\,\mathrm{rad}$, just inside. Clocking: $60 \times 0.000262 = 0.0157$ of opposite motion, minus the $0.006$ float, leaves $0.0097$ for position, so $\varnothing\,0.008$ gives $(0.008 + 0.006)/60 = 0.00023\,\mathrm{rad}$, inside. An Ⓜ on the pin holes would let bigger holes take bigger zones, and both add straight into clocking. Angularity to A alone lets the seat spin about A's normal like a weathervane while staying at $30^\circ$, so it needs B. The $0.75$ and $0.9$ come from multiplying degrees by millimeters. +++ question | A CMM's position number
--- ask
A hole's true position is at basic $(25, 40)\,\mathrm{mm}$ in the A, B, C frame. After building the frame, the CMM reports the hole's axis at $(25.018, 39.976)$. What position deviation does it report, as a diameter in millimeters?

The misses are $\Delta x = 0.018$ and $\Delta y = -0.024$. Squared and added: $0.000324 + 0.000576 = 0.0009$, and $\sqrt{0.0009} = 0.030$ (a 3-4-5 triangle, scaled down). +++ question | Which results a machine can back
--- ask
A shop's CMM has an uncertainty of about $0.004\,\mathrm{mm}$ for a bracket. The bracket carries a profile of $0.1$, a position of $\varnothing\,0.04$ and a flatness of $0.015$. By the ten-to-one rule of thumb, which of them can this machine check well?
--- choice correct
The profile and the position, at $25$ to $1$ and $10$ to $1$. The flatness, at under $4$ to $1$, needs a better instrument.
--- choice
All three, since $0.004$ is smaller than every one of the tolerances.
--- choice
Only the profile; a position needs at least $20$ to $1$.
--- choice
None. The machine must be ten times better than the tightest tolerance on the part before any of its results count.
--- why
The rule of thumb asks the measuring system to be about ten times finer than each tolerance it checks: $0.1 / 0.004 = 25$, $0.04 / 0.004 = 10$, $0.015 / 0.004 = 3.75$. Being merely smaller than the tolerance is not enough: at under four to one, a large share of the band is doubt, so results near the limit prove nothing. The ratio is judged tolerance by tolerance, so one tight feature does not disqualify the machine for the others; it sends that one feature to a better instrument.

+++ question | A hole exactly at its limit
--- ask
A hole has MMC $5.00\,\mathrm{mm}$ and LMC $5.12$, with `| ⌖ | ⌀0.08 Ⓜ | A | B | C |`. The CMM reports its actual mating size as $5.07$ and its axis $0.045$ off in $x$ and $0.060$ off in $y$. What is the verdict, and what size are the pins on a functional gauge for this hole?
--- choice correct
It passes, exactly at the limit: it needs $\varnothing\,0.15$ and has earned $\varnothing\,0.15$. The gauge pins are $\varnothing\,4.92$.
--- choice
It fails: it needs $\varnothing\,0.15$, which is more than the stated $0.08$. The gauge pins are $\varnothing\,4.92$.
--- choice

--- choice

--- why
The miss is $\sqrt{0.045^2 + 0.060^2} = \sqrt{0.005625} = 0.075$, so the position deviation is $\varnothing\,0.15$. The bonus is $5.07 - 5.00 = 0.07$, so the zone is $0.08 + 0.07 = 0.15$: a pass with nothing to spare. Comparing $0.075$ with $0.08$ compares a radius with a diameter. The gauge pin is the hole's virtual condition, $5.00 - 0.08 = 4.92$ (less a small gauge-maker's tolerance), and this hole would just drop over it. A result this close to the limit is only as good as the measurement's uncertainty.

+++ question | A guard band
--- ask
A hole carries position $\varnothing\,0.05$. The CMM's uncertainty for this measurement is $0.004\,\mathrm{mm}$. What is the largest reported position deviation, in millimeters, that still proves the hole is in tolerance?
--- answer
0.046
⌀0.046
0.046 mm
--- why
The true value can be anywhere within the uncertainty of the reading, so a reading $r$ proves a pass only if $r + 0.004 \le 0.05$, that is $r \le 0.046$. A reading of $0.048$ is "probably in, not proven in". Setting the internal acceptance limit at $0.046$ is a **guard band**: a part the shop accepts is in tolerance even after the doubt. Readings between $0.046$ and $0.05$ get remeasured with more care, measured on a better instrument, or reviewed.

+++ question | A report that looks fine
--- ask
A CMM report shows a tracker seat's perpendicularity to datum A as $0.018$, against a limit of $0.02$. Reviewing the program, you find that datum A, the bracket's base, was fit by least squares through five points bunched near the middle of the base. What should you conclude?
--- choice correct
The number cannot be trusted. A least-squares plane runs through the middle of the bumps instead of resting on the high points as a surface plate would, and five bunched points may miss the high points altogether. Refit A on its high points, with points spread out to the edges, and remeasure.
--- choice
The report is fine: least squares is the most accurate fit because it uses every point.
--- choice
The only problem is that five points is more than the three a plane needs; extra points add noise.
--- choice
The result errs on the safe side, because few points always make results look worse.
--- why
A datum must copy its physical simulator. The surface plate touches the base's highest points; a least-squares plane sits between the bumps, shifted and tilted, and every orientation result measured from it moves too. Bunched points make it worse: a tilt found from points close together is poorly determined, the short-base problem again, and the real high points near the edges may never be touched. Few points bias *form* results toward passing, not toward failing, and they make a datum fit less reliable, not more.

+++ question | A model with nothing inside

+++ question | Two runs, two answers
--- ask
A bracket's holes carry `| ⌖ | ⌀0.3 | A | B | C |`. Its mounting face A and its side B are $0.15\,\mathrm{mm}$ out of square over B's height. A first CMM run fits B first, then A, then C, and every hole passes. A second run fits A first, then B, then C, and two holes fail. Which statement is right?

+++ question | One frame too many

+++ question | Three ways to see a lobed pin

A centerless-ground pin carries circularity $0.005\,\mathrm{mm}$. A micrometer reads $10.002$ across it in every direction. --- choice correct
It fails. The scan is the one to believe: a micrometer cannot see odd lobes, because each bump faces a dip, and three points always lie exactly on some circle, so they can never show any out-of-roundness.
--- choice

--- choice

--- choice
