+++ question | What To Workspace keeps
--- ask
A model runs with the fixed-step solver `ode4` at $h = 0.01\,\mathrm{s}$ from $t = 0$ to $t = 2\,\mathrm{s}$. A To Workspace block logs the output with no decimation. The solver makes 800 derivative evaluations in total. How many rows of data does To Workspace log?
--- choice
800: one row for every derivative evaluation, because the blocks run at each of them.
--- choice correct
201: one row for every major time step, $t = 0, 0.01, \ldots, 2$, including the start.
--- choice
1601: one row at each major step and one at each midpoint slope sample in between.
--- choice
50: the solver only records once every 50 steps unless you shorten the Max step size.
--- why
The four slope samples inside each `ode4` step are minor time steps. Simulink runs the blocks there, but only to compute slopes; nothing is recorded. To Workspace, Scopes and discrete blocks act at major time steps only. There are $2 / 0.01 = 200$ steps, and the logged times are their 200 end points plus the starting point at $t = 0$: 201 rows. The 800 evaluations ($4 \times 200$) measure the work, not the output.

+++ question | An accepted step, and the next one
--- ask
The Python adaptive stepper from the lesson (Euler inside Heun, tolerance $10^{-3}$) tries a step of $0.1\,\mathrm{s}$ and gets an error estimate of $2.5 \times 10^{-4}$. The step is accepted. What step, in seconds, does the step-size rule try next?
--- answer
0.18
0.18 s
.18
--- why
The rule is $h_{\text{new}} = 0.9\,h\,(\text{tol}/\text{err})^{1/(p+1)}$ with $p = 1$ for Euler, so the power is $\tfrac{1}{2}$. Here $\text{tol}/\text{err} = 10^{-3} / (2.5 \times 10^{-4}) = 4$, whose square root is 2. So $h_{\text{new}} = 0.9 \times 0.1 \times 2 = 0.18\,\mathrm{s}$. The error came out four times smaller than allowed, and because the error grows like $h^2$, the step can double before it reaches the limit. The 0.9 aims a little short, so the next step is less likely to be rejected.

+++ question | How much of the frame goes to the solver
--- ask
A real-time plant model runs with `ode4` at a fixed step of $1\,\mathrm{ms}$ on a computer whose frame is $10\,\mathrm{ms}$. One derivative evaluation, a pass through the whole diagram, takes $20\,\mu\mathrm{s}$. What percentage of each frame does the solver use? Type a number.
--- answer
8
8%
8 %
8 percent
--- why
Each frame covers $10 / 1 = 10$ steps. Each `ode4` step makes 4 derivative evaluations, so a frame makes 40 of them: $40 \times 20\,\mu\mathrm{s} = 800\,\mu\mathrm{s} = 0.8\,\mathrm{ms}$, which is 8% of $10\,\mathrm{ms}$. Because the step is fixed, this number is the same in every frame, whatever the vehicle is doing. That is exactly the promise a variable-step solver cannot make, and it is why this calculation can be done before the model ever runs.

+++ question | An error exactly at the limit
--- ask
A variable-step solver using the lesson's step-size rule tries a step of $0.05\,\mathrm{s}$, and the error estimate comes out exactly equal to the tolerance. What happens?
--- choice
The step is rejected, because an error equal to the tolerance leaves no safety margin, and the solver retries at $0.045\,\mathrm{s}$.
--- choice correct
The step is accepted, and the next step tried is slightly smaller, $0.045\,\mathrm{s}$, because of the 0.9 safety factor.
--- choice
The step is accepted, and the next step is the same $0.05\,\mathrm{s}$, because the ratio tol / err is exactly 1.
--- choice
The step is accepted, and the next step doubles to $0.1\,\mathrm{s}$, because the solver always tries to grow after an accepted step.
--- why
The test is "error at or below the tolerance", so an estimate exactly at the limit is accepted and the clock moves on. The rule then gives $0.9 \times 0.05 \times 1^{1/2} = 0.045\,\mathrm{s}$. The ratio of 1 alone would keep the step the same, but the safety factor pulls it a little below the step that would exactly hit the tolerance, because a rejected step wastes every evaluation it made.

+++ question | A stepper whose clock runs away
--- ask
Someone edits the lesson's adaptive stepper. Its output ends at $x = 0.9995$ when the exact answer at 5 s is $0.99995$, and a plot shows the true curve shifted about one second late. Which change caused it?

```python
while t < 5.0:
    h = min(h, 5.0 - t)
    k1 = f(t, x)
    k2 = f(t + h, x + h * k1)
    x_euler = x + h * k1
    x_heun = x + h * (k1 + k2) / 2
    err = abs(x_heun - x_euler)
    if err <= tol:
        x = x_heun
        accepted += 1
    else:
        rejected += 1
    t = t + h
    h = 0.9 * h * math.sqrt(tol / max(err, 1e-16))
```
--- choice
`h = min(h, 5.0 - t)` should be removed, because it forces the last step to be too short.
--- choice
The error estimate should be `abs(x_heun - x)`, the change made by the step, not the gap between the two answers.
--- choice correct
`t = t + h` must only run when the step is accepted. Now a rejected step moves the clock forward without changing the state.
--- choice
The 0.9 safety factor makes every step too small, so the solution falls behind.
--- why
A rejected step must leave both the clock and the state where they were, and retry from the same point with a smaller step. Here the first attempt, a rash 1 s step, is rejected: the state stays at 0 but the clock jumps to 1 s. Everything after that is computed correctly, just one second late, which is exactly the shift in the plot. The fix is to move `t = t + h` inside the `if err <= tol:` branch, next to `x = x_heun`.

+++ question | A measured maximum is not a worst case
--- ask
A team runs its variable-step attitude model on the flight processor over 500 recorded test scenarios. The busiest frame took $3\,\mathrm{ms}$ of a $10\,\mathrm{ms}$ frame. They propose to fly it, arguing "the worst case has a threefold margin". What is the flaw?
--- choice
None. 500 scenarios is enough to call $3\,\mathrm{ms}$ the worst-case execution time, and a threefold margin covers the rest.
--- choice correct
$3\,\mathrm{ms}$ is only the largest time seen in those scenarios. The number of steps and rejections depends on the data, so an input they did not try, such as a burst of events or a violent maneuver, can take more; no worst-case bound can be stated.
--- choice
The measurement is wrong because a variable-step solver always takes the same time per frame on average, so the $3\,\mathrm{ms}$ frame must be a timer error.
--- choice
The flaw is only the processor: on a faster flight computer the variable-step model would be deployable.
--- why
A worst-case execution time is a bound over every possible input, not the largest value in a test set. A fixed-step solver's work per frame depends only on the step and the method, so its bound can be written down before any test. A variable-step solver's work grows with what the signals do, and nothing stops some untested input from forcing many more small steps and rejections. A faster processor shrinks the times but does not create a bound. This is also why code generators for embedded targets require a fixed-step solver.

+++ question | When AbsTol takes over
--- ask
A vehicle's speed state is about $250\,\mathrm{m/s}$ for most of a run, but at one moment it passes through $0.004\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Its RelTol is $10^{-4}$ and its AbsTol is $10^{-6}$. What error per step does the solver allow at that moment, in m/s?
--- answer
1e-6
1e-06
0.000001
10^-6
1x10^-6
1 x 10^-6
1e-6 m/s
0.000001 m/s
10^-6 m/s
--- why
The test is $e_i \le \max(\text{RelTol} \cdot |x_i|, \text{AbsTol}_i)$. At $0.004\,\mathrm{m/s}$, RelTol gives $10^{-4} \times 0.004 = 4 \times 10^{-7}$, which is smaller than AbsTol, so AbsTol sets the limit: $10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m/s}$. At $250\,\mathrm{m/s}$ the same state is allowed $10^{-4} \times 250 = 0.025\,\mathrm{m/s}$ per step. AbsTol is the floor that stops the solver chasing impossibly small errors when a state passes near zero.

+++ question | What an ode45 run really cost
--- ask
An `ode45` run finishes with 500 accepted steps and 40 rejected steps. About how many derivative evaluations did it make?
--- choice
About 3,000: six per accepted step. A rejected step costs nothing, because its result is thrown away.
--- choice correct
About 3,240: six for every attempt, accepted or rejected.
--- choice
About 3,780: seven for every attempt, since each Dormand–Prince step has seven slope samples.
--- choice
About 1,080: two for every attempt, as in a predict-evaluate-correct-evaluate step.
--- why
Dormand–Prince takes seven slope samples per step, but the first is the last sample of the previous accepted step (first same as last), so each attempt costs six new evaluations. A rejected attempt still makes its six evaluations before the error test throws them away; the retry reuses the slope at the unchanged starting point and makes six more. So $6 \times (500 + 40) = 3240$. The lesson's stiff run fits the same count: $6 \times (6039 + 988) = 42{,}162$ against the 42,164 reported. Two evaluations per step is `ode113`, not `ode45`.

+++ question | A hundred thousand times tighter
--- ask
On a smooth model, `ode45` takes 800 steps at RelTol $10^{-5}$. About how many steps should it take at RelTol $10^{-10}$, with every other setting the same and no step cap in play?
--- answer
8000
8,000
8000 steps
8,000 steps
--- why
The step error of `ode45`'s method goes like $h^5$, so the step count grows like $\text{tol}^{-1/5}$. The tolerance is $10^5$ times tighter, and $(10^5)^{1/5} = 10$, so about $10 \times 800 = 8000$ steps. `ode23`, whose step error goes like $h^3$, would need $(10^5)^{1/3} \approx 46$ times as many steps as it took at $10^{-5}$. That gap is why high-order methods win at tight tolerances.

+++ question | An AbsTol for a tiny state
--- ask
A gyro-bias state lives far below 1 rad/s, and biases as small as $2 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ matter to the navigation engineer. The model uses RelTol $10^{-3}$. By the lesson's rule of thumb, what Absolute tolerance should that Integrator get, in rad/s?
--- answer
2e-9
2e-09
2*10^-9
2x10^-9
2 x 10^-9
2 * 10^-9
0.000000002
2e-9 rad/s
--- why
The rule of thumb is AbsTol ≈ RelTol × the smallest size of the state you care about: $10^{-3} \times 2 \times 10^{-6} = 2 \times 10^{-9}$. With a model-wide AbsTol of $10^{-6}$ instead, the solver would allow an error of $10^{-6}\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ per step, half the size of the smallest bias that matters, and would treat those biases as noise. Setting the Integrator's own Absolute tolerance, or rescaling the state to microradians per second, keeps the rest of the model on its usual settings.

+++ question | A leash that is still too long
--- ask
A 200 s simulation has a valve spike $0.05\,\mathrm{s}$ long, computed from the clock inside a MATLAB Function block at an unknown time. An engineer runs:

```matlab
set_param(mdl, 'MaxStep', '0.1');
```

The spike still vanishes from some runs. Why, and what setting would guarantee at least one sample inside it?
--- choice
RelTol is too loose; tightening it to $10^{-8}$ makes the solver notice the spike even at a 0.1 s step.
--- choice correct
A 0.1 s step can start before the spike and end after it, so no sample lands inside. The Max step size must be shorter than 0.05 s, for example 0.025 s; building the spike from Step blocks with zero-crossing detection would be cheaper.
--- choice
The default Max step size of $200/50 = 4\,\mathrm{s}$ overrides the typed value, so `MaxStep` has no effect.
--- choice
MATLAB Function blocks run only at major time steps, so no Max step size can make the solver see the spike.
--- why
The solver only sees the model at its steps and minor steps. One model holds a rocket's altitude, near $10^{5}\,\mathrm{m}$, and its pointing error, near $10^{-4}\,\mathrm{rad}$. RelTol is $10^{-3}$ and AbsTol is $10^{-3}$ for every state. +++ question | The ceiling a fast mode sets
--- ask
A model's eigenvalues are $-0.2$, $-1.5 \pm 2j$ and $-5000\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. About what is the largest step, in milliseconds, that `ode45`'s stability limit allows?
--- answer
0.66
0.66 ms
0.66ms
.66
0.00066
0.00066 s
--- why
An explicit solver's stable step is set by the fastest eigenvalue: for the Dormand–Prince pair inside `ode45`, $h|\lambda| \lesssim 3.3$, so $h \le 3.3 / 5000 = 0.00066\,\mathrm{s} = 0.66\,\mathrm{ms}$. The complex pair has size $\sqrt{1.5^2 + 2^2} = 2.5$ and the slowest mode is $0.2$, so the stiffness ratio is $5000 / 0.2 = 25{,}000$. A 60 s run would need at least about 91,000 steps, although nothing you care about changes faster than about a second.

+++ question | Four forward Euler steps on a fast mode
--- ask
Forward Euler is used on the mode $\dot{x} = -500\,x$, starting at $x = 1$, with $h = 0.005\,\mathrm{s}$. What is $x$ after four steps?
--- answer
5.0625
5.06
5.063
+5.0625
--- why
The amplification factor is $1 + h\lambda = 1 + 0.005 \times (-500) = -1.5$. Four steps give $(-1.5)^4 = 5.0625$: the sign flipped every step (to $-1.5$, $2.25$, $-3.375$, $5.0625$) and the size grew by half each time. The true mode, $e^{-10}$ after 0.02 s, is effectively gone. The step is past the limit $2/500 = 0.004\,\mathrm{s}$. Backward Euler at the same step multiplies by $1/(1 + 2.5) = 0.286$ and decays.

+++ question | Which run is stiff
--- ask
Two models each take 12,040 steps with `ode45` at RelTol $10^{-4}$. At RelTol $10^{-2}$, model A takes 11,980 steps and model B takes 4,800. Which model is stiff?
--- choice
Model B, because its step count reacts strongly to the tolerance, which is what an unstable model does.
--- choice correct
Model A. A tolerance 100 times looser barely changed its steps, so something other than accuracy, a stability limit, is setting them. Model B's steps fell by about $100^{1/5} \approx 2.5$, as an accuracy-limited `ode45` run should.
--- choice
Both, because 12,040 steps is too many for any model that is not stiff.
--- choice
Neither can be judged from step counts; only the eigenvalues can show stiffness.
--- why
For an accuracy-limited run, `ode45`'s steps grow like $\text{tol}^{-1/5}$, so loosening the tolerance a hundredfold should cut them by about 2.5; model B did exactly that ($12040 / 4800 = 2.5$). Model A's step count ignored the tolerance, the signature of a solver riding a stability limit set by a fast mode. A big step count on its own proves nothing: a long run with a fine output can be expensive and perfectly healthy.

+++ question | Stable, but still rattling
--- ask
The trapezoidal rule, the method inside `ode23t`, is used on a fast mode with $h\lambda = -8$, starting at $x = 1$. What is $x$ after five steps, to three significant figures?
--- answer
-0.0778
-0.07776
-0.078
-0.0777
--- why
The trapezoidal factor is $\frac{1 + h\lambda/2}{1 - h\lambda/2} = \frac{1 - 4}{1 + 4} = -0.6$. Five steps give $(-0.6)^5 = -0.07776$: the mode flips sign every step and shrinks only slowly. It is stable, since the factor's size is below 1, but it rings. As $h\lambda$ grows more negative the factor heads to $-1$, so very fast modes are hardly damped at all. Backward Euler at the same $h\lambda$ multiplies by $1/9 = 0.111$ and has shrunk the mode below $2 \times 10^{-5}$ after five steps. That is why `ode23t` is for moderately stiff models.

+++ question | A stiff model full of events
--- ask
A propulsion model has very fast valve dynamics (stiffness ratio about $10^5$), and its valve opens and closes about 40 times a second through zero-crossing events. A tolerance of RelTol $10^{-2}$ is good enough for the study. With `ode15s` it is slow, and each event knocks it back to low order with small steps. Which solver should you try next?
--- choice
`ode113`, because it varies its order up to 13 and so recovers faster after each event.
--- choice correct
`ode23tb`: implicit, so the fast valve mode does not limit the step; one-step, so it restarts cleanly after events; and efficient at crude tolerances.
--- choice
`ode23t`, because it adds no numerical damping, so the valve motion stays accurate.
--- choice
`ode45` with a small Max step size, because explicit solvers restart instantly after events.

+++ question | The ode4 limit for a slower actuator
--- ask
A model's fastest eigenvalue is $-120\,\mathrm{rad/s}$. What is the largest fixed step that keeps `ode4` stable, in milliseconds, to one decimal place?
--- answer
23.2
23.2 ms
23.2ms
23.21
0.0232
0.0232 s
--- why
RK4 is stable on a real, decaying mode while $h|\lambda| < 2.785$, so $h < 2.785 / 120 = 0.0232\,\mathrm{s} = 23.2\,\mathrm{ms}$. For comparison, `ode1` needs $h < 2/120 = 16.7\,\mathrm{ms}$. +++ question | The largest ode4 step for a 25 Hz loop
--- ask
A discrete controller runs at 25 Hz, every $0.04\,\mathrm{s}$. The plant's fastest eigenvalue is $-150\,\mathrm{rad/s}$. The fixed step must divide the controller's period. What is the largest step, in seconds, that keeps `ode4` stable?

0.0133
0.01333

0.04/3
13.3 ms
13.33 ms
0.0133 s
--- why
The allowed steps are $0.04/n$: 0.04, 0.02, 0.0133, 0.01 s, and so on. RK4's limit is $2.785/150 = 0.0186\,\mathrm{s}$. At $0.02\,\mathrm{s}$, $z = h\lambda = -3$ and $R(-3) = 1 - 3 + 4.5 - 4.5 + 3.375 = 1.375$, above 1, so it grows. At $0.04/3 = 0.0133\,\mathrm{s}$, $z = -2$ and $R(-2) = 1 - 2 + 2 - 1.333 + 0.667 = 0.333$, so it decays. +++ question | A stiff plant that must keep its beat

+++ question | A factor of exactly minus one

Same 25 Hz loop as before, period $0.04\,\mathrm{s}$, fastest eigenvalue $-150\,\mathrm{rad/s}$, but now with `ode1`. +++ question | A stability script that says everything is fine
--- ask
A script meant to print each solver's growth factor reports `ode1 +1.000` for every step size, even $h = 0.02\,\mathrm{s}$ with $\lambda = -250$. Which line is wrong?

```python
from math import factorial

lam = -250.0
R = lambda z, p: sum(z**k / factorial(k) for k in range(p))   # line A
for n in [1, 2, 3, 4]:
    h = 0.02 / n                                                # line B
    z = h * lam                                                 # line C
    print(f"h={h:.5f}  ode1 {R(z, 1):+.3f}  ode4 {R(z, 4):+.3f}")
```
--- choice correct
Line A: `range(p)` stops one term short. The factor of an order-$p$ method needs powers up to $z^p$, so it must be `range(p + 1)`; as written, `R(z, 1)` is just 1.
--- choice
Line B: it should be `0.02 * n`, because more solver steps per cycle means a longer step.
--- choice
Line C: `z` should be `h / lam`, because the limit is $2/|\lambda|$.
--- choice
Nothing is wrong: at a step this small, Euler's factor really is 1.
--- why
The growth factor is the Taylor series of $e^z$ cut after the $z^p$ term: $1 + z$ for Euler, up to $z^4/24$ for RK4. `range(p)` gives the powers 0 to $p - 1$, so Euler becomes the constant 1, which can never exceed 1 and hides every instability, and "ode4" becomes RK3's polynomial. At $h = 0.02$ the true Euler factor is $1 - 5 = -4$. An off-by-one in a checking script is dangerous exactly because it errs on the reassuring side.

+++ question | When a Saturation engages
--- ask
The input $u = 8\sin(2t)$ feeds a Saturation block with limits $\pm 6$. Its zero-crossing function for the upper limit is $u - 6$. At what time, in seconds, does that function first cross zero? Give three decimal places.
--- answer
0.424
0.4240
0.424 s
--- why
The crossing is where $8\sin(2t) = 6$, so $\sin(2t) = 0.75$ and $2t = \arcsin 0.75 = 0.8481\,\mathrm{rad}$, giving $t = 0.424\,\mathrm{s}$. That is the instant the block switches from passing the input through to clipping it. With detection on, a variable-step solver brackets this moment and ends a step exactly there, so no step integrates across the corner.

+++ question | A less bouncy ball
--- ask
The lesson's ball is dropped from 10 m with $g = 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, but now the coefficient of restitution is $e = 0.5$. At what time, in seconds, does it come to rest? Give two decimal places.
--- answer
4.28
4.284
4.2835
4.28 s
--- why
The first impact is at $t_1 = \sqrt{2 \times 10 / 9.81} = 1.4278\,\mathrm{s}$. Each flight is $e$ times the one before, and the total is $T = t_1\left(1 + \frac{2e}{1 - e}\right)$. With $e = 0.5$ the bracket is $1 + 1/0.5 = 3$, so $T = 4.28\,\mathrm{s}$. There are still infinitely many bounces, packed ever closer before 4.28 s: a smaller $e$ only shortens the Zeno sum. The model still needs a rule for "it has stopped".

+++ question | Halving the step barely helped
--- ask
A fixed-step `ode4` model integrates the output of a Saturation block. Halving the step cuts the error by a factor of about 2, not the 16 that fourth order promises. What is the most likely reason?
--- choice
`ode4` is only fourth order on linear models; a Saturation makes every model second order.
--- choice correct
A corner, where the Saturation starts or stops clipping, falls inside a step. A step that straddles a corner makes an error that shrinks only like $h$, so it dominates. Putting a step boundary on the corner restores fourth-order behavior.
--- choice
The step is past the stability limit, so the error only halves.
--- choice
Round-off error has taken over, because halving the step doubled the number of additions.
--- why
Runge–Kutta formulas assume the model is smooth inside a step. When the slope jumps inside one, the fitted curve is wrong on one side of the jump, and the step's error is proportional to $h$, whatever the method's order. The lesson's example showed the same thing: stepping over the corners, a 3 times smaller step gave only about 6 times less error, while landing on them gave close to the full $3^4 = 81$. Zero-crossing detection does that landing for a variable-step solver.

+++ question | Robust with an explicit solver
--- ask
An `ode45` model slows down after a touchdown event. A colleague sets Solver reset method to Robust "to recompute everything after the event". Why will this change almost nothing?
--- choice
Robust only applies to fixed-step solvers.
--- choice correct
Robust recomputes the Jacobian at each reset, and `ode45` is explicit: it never uses a Jacobian, so there is nothing to recompute. The setting matters for the implicit solvers such as `ode15s`, `ode23t` and `ode23tb`.
--- choice
Robust is already the default, so selecting it changes nothing.
--- choice
Robust only changes how zero crossings are bracketed, and touchdown is already located exactly.
--- why
Fast, the default, reuses the Jacobian an implicit solver already has; Robust recomputes it and related quantities at every reset, which costs more per event but helps Newton's method converge after the dynamics change sharply. An explicit one-step solver like `ode45` has neither a Jacobian nor a long history, so a reset is already cheap for it. If an `ode45` model crawls after touchdown, look instead for stiffness from a stiff contact spring (lesson 3) or chattering at the contact.

+++ question | A ball that falls through the floor
--- ask
This `solve_ivp` script should stop when the ball first reaches the floor. It prints `0 0 -112.6`: status 0, no events found, and a final height of $-112.6\,\mathrm{m}$. Which line is the bug?

```python
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

g = 9.81
def fall(t, s):
    return [s[1], -g]

def floor(t, s):
    return s[0]
floor.terminal = True
floor.direction = 1

sol = solve_ivp(fall, (0.0, 5.0), [10.0, 0.0], events=floor, rtol=1e-9, atol=1e-12)
print(sol.status, sol.t_events[0].size, round(sol.y[0][-1], 1))
```
--- choice
`floor.terminal = True`, which tells the solver to ignore the event.
--- choice correct
`floor.direction = 1`, which counts only crossings where the height is increasing. A falling ball crosses downward, so it must be $-1$ (or 0 for both directions).
--- choice
`return s[0]`: the zero-crossing function must be the velocity, `s[1]`.
--- choice
`rtol=1e-9` is too tight, so the solver steps over the floor.
--- why
The height does change sign, from $+10$ to negative, but only downward. With `direction = 1` the event watches for upward crossings only, so it never fires; the integration runs to 5 s and ends at $10 - \tfrac{1}{2} \times 9.81 \times 25 = -112.6\,\mathrm{m}$. With `direction = -1` the same script stops at 1.4278 s with status 1, "stopped by an event". Height is the right zero-crossing function: it is zero exactly at contact.

+++ question | The worst case of fixed-step event handling
--- ask
A HIL plant model runs at a fixed step of $1\,\mathrm{ms}$ with fixed-step zero-crossing detection. A step with no events takes $0.55\,\mathrm{ms}$. Each bracketing iteration costs $0.03\,\mathrm{ms}$. The solver allows at most 5 bracketing iterations per crossing and at most 2 crossings per step. What is the worst-case time for one step, in milliseconds?
--- answer
0.85
0.85 ms
0.85ms
.85
--- why
The worst step handles the maximum of 2 crossings with the maximum of 5 iterations each: $2 \times 5 \times 0.03 = 0.3\,\mathrm{ms}$ of extra work, so $0.55 + 0.3 = 0.85\,\mathrm{ms}$, inside the 1 ms slot. Those two caps are the whole point of fixed-step detection: the event cost has a known worst case that can be fit into the slot. A variable-step event search has no such cap; a chattering signal could demand any number of extra steps in one slot.

[0.050, 0.010]
--- why
The period is $1/20 = 0.05\,\mathrm{s}$ and the offset is $0.01\,\mathrm{s}$, so the block runs at $t = 0.05\,n + 0.01$: 0.01, 0.06, 0.11 s and so on, always 10 ms after guidance at 0, 0.05, 0.1 s. +++ question | A sample time Simulink rejects
--- ask
Which of these sample times is not allowed?
--- choice
`[0.1, 0.05]`
--- choice
`[0.02, 0]`
--- choice correct
`[0.01, 0.015]`
--- choice
`[0.5, 0.49]`

+++ question | Keeping a filter's time constant at a new rate
--- ask
A smoothing filter $y_n = 0.8\,y_{n-1} + 0.2\,u_n$ was tuned at 50 Hz. It is reused at 200 Hz, and the team wants the same time constant in seconds. What coefficient should replace 0.8? Give three decimal places.
--- answer
0.946
0.9457
0.94574
0.8^(1/4)
0.8^0.25
--- why
At 50 Hz the filter keeps 0.8 per $0.02\,\mathrm{s}$ step, so $\tau = -0.02/\ln 0.8 = 0.0896\,\mathrm{s}$. At 200 Hz a step is $0.005\,\mathrm{s}$, and the same $\tau$ needs $a = e^{-0.005/0.0896} = 0.946$. Equivalently, four 200 Hz steps must keep what one 50 Hz step kept: $a^4 = 0.8$, so $a = 0.8^{1/4}$. Left at 0.8, the filter would respond four times faster. Computing $a$ from the actual sample time inside the subsystem stops an inherited rate from silently retuning it.

+++ question | Base steps that do nothing
--- ask
A model has discrete blocks at periods $0.003\,\mathrm{s}$ and $0.01\,\mathrm{s}$, both with zero offset, and a fixed step left on `auto`. From $t = 0$ up to but not including $t = 0.03\,\mathrm{s}$, how many of the base steps have no block due?
--- answer
18
--- why
The fundamental sample time is the greatest common divisor of 0.003 and 0.01: $0.001\,\mathrm{s}$. The window holds 30 base steps, at 0, 1, …, 29 ms. The 3 ms block is due at 10 of them, the 10 ms block at 3 (0, 10 and 20 ms), and both at $t = 0$, so $10 + 3 - 1 = 12$ steps do work and $30 - 12 = 18$ do none. Choosing periods that divide each other, such as 0.002 and 0.01, keeps the base step large and every tick useful.

+++ question | A fundamental step that forgot the offset

--- why
Slow to fast with deterministic transfer is a unit delay at the slow rate: each guidance output reaches the controller exactly one slow period, $0.1\,\mathrm{s}$, after the instant it was computed for. The controller's ticks from 0.30 to 0.39 s hold the output guidance computed for $t = 0.2\,\mathrm{s}$, which is $c_2$. +++ question | Where a fast-to-slow block runs
--- ask
A Rate Transition with both options checked carries a signal from a 100 Hz controller to a 10 Hz telemetry task, in multitasking mode. How is it scheduled, and what delay does it add?
--- choice
At the fast rate and fast priority, copying every fast sample; the telemetry task sees a value up to 10 ms old.
--- choice correct
At the slow rate but with the fast task's priority: at each slow tick it copies the fast task's value for that instant, before the slow task starts. No delay is added.
--- choice
At the slow rate and slow priority, so it adds one fast period of delay.
--- choice
At the slow rate and slow priority, so it adds one slow period of delay, the same as slow to fast.
--- why
Fast to slow is a zero-order hold. At $t = 0.1\,\mathrm{s}$ the fast task runs first, then the block, which runs only at slow ticks but with the fast priority, copies the value the fast task computed at 0.1 s. The slow task then reads that copy for its whole run, and no fast update can change it midway. Only the slow-to-fast direction pays a delay, one slow period, because the fast task cannot wait for a slow task that has not finished.

+++ question | A copy that may go unprotected

+++ question | Which ring is algebraic
--- ask
Each ring below is a closed feedback loop with a Sum block. Which one is an algebraic loop?
--- choice
Sum, Gain, Transfer Fcn $\frac{s + 2}{s^2 + 3s + 2}$
--- choice correct
Sum, Gain, Transfer Fcn $\frac{3s^2 + 1}{s^2 + s + 1}$, Saturation
--- choice
Sum, Gain, State-Space block with $D = 0$
--- choice
Sum, Gain, Unit Delay
--- why
A loop is algebraic only when every block on it has direct feedthrough. Sum, Gain and Saturation all do. $\frac{3s^2 + 1}{s^2 + s + 1}$ has numerator and denominator of the same degree, so a jump in its input shows up in its output at once: it has feedthrough too, and the ring has no place to start. $\frac{s + 2}{s^2 + 3s + 2}$ is strictly proper, a State-Space block with $D = 0$ computes its output from its state, and a Unit Delay outputs last sample's input, so each of those rings has a block that can go first.

+++ question | A loop with a feedback gain
--- ask
A Sum block computes $e = u - H y$, where a feedback Gain has $H = 0.5$, and a forward Gain computes $y = K e$ with $K = 4$. The loop is solved exactly. What is $y$ when $u = 3$?
--- answer
4
4.0
--- why
Substitute: $y = K(u - H y)$. Move the $y$ terms together: $y + KHy = Ku$, so $y = \frac{K}{1 + KH}\,u = \frac{4}{1 + 2} \times 3 = 4$. Check: $e = 3 - 0.5 \times 4 = 1$, and $4 \times 1 = 4$. Doing the algebra by hand, and putting a single Gain of $K/(1 + KH)$ in place of the ring, is fix 1: exact and free.

+++ question | When the delayed loop stays stable

+++ question | A flat line at a round number
--- ask
A 1 s simulation is profiled. The step size sits flat at exactly $0.02\,\mathrm{s}$ for the whole run, with no rejected steps and no zero crossings. Nobody set a Max step size. What is holding the step?

Stiffness: a fast mode is pinning an explicit solver at its stability limit.

--- choice
Chattering: zero crossings too close together to be counted.

--- why
A stability limit shows up as a flat line with many rejected steps charged to one state, at a value set by the fast eigenvalue, not a round number. +++ question | The processor share of three tasks
--- ask
A flight computer runs three tasks with periods $1\,\mathrm{ms}$, $5\,\mathrm{ms}$ and $50\,\mathrm{ms}$, and measured worst-case times $0.2\,\mathrm{ms}$, $1.5\,\mathrm{ms}$ and $12\,\mathrm{ms}$. What is the utilization, as a decimal?
--- answer
0.74
.74
74%
0.740
--- why
Utilization is the sum of each worst-case time over its period: $U = 0.2/1 + 1.5/5 + 12/50 = 0.20 + 0.30 + 0.24 = 0.74$. The tasks use 74% of the processor. That is under 100% and under the three-task rate-monotonic guarantee of about 78%, so with faster tasks at higher priority every deadline is met. The periods also divide each other ($5/1 = 5$, $50/5 = 10$), so every transfer can be made deterministic.

+++ question | The highest order that fits the slot
--- ask
A plant model runs on a HIL rig at a fixed step of $1\,\mathrm{ms}$. One derivative evaluation takes $90\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ on the real-time computer, and the solver may use at most half of each slot. Of the explicit fixed-step solvers `ode1`, `ode2`, `ode3`, `ode4`, `ode5` and `ode8`, which is the highest-order one that fits? Type its name.
--- answer
ode4
ode 4
--- why
The budget is $500\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, and a fixed-step solver's cost per step is its number of stages times the cost of one. `ode4` needs $4 \times 90 = 360\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ and fits. `ode5` needs 6 stages, $540\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, and `ode8` needs 13, $1170\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, a certain overrun. Because the step is fixed, this can be settled before the rig runs. The step must still be checked for stability against the fastest eigenvalue, and for accuracy against a variable-step reference.

+++ question | ode45's next step after a big miss
--- ask
`ode45` attempts a step of $0.3\,\mathrm{s}$, and its error estimate comes out 243 times the allowed tolerance. Using the lesson-1 step-size rule with its 0.9 safety factor, what step, in seconds, does it try next?
--- answer
0.09
0.09 s
.09
--- why
In the rule $h_{\text{new}} = 0.9\,h\,(\text{tol}/\text{err})^{1/(p+1)}$, $p$ is the order of the lower method of the pair. `ode45` is Dormand–Prince 4(5), so $p = 4$ and the power is $\tfrac{1}{5}$. Since $243 = 3^5$, the step shrinks by 3: $0.9 \times 0.3 / 3 = 0.09\,\mathrm{s}$. The step is rejected and retried from the same time. With the square root of the Euler–Heun pair it would have shrunk by about 15.6 instead, far more than a fifth-order error needs.

+++ question | Two ways to build the same pulse
--- ask
A 250 s simulation runs on `ode45` with default settings. A valve opens for $1\,\mathrm{s}$ at a time chosen by the test. In version A the pulse is computed from the clock inside a MATLAB Function block. In version B it is the sum of two Step blocks, one switching on and one switching off. Which version can miss the pulse entirely?
--- choice
Both: the default Max step size of 5 s can step over any 1 s pulse.
--- choice correct
Only A. The default Max step size is $250/50 = 5\,\mathrm{s}$, and nothing tells the solver where the MATLAB Function's output jumps. The Step blocks have zero-crossing detection, so the solver finds both edges even inside a long step.
--- choice
Only B, because Step blocks switch at major time steps only and a 5 s step can skip the switching time.
--- choice
Neither: a variable-step solver's error control notices any change that lasts a whole second.
--- why
Error control only judges what the solver samples. With a 5 s leash, a quiet stretch lets the solver stride right over a 1 s pulse that no slope sample lands in, and version A's black-box function gives no warning. A Step block registers a zero-crossing function for its switching time, so the solver watches for the sign change and lands on each edge. That is why building events from blocks the solver understands is cheaper than shortening the leash for the whole run.

+++ question | Tightening RelTol did nothing
--- ask
A pointing model's attitude-error Integrator holds values around $2 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{rad}$. The model uses RelTol $10^{-3}$ and AbsTol $10^{-6}$ for every state. The lightly damped jitter mode grows in the plot, which the physics cannot do. Tightening RelTol to $10^{-6}$ changes nothing. Why, and what fixes it?
--- choice
The model is stiff, so the tolerances do not matter; switch to `ode15s`.
--- choice correct
The limit is $\max(\text{RelTol} \cdot |x|, \text{AbsTol})$, and AbsTol wins for this state at either RelTol: $10^{-6}$ is about 5% of the whole signal per step. Give that Integrator its own Absolute tolerance, about $10^{-9}\,\mathrm{rad}$, or rescale it to microradians.
--- choice
RelTol cannot go below $10^{-3}$ in Simulink, so the typed value was ignored.
--- choice
The Max step size is too small; raising it lets the error control work properly.
--- why
At RelTol $10^{-3}$, $\text{RelTol} \cdot |x| = 2 \times 10^{-8}$; at $10^{-6}$ it is $2 \times 10^{-11}$. Both are far below the AbsTol of $10^{-6}$, so the error limit for this state stayed at $10^{-6}$ and the solver kept striding. As in the lesson's solar-array example, big steps on a lightly damped mode can make it grow. An AbsTol below the smallest error worth caring about fixes it without slowing the rest of the model, which a global RelTol change never would.

--- choice

--- choice correct
About 24,000.
--- choice

--- why

+++ question | ode4 on a very fast mode
--- ask
A HIL plant model has a fast mode with $\lambda = -4000\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and runs at a fixed step of $1\,\mathrm{ms}$. Under `ode4`, what number multiplies that mode at every step?
--- answer
5
5.0
+5
--- why
Here $z = h\lambda = -4$, and RK4's growth factor is $R(z) = 1 + z + z^2/2 + z^3/6 + z^4/24 = 1 - 4 + 8 - 10.667 + 10.667 = 5$. The mode is multiplied by 5 every step, so the run explodes; $h|\lambda| = 4$ is past RK4's limit of 2.785. `ode1be` at the same step multiplies it by $1/(1 + 4) = 0.2$, with a fixed number of Newton iterations per step, so it keeps both the stability and the fixed cost the rig needs.

+++ question | A sawtooth on the current
--- ask
A stiff electromechanical actuator model runs on `ode23t`. The motor current shows a fine sawtooth that flips sign every step and fades only slowly, riding on an otherwise sensible curve. Which change removes it?
--- choice correct
Switch to `ode23tb`, whose TR-BDF2 steps strongly damp very fast modes.
--- choice
Switch to `ode45`, because explicit solvers do not ring.
--- choice
Switch to `ode113`, whose high order removes numerical artifacts.
--- choice
Turn zero-crossing detection off, because the sawtooth is chattering.
--- why
This is ringing. The trapezoidal rule is stable, but its factor on a very fast mode is close to $-1$, so the mode flips every step and hardly decays. TR-BDF2 and backward Euler send that factor toward 0, wiping the mode out. An explicit solver would not ring here; it would be pinned to tiny steps or blow up, since the model is stiff. Chattering is a pile-up of zero crossings in a tiny time interval, not a smooth sawtooth over many steps.

+++ question | Dry friction and the Adaptive algorithm
--- ask
A mechanism model uses dry friction whose force is a hard switch on the sign of the velocity. Once the part should be stuck, the run stops with a consecutive zero-crossings error. A colleague sets the zero-crossing Algorithm to Adaptive, and the run finishes. Which statement is right?
--- choice
Adaptive located each crossing more precisely, so the model is now correct.
--- choice
Adaptive raised the consecutive-crossing limit, which is the standard fix.
--- choice correct
Adaptive stopped bracketing the chattering crossings while the velocity sat within the signal threshold, and stepped through them. The run finishes, but the model still has a friction force flipping infinitely fast; the real fix is a stick region, where below a small speed the part stays stuck until the applied force exceeds breakaway.
--- choice
Adaptive switched the solver to a fixed step while the part was stuck.
--- why
The Adaptive algorithm notices a signal flip-flopping in a small band around zero and turns bracketing off for it until it moves clear. That rescues a run that chatters for a harmless reason, but the model is still posed badly: the friction sign flips back and forth on a part that should be held still. A deadband or stick band removes the chattering at its source, the same idea as hysteresis on a relay. Raising the crossing limit would only let the stall run longer.

+++ question | What fixed-step detection keeps
--- ask
You enable zero-crossing detection for a fixed-step real-time plant model. What stays true?
--- choice
Nothing changes: fixed-step solvers can only notice an event at the next step.
--- choice correct
The major steps stay on the same fixed grid. A crossing inside a step is refined with a capped number of bracketing iterations, with a capped number of crossings per step, so each step's worst-case cost is still known.
--- choice
The solver shortens the step to land exactly on the crossing, like a variable-step solver, then returns to the grid.
--- choice
Events are located exactly, at the cost of an unbounded search on the rare steps that contain one.
--- why
Fixed-step detection is a compromise built for real time. The beat never moves, so every step still starts and ends on its tick. The crossing is located only as well as a few bracketing iterations allow, which is less exact than a variable-step search but far better than handling the event a whole step late. The two caps are what keep the cost bounded and deterministic, the promise a HIL slot depends on.

+++ question | Resets at a regular beat
--- ask
A smooth orbit plant runs on `ode113` at RelTol $10^{-8}$, closed around a 50 Hz discrete attitude controller that changes the thruster torque every $0.02\,\mathrm{s}$. The Solver Profiler shows a solver reset every 0.02 s, and the step size never climbs above about 2 ms. What is going on, and what is the sensible change?
--- choice
The model is stiff; switch to `ode15s`.
--- choice
The controller is chattering; add hysteresis to its output.
--- choice correct
Each controller update changes the plant's input, which resets the solver. `ode113` is multistep, so every reset throws away its history and restarts it at low order with small steps. A one-step solver such as `ode45` restarts at full strength and suits this model better.
--- choice
Set Solver reset method to Robust, which makes each reset cheaper.
--- why
Resets at a regular beat are the profiler's sign of a discrete signal feeding a continuous part. They are normal in a hybrid model, but their cost depends on the solver. `ode113` wins on smooth models only after it has built up its history, and a reset every 20 ms never lets it. The Solver reset method applies to the implicit solvers' Jacobians, and Robust makes resets more expensive, not cheaper. Nothing here is stiff or chattering.

+++ question | A step that divides and stays stable
--- ask
A fixed-step model has a discrete controller with sample time `[0.02, 0.005]`. The plant's fastest eigenvalue is $-300\,\mathrm{rad/s}$, and the solver is `ode4`. What is the largest fixed step, in seconds, that is allowed and stable?
--- answer
0.005
0.005 s
5 ms
5ms
.005
--- why
The fixed step must divide both the period and the offset, so it must divide $0.005\,\mathrm{s}$: the candidates are 0.005, 0.0025 s and so on. RK4's limit is $2.785/300 = 0.0093\,\mathrm{s}$, and $0.005 \times 300 = 1.5$ is well inside it. So the offset, not stability, sets the step here. +++ question | What removes an algebraic loop
--- ask
A model has a ring of a Sum, a controller Gain, a lead filter $\frac{s + 1}{0.1s + 1}$, an actuator modeled as a Saturation only, and a sensor Gain back to the Sum. Simulink reports an algebraic loop. Which change removes it?

+++ question | Fixing the rates, then the budget
--- ask
A controller model has tasks at $1\,\mathrm{ms}$, $10\,\mathrm{ms}$ and $25\,\mathrm{ms}$, with worst-case times $0.25\,\mathrm{ms}$, $2.5\,\mathrm{ms}$ and $6\,\mathrm{ms}$. 0.620
--- why
$25/10 = 2.5$ is not whole, so the 25 ms and 10 ms rates cannot exchange data deterministically; 50 ms fixes that, since $50/10 = 5$. Then $U = 0.25/1 + 2.5/10 + 6/50 = 0.25 + 0.25 + 0.12 = 0.62$. +++ question | What the halving test says
--- ask
A fixed-step `ode4` attitude model is compared with an `ode45` run at RelTol $10^{-10}$. At $h = 5\,\mathrm{ms}$ the largest difference is 2 mrad; at $h = 2.5\,\mathrm{ms}$ it is 0.13 mrad. What does this tell you?
--- choice
The fixed-step model is wrong, because it disagrees with the reference at both steps.
--- choice correct
The difference fell about 16 times when the step halved, as fourth order predicts, so it is the fixed step's own truncation error and no corner is being stepped over. Pick the step whose error meets the requirement.
--- choice
An event is being stepped over, since the error changed with the step.
--- choice
The reference is not accurate enough, because a tighter RelTol would have made the two agree.
--- why
$2 / 0.13 \approx 15$, close to $2^4 = 16$: the error shrinks like $h^4$, exactly what `ode4` does on a smooth model. Had it only halved, a corner or event falling inside a step would be the suspect, since a straddled corner makes an error that shrinks only like $h$. A fixed-step solver never reports its own error, so this comparison against a tight variable-step reference, with the step halved, is how you verify it.
