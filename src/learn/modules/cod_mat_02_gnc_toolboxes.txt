=== mat02-l01 | Practice: Describing a system with tf, zpk, ss and frd
--- for
l01-lti-models

+++ question | The right coefficient vector
--- ask
Which call builds the transfer function below?

$$
G(s) = \frac{2s + 6}{s^3 + 4s^2}
$$
--- choice correct
`tf([2 6], [1 4 0 0])`
--- choice
`tf([2 6], [1 4 0])`
--- choice
`tf([6 2], [0 0 4 1])`
--- choice
`zpk(3, [0 0 4], 2)`
--- why
`tf` wants the coefficients highest power first, with every missing power written as a zero. The bottom is $s^3 + 4s^2 + 0s + 0$, a degree-3 polynomial, so it needs four entries: `[1 4 0 0]`. Dropping one zero gives `[1 4 0]`, which is $s^2 + 4s$: a different system, with no error message. Writing the coefficients lowest power first is the order some textbooks use, but not MATLAB's. The `zpk` line has the sign slip: the zero of $2s + 6 = 2(s + 3)$ sits at $s = -3$ and the poles at $0, 0, -4$, so the right call would be `zpk(-3, [0 0 -4], 2)`.

+++ question | Two blocks side by side
--- ask
With `P = tf(2, [1 1])` and `Q = tf(1, [1 4])`, what is the numerator of `parallel(P, Q)`? Type the polynomial, for example `5s + 2`.
--- answer
3s + 9
3s+9
3 s + 9
3*s + 9
3*s+9
9 + 3s
[3 9]
--- why
Parallel means the two outputs are added, so the result is $P + Q$. Over the common denominator $(s + 1)(s + 4) = s^2 + 5s + 4$, the top is $2(s + 4) + 1(s + 1) = 3s + 9$. A series connection would have multiplied instead, giving $\frac{2}{s^2 + 5s + 4}$.

+++ question | A sensor that reads double
--- ask
A turntable's speed loop has forward path `G = tf(5, [1 0])` and a speed sensor that reports twice the true speed, `H = 2`. What does `dcgain(feedback(G, H))` return?
--- answer
0.5
.5
1/2
0.50
--- why
$T = \frac{G}{1 + GH} = \frac{5/s}{1 + 10/s} = \frac{5}{s + 10}$, and at $s = 0$ that is $5/10 = 0.5$. The loop drives the error $R - HY$ to zero, so it settles where the sensor reads the command: $HY = R$, which means $Y = R/2$. A loop can only be as accurate as its sensor. With a perfect sensor, `feedback(G, 1)`, the DC gain would be 1.

+++ question | The unstable pole comes first
--- ask
An engineer puts `G1 = tf(1, [1 -2])` first and `G2 = tf([1 -2], [1 3])` after it. `minreal(G2*G1)` prints $\frac{1}{s + 3}$. What is the truth about the connected hardware?
--- choice
It is stable: after the cancellation only the pole at $-3$ is left.
--- choice correct
It is unstable. The pole at $+2$ is still inside `G1`. The input can push it, so the signal between the two blocks grows like $e^{2t}$, even though `G2`'s zero stops it from reaching the output.
--- choice
It is stable, because the zero comes after the pole, and a zero that follows a pole cancels it in the hardware too.
--- choice
It is unstable, because `G2` has a zero in the right half plane, and a right-half-plane zero makes a system unstable.
--- why
`pole(ss(G2)*ss(G1))` still lists $+2$ and $-3$: the state-space form keeps every mode, as lesson 1 showed. This time the mode is easy to push from the input but cannot be seen at the output, the mirror image of the lesson's example. Either way the cancellation is only algebra. Inside `G1`, the signal feeding `G2` runs away until something saturates or breaks. Zeros never decide stability; poles do.

+++ question | A loop that runs away
--- ask
This should build the same closed loop as `feedback(6*G, 1)`, but its step response grows without limit. Which line is wrong?

```matlab
G = tf(1, [1 3 0]);  G.InputName = 'u';  G.OutputName = 'y';
K = tf(6);           K.InputName = 'e';  K.OutputName = 'u';
S = sumblk('e = r + y');
T = connect(G, K, S, 'r', 'y');
```
--- choice
The `K` line: its output should be named `'e'`, so that it feeds the junction.
--- choice correct
The `sumblk` line: `'e = r + y'` adds the measurement, which is positive feedback. It must be `'e = r - y'`.
--- choice
The `connect` line: `'e'` must also be listed as an input.
--- choice
The `G` line: the plant's input must be named `'r'`, the command.
--- why
The string in `sumblk` is the junction's equation, signs included. With $+y$ the error grows when the output grows, so the loop pushes itself further. The closed loop becomes $\frac{6}{s^2 + 3s - 6}$, with a pole at about $+1.37$. The names are fine: `K` turns the error `e` into the command `u`, `G` turns `u` into `y`, and `connect` only needs the outside input `r` and output `y`.

+++ question | A gyro on an antenna with friction
--- ask
An antenna mount has inertia $J = 2\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}$ and friction torque $b\,\omega$ with $b = 4\,\mathrm{N\,m\,s}$, so $J\dot{\omega} = \tau - b\,\omega$. The state is $\mathbf{x} = [\theta;\ \omega]$, the input is the motor torque $\tau$, and the only sensor is a rate gyro. Which matrices describe it?
--- choice
`A = [0 1; 0 -4]`, `B = [0; 1]`, `C = [0 1]`
--- choice correct
`A = [0 1; 0 -2]`, `B = [0; 0.5]`, `C = [0 1]`
--- choice
`A = [0 1; 0 -2]`, `B = [0; 0.5]`, `C = [1 0]`
--- choice
`A = [0 1; -2 0]`, `B = [0; 0.5]`, `C = [0 1]`
--- why
Divide the physics by $J$: $\dot{\omega} = -\frac{b}{J}\omega + \frac{1}{J}\tau = -2\omega + 0.5\tau$, and $\dot{\theta} = \omega$. A gyro sees the rate, the second state, so $C = [0\ 1]$. The first choice forgot to divide by $J$. `C = [1 0]` would be an angle sensor. `A = [0 1; -2 0]` describes a spring on the angle, which this mount does not have. From torque to rate the transfer function is only $\frac{0.5}{s + 2}$: the angle's pole at $s = 0$ never shows, because no rate measurement can tell where the angle started.

+++ question | Rise time of a lag with a gain
--- ask
What is the 10 to 90 percent rise time of `G = tf(5, [1 4])`, in seconds, to two decimal places?
--- answer
0.55
0.549
0.55 s
0.549 s
.55
--- why
Rewrite it in time-constant form: $\frac{5}{s + 4} = \frac{1.25}{0.25s + 1}$. So $\tau = 0.25\,\mathrm{s}$ and the rise time is $\tau\ln 9 = 2.197 \times 0.25 = 0.549\,\mathrm{s}$. The gain 1.25 only sets the final value. Rise time is measured as a fraction of that final value, so the gain does not change it.

+++ question | Starting one degree off
--- ask
You want to see the antenna loop recover from starting $1^\circ$ off target, at rest, with no command. The loop is stored as a transfer function `T`. Which approach does that correctly?
--- choice
`initial(T, [1; 0])`
--- choice
`initial(ss(T), [1; 0])`, because `ss` puts the states in the order angle, then rate.
--- choice correct
Build `cl = ss(A, B, C, D)` yourself with the state $[\theta;\ \omega]$, then call `initial(cl, [1; 0])`.
--- choice
`impulse(T)`, because a start away from rest is the same as a kick.
--- why
`initial` needs a state-space model, because "where it starts" is a state vector; a transfer function has no states to start from. Converting with `ss(T)` runs, but MATLAB picks the states, and they need not be "angle, then rate", so `[1; 0]` may mean something else. Building the matrices from the physics gives the starting vector a meaning. An impulse is a kick to the input, which starts the rate moving, not the angle offset.

+++ question | Two asserts, one fails
--- ask
A loop is `T = feedback(16*G, 1)` with `G = tf(1, [1 4 0])`. The requirement script runs:

```matlab
S = stepinfo(T);
assert(S.Overshoot <= 10, 'overshoot')
assert(S.SettlingTime <= 3, 'settling')
```

What happens?
--- choice
Both asserts pass silently.
--- choice correct
The overshoot assert stops the script. The overshoot is about 16 percent, while the settling time, about 2 s, would pass.
--- choice
The settling assert stops the script. The settling time is about 4 s.
--- choice
Both asserts fail.
--- why
The closed loop is $\frac{16}{s^2 + 4s + 16}$, so $\omega_n = 4\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ and $2\zeta\omega_n = 4$ gives $\zeta = 0.5$. That damping always overshoots by $e^{-\pi \times 0.5/0.866} = 16.3\%$, whatever $\omega_n$ is. The settling estimate is $4/(\zeta\omega_n) = 4/2 = 2\,\mathrm{s}$, and `stepinfo` reports about 2.02 s. The first `assert` that fails stops the script, so the second one never runs.

+++ question | A step that starts at 2
--- ask
A flight log `y`, with times `t`, records a pitch channel stepping from 2 degrees to a new steady value of 3 degrees. Its peak is 3.2 degrees. Which call reports the true overshoot, and what is it?
--- choice
`stepinfo(y, t)`, which reports about 20 percent.
--- choice correct
`stepinfo(y, t, 3, 2)`, which reports 20 percent: the swing past 3 is 0.2 on a step of size 1.
--- choice
`stepinfo(y, t, 2, 3)`, which reports 20 percent.
--- choice
`stepinfo(y/3, t)`, which scales the response so the final value is 1.
--- why
Overshoot is measured against the size of the step, from the starting value to the final value. Here that step is $3 - 2 = 1$, and the peak goes $0.2$ past the final value: 20 percent. `stepinfo(y, t)` assumes the step started at zero, so it measures against the wrong baseline. The optional inputs come in the order final value, then starting value, so `(2, 3)` swaps them. Dividing by 3 moves the final value to 1 but leaves the start at $2/3$, so the baseline is still wrong.

+++ question | A short thruster firing
--- ask
A satellite with $J = 10\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}$ has `P = tf(1, [10 0 0])` from torque to angle. A thruster pair gives $0.5\,\mathrm{N\,m}$ for $0.1\,\mathrm{s}$, much shorter than the satellite's motion. The script plots `impulse(P)` as the angle after the firing. What is wrong?
--- choice
Nothing. The firing is short, so `impulse(P)` is the answer as it stands.
--- choice correct
`impulse` answers a kick of total size 1. This firing's size is $0.5 \times 0.1 = 0.05\,\mathrm{N\,m\,s}$, so the curve must be multiplied by 0.05.
--- choice
The curve must be multiplied by 0.5, the torque, because only the strength of the kick matters.
--- choice
A firing must be simulated with `step`, because the thruster pushes with a constant torque.
--- why
An impulse is a push whose strength times duration is 1. A system that is linear answers a push twice the size with twice the output, so a kick of size 0.05 gives 0.05 times the impulse response. That works as long as the firing is much shorter than the response. `step` would model a torque that stays on forever. Here the impulse response of $\frac{1}{10s^2}$ is the ramp $t/10$, so the satellite turns at $0.005\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ after the firing.

=== mat02-l03 | Practice: Poles, zeros and the root locus

+++ question | The frequency column of damp
--- ask
`damp(G)` for `G = tf(34, [1 6 34])` prints one row per pole. What does its frequency column show, in rad/s, to two decimal places?
--- answer
5.83
5.831
sqrt(34)
5.83 rad/s
--- why
The poles are the roots of $s^2 + 6s + 34$: $s = -3 \pm 5j$. `damp` reports the natural frequency $\omega_n = |p| = \sqrt{3^2 + 5^2} = \sqrt{34} = 5.83\,\mathrm{rad/s}$, not the imaginary part 5. The imaginary part is the wiggle frequency $\omega_d$. The damping column shows $\zeta = 3/5.83 = 0.51$, and the time constant column shows $1/3 = 0.33\,\mathrm{s}$.

+++ question | Which one starts the wrong way
--- ask
Each model below has a final value above zero after a unit step. Which one first moves below zero?
--- choice
`(s + 2)/((s + 1)*(s + 3))`
--- choice correct
`(2 - s)/((s + 1)*(s + 3))`
--- choice
`2/((s + 1)*(s + 3))`
--- choice
`(s + 2)/((s + 1)*(s + 4))`
--- why
A wrong-way start comes from a zero in the right half plane, and only $2 - s$ is zero at a positive value, $s = +2$. Its poles, $-1$ and $-3$, are stable, so it still settles, at $\frac{2}{3}$, after first dipping below zero. That makes it non-minimum phase, and `stepinfo` would report a nonzero `Undershoot`. The other two models with zeros have them at $s = -2$, in the left half plane, which speeds the response up and can add overshoot but never reverses the start.

+++ question | A gain for 16 percent overshoot
--- ask
For the open loop $L(s) = \frac{1}{s(s + 6)}$ with a gain $K$ in front and unity negative feedback, what value of $K$ gives a closed-loop step overshoot of about 16 percent?
--- answer
36
K = 36
k=36
--- why
From lesson 2, 16.3 percent overshoot means $\zeta = 0.5$. The closed-loop polynomial is $s^2 + 6s + K$, so $\omega_n = \sqrt{K}$ and $2\zeta\omega_n = 6$, which gives $\zeta = 3/\sqrt{K}$. Setting $\zeta = 0.5$ gives $\sqrt{K} = 6$, so $K = 36$. The poles are then $-3 \pm 5.20j$: on the locus's vertical line at $-3$, where every gain above 9 lands.

+++ question | How much gain a wrong-way zero allows
--- ask
The open loop is $L(s) = \frac{2 - s}{s(s + 3)}$, with a gain $K$ in front and unity negative feedback. Above what value of $K$ does the closed loop go unstable?
--- answer
3
K = 3
k=3
--- why
The closed-loop polynomial is $s(s + 3) + K(2 - s) = s^2 + (3 - K)s + 2K$. For a second-order polynomial, both poles are stable exactly when every coefficient is positive, and the middle one, $3 - K$, turns negative once $K > 3$. At $K = 3$ the poles sit on the imaginary axis at $\pm j\sqrt{6} = \pm 2.45j$, and the loop oscillates at 2.45 rad/s. The zero at $+2$ is where one branch of the root locus must end, so more gain drags the poles across into the right half plane.

+++ question | A damping ratio that comes out negative
--- ask
A script checks the damping of a closed loop whose poles are $-1 \pm 1.73j$, and it prints `zeta = -0.5`. Which line is wrong?

```matlab
p    = pole(T);
wn   = abs(p);
zeta = real(p)./abs(p);
fprintf('zeta = %.2f\n', zeta(1));
```
--- choice
Line 1: `pole` returns the zeros when the model is a transfer function.
--- choice
Line 2: the natural frequency is the imaginary part, `imag(p)`.
--- choice correct
Line 3: the damping ratio is `-real(p)./abs(p)`. A stable pole has a negative real part, so without the minus sign the answer comes out negative.
--- choice
Line 4: `%.2f` prints the magnitude with the wrong sign.
--- why
$\zeta = -\operatorname{Re}(p)/|p|$. For $p = -1 + 1.73j$, $|p| = 2$ and $\zeta = 1/2 = 0.5$. The minus sign is there because stable poles sit to the left, where the real part is negative. `abs(p)` is right for the natural frequency. `[wn, zeta] = damp(T)` does the same job with no sign to forget.

+++ question | A fast loop around a wrong-way zero
--- ask
Because of where the gyro sits relative to a bending mode's shape, the plant from nozzle to gyro has a zero at $s = +4\,\mathrm{rad/s}$. The team wants a loop crossover of 6 rad/s. What is the sound response?
--- choice
Add a controller pole at $s = +4$ to cancel the zero, then tune for 6 rad/s.
--- choice
Raise the loop gain until the crossover reaches 6 rad/s; margins can be added afterwards.
--- choice correct
Accept a crossover below about 2 rad/s, or move the gyro, or measure elsewhere, so that the zero moves or disappears.
--- choice
Add a controller zero at $s = -4$, which mirrors the plant's zero and removes the lag it adds.
--- why
A right-half-plane zero is part of the plant, and no controller can undo its wrong-way start or its extra lag. The rule of thumb keeps crossover below about half the zero's frequency: 2 rad/s here. Canceling it with an unstable controller pole is lesson 1's hidden-mode trap: the pole at $+4$ would still be inside the controller, growing. A mirror zero at $-4$ adds phase lead but cannot remove the lag of the zero at $+4$. Where the sensor sits decides whether the zero exists at all.

+++ question | Gain margin from a Nyquist plot

The loop's Nyquist curve crosses the negative real axis at $-0.4$, and nowhere else. What is the gain margin in dB, to one decimal place?

--- why
At that crossing the phase is $-180^\circ$ and the gain is 0.4. So the gain could grow by a factor of $1/0.4 = 2.5$ before the curve reached $-1$. In decibels, $20\log_{10} 2.5 = 7.96\,\mathrm{dB}$, about 8 dB. That clears a 6 dB requirement, which would be a factor of about 2.

+++ question | Phase at the corner of a lag
--- ask
What is the phase of $G(s) = \frac{20}{s(s + 4)}$ at $\omega = 4\,\mathrm{rad/s}$, in degrees?
--- answer
-135
-135 degrees
-135 deg
-135°
--- why
Phases of blocks in a chain add. The integrator $\frac{1}{s}$ adds $-90^\circ$ at every frequency. The lag $\frac{1}{s + 4}$ adds $-45^\circ$ exactly at its corner, $\omega = 4$. +++ question | The antenna loop's phase margin
--- ask
The antenna loop from lesson 1, $L(s) = \frac{4}{s(s + 2)}$, has its gain crossover at $\omega_c = 1.572\,\mathrm{rad/s}$. What is its phase margin, in degrees, to one decimal place?
--- answer
51.8
51.8 degrees
51.8 deg
51.83
52
--- why
$\text{PM} = 180^\circ + \angle L(j\omega_c)$. The phase is $-90^\circ$ from the integrator plus $-\arctan(1.572/2) = -38.2^\circ$ from the lag, $-128.2^\circ$ in all, so $\text{PM} = 51.8^\circ$. +++ question | An infinite gain margin
--- ask
`[Gm, Pm] = margin(L)` for $L(s) = \frac{4}{s(s + 2)}$ returns `Gm = Inf`. What does that mean, and what happens after a 50 ms delay is added?
--- choice
The loop is unstable: an infinite number means the margin could not be computed.
--- choice correct
The phase never reaches $-180^\circ$, so in the model no gain increase makes it unstable. With the delay, the phase keeps falling as frequency rises, it does cross $-180^\circ$, and `Gm` becomes finite.
--- choice
The delay changes nothing, because a delay has a gain of 1 at every frequency.
--- choice
`Gm = Inf` means the phase margin is also infinite.
--- why
The phase of this loop goes from $-90^\circ$ toward $-180^\circ$ but never reaches it, so there is no phase crossover, and `margin` reports `Inf`. A delay has no gain, but its lag $\omega T$ grows without limit, so it pushes the phase through $-180^\circ$ at some finite frequency, and the gain margin becomes finite. Every real loop has some delay, so an infinite gain margin never survives contact with hardware.

+++ question | A delay margin from the wrong frequency
--- ask
This script should compute the delay margin, the largest extra delay the loop can take before its phase margin hits zero. Which line is wrong?

```matlab
[Gm, Pm, Wcg, Wcp] = margin(L);
PMrad = deg2rad(Pm);
Tmax  = PMrad/Wcg;
fprintf('delay margin %.0f ms\n', 1000*Tmax);
```
--- choice
Line 1: `margin` returns the phase margin in radians already.
--- choice
Line 2: the phase margin should stay in degrees, since `fprintf` prints milliseconds.
--- choice correct
Line 3: it divides by `Wcg`, the phase crossover. The phase margin is measured at the gain crossover, `Wcp`, so it must be `PMrad/Wcp`.
--- choice
Line 4: the factor 1000 turns milliseconds into seconds, so it should divide.
--- why
The delay margin is $T_{\max} = \text{PM}/\omega_c$, where $\omega_c$ is the gain crossover, the frequency where the phase margin is measured. `margin` names that frequency `Wcp`, for "phase margin", and names the phase crossover `Wcg`, for "gain margin". The names describe which margin is read there, not which curve crosses. Dividing by the wrong one gives a delay margin that can be far too small or far too large.

+++ question | Designing a notch for a bending peak
--- ask
With the rigid-body loop done, $|L|$ peaks at $+8\,\mathrm{dB}$ at a 45 rad/s bending mode. You want that peak at least 10 dB below 0 dB. With a notch centered at 45 rad/s and $\zeta_p = 0.4$, what is the largest $\zeta_z$ you may use? Give two decimal places.

0.05
0.050
0.0504
.05

+++ question | What the flight computer's timing costs
--- ask
A pitch loop crosses over at 8 rad/s. It runs at 100 Hz, and the command computed from tick $k$ goes out at tick $k + 1$. Adding the zero-order hold's lag to that one-tick wait, about how much phase margin do the two cost together, in degrees?

+++ question | A notch close to the Nyquist frequency
--- ask
A continuous notch sits at 100 rad/s, and it is discretized with `c2d(N, 0.02, 'tustin')`, with no prewarping. At what frequency does the digital notch land, in rad/s, to one decimal place?
--- answer
78.5
78.54
78.5 rad/s
--- why
Tustin moves a frequency $\omega_a$ to $\omega_d = \frac{2}{T_s}\arctan\left(\frac{\omega_a T_s}{2}\right) = 100\arctan(1) = 100 \times 0.7854 = 78.5\,\mathrm{rad/s}$. +++ question | A stability test that lies
--- ask
A script checks discrete controllers before they go into flight code. It passes a controller whose output grows every tick. Which line is wrong?

```matlab
Cd = c2d(C, 0.02, 'tustin');
p  = pole(Cd);
isStable = all(real(p) < 0);
assert(isStable, 'unstable controller')
```
--- choice
Line 1: Tustin can turn a stable controller into an unstable one.
--- choice
Line 2: `pole` returns the continuous poles of a discrete model.
--- choice correct
Line 3: the test for a discrete model is `all(abs(p) < 1)`. A negative real part says nothing about a pole in $z$.
--- choice
Line 4: `assert` needs a number, not a true or false value.
--- why
A left-half-plane pole in $s$ lands inside the unit circle in $z$, so the discrete rule is $|z| < 1$. The transpose fixes a different mismatch, what the matrix means, and only after the order is right. On SciPy 1.14 or later, `R.from_quat(q, scalar_first=True)` also works.

120 degrees
120 deg
120°
--- why

+++ question | Overshoot from the locus
--- ask
The open loop $L(s) = \frac{10}{s(s + 2)}$ is closed with unity negative feedback. What percent overshoot does `stepinfo(feedback(L, 1))` report, to one decimal place?
--- answer

--- why
The closed-loop polynomial is $s^2 + 2s + 10$, with poles $-1 \pm 3j$. So $\omega_n = \sqrt{10} = 3.16$ and $\zeta = 1/\sqrt{10} = 0.316$. +++ question | A controller that cancels an unstable pole
--- ask
The plant is $P = \frac{1}{s - 1}$. A designer picks $C = \frac{s - 1}{s(s + 4)}$ so that the unstable pole cancels. `minreal(C*P)` gives $\frac{1}{s(s + 4)}$, and `pole(feedback(minreal(C*P), 1))` shows $-0.27$ and $-3.73$. What is the truth?

+++ question | Gravity for a rotating-frame simulation
--- ask
A launch simulation integrates $\ddot{\mathbf{r}} = \mathbf{F}/m - 2\,\boldsymbol{\omega}_E \times \dot{\mathbf{r}} - \boldsymbol{\omega}_E \times (\boldsymbol{\omega}_E \times \mathbf{r})$ in ECEF. Which gravity should go into $\mathbf{F}/m$?
--- choice
`gravitywgs84`, because it is the WGS84 gravity that matches the ECEF frame.
--- choice correct
The gravitational pull alone, for example from `gravitysphericalharmonic`, because the centrifugal term is already in the equation.
--- choice
A constant $9.80665\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ pointing to Earth's center, because the rotating terms correct the rest.
--- choice
`gravitywgs84` plus the centrifugal term a second time, for accuracy.
--- why
`gravitywgs84` returns normal gravity: gravitation minus the spin's outward push, about $0.034\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ less at the equator. This equation already carries that push in its centrifugal term, so normal gravity would count it twice. The pull alone, point mass plus $J_2$ or a spherical-harmonic model, is what belongs there. A constant pull toward the center ignores both the bulge and the fall of gravity with height.

+++ question | Gusts that come out twice as strong
--- ask
A Dryden simulation at 500 ft produces vertical gusts with a standard deviation near 1.5 m/s, where about 0.77 m/s was expected for light turbulence. Which line is wrong?

```matlab
W20  = 15;                               % 20 ft wind from the forecast, knots
h_ft = 500;
sw   = 0.1*W20;                          % vertical intensity, m/s
Lw   = convlength(h_ft, 'ft', 'm');      % vertical scale length, m
n    = sqrt(pi/dt)*randn(size(t));       % white noise of unit strength
```
--- choice
Line 4: the scale length must stay in feet.
--- choice
Line 5: the factor `sqrt(pi/dt)` doubles the gusts and should be removed.
--- choice correct
Line 1: the knots are used as m/s. It needs `W20 = convvel(15, 'kts', 'm/s')`, which is 7.72 m/s.
--- choice
Line 3: the intensity is $0.1 W_{20}$ only above 2,000 ft.
--- why
15 knots is 7.72 m/s, so $\sigma_w = 0.1 \times 7.72 = 0.77\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Treating the number 15 as m/s makes the gusts $1/0.5144 = 1.94$ times too strong. The filters take SI lengths and speeds, so the scale length in meters is right; only the height inside the scale-length formulas stays in feet. The noise factor is what makes the output's standard deviation match $\sigma$ at any time step. Convert once, at the edge, and check the simulated standard deviation.

+++ question | Passes for a station north of the orbit
--- ask
A scenario holds `satellite(sc, 6978137, 0, 45, 0, 0, 0)` and a ground station at $60^\circ$ N with `MinElevationAngle=10`. For that orbit and mask, the visibility circle's central angle is $\lambda = 15.8^\circ$. What will `accessIntervals` show over a day?
--- choice
An empty table: a $45^\circ$ orbit never passes north of $45^\circ$.
--- choice correct
Some short, low passes: the station is $15^\circ$ north of the track's highest latitude, just inside $15.8^\circ$.
--- choice
Passes straight overhead twice a day, since Earth's turning brings the station under the track.
--- choice
Nothing until the orbit's altitude is raised: 6,978,137 m is below the surface.
--- why
The first element is the semi-major axis from Earth's center, so this is a 600 km orbit, well above the surface. Its ground track tops out at $45^\circ$. The satellite is visible whenever the point below it lies within $15.8^\circ$ of the station, and $60^\circ - 45^\circ = 15^\circ$ just fits. So the station sees the satellite only near the top of its track, low on the southern horizon, for a few minutes. The sample time must be short enough not to miss such grazing passes.

+++ question | A bending mode above the Nyquist frequency
--- ask
A structures report lists the first bending mode at 12 Hz with 1 percent damping. The attitude loop runs at 20 Hz. Which statement is right?
--- choice
The mode is at $2\pi \times 12 = 75\,\mathrm{rad/s}$, well below $\pi/T_s = 157\,\mathrm{rad/s}$, so a prewarped Tustin notch at 75 rad/s handles it.
--- choice correct
The mode lies above the 10 Hz Nyquist frequency, so the samples show it disguised as a slower wave inside the loop's band. No digital notch can sit at 12 Hz; filter it before sampling with an analog filter, or sample faster.
--- choice
A lightly damped mode above the Nyquist frequency is invisible to a digital controller and can be ignored.
--- choice
`damp` would report the mode at 12, so it must be compared with the 20 Hz rate directly: it is safely below.
--- why
At 20 Hz, $T_s = 0.05\,\mathrm{s}$ and the Nyquist frequency is 10 Hz, or $\pi/0.05 = 62.8\,\mathrm{rad/s}$; 157 rad/s belongs to a 50 Hz loop. A 12 Hz mode is faster than the samples can show, so it aliases: it appears as a slower wave and can drive the loop. A digital filter only exists up to the Nyquist frequency, so the fix happens before sampling, or with a faster rate. `damp` reports rad/s, 75.4 here, so every comparison needs the units written down.

+++ question | PI gains for a chosen crossover
--- ask
At $\omega = 2\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ the plant is worth $G(2j) = 0.25$ at an angle of $-100^\circ$. You want a PI controller that puts the gain crossover at 2 rad/s with a $55^\circ$ phase margin. What is $K_p$, to two decimal places?
--- answer
3.63
3.625
3.62
--- why
At crossover the loop must be $L(2j) = 1$ at an angle of $-180^\circ + 55^\circ = -125^\circ$. So $C(2j) = L/G$ has size $1/0.25 = 4$ and angle $-125^\circ - (-100^\circ) = -25^\circ$: $4\cos 25^\circ - 4j\sin 25^\circ = 3.625 - 1.690j$. A PI controller is $K_p - jK_i/\omega$, so $K_p = 3.63$ and $K_i = 2 \times 1.690 = 3.38$. `margin(pid(3.63, 3.38)*G)` would confirm $55^\circ$ at 2 rad/s.
