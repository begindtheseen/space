--- teach
This course puts the three SQL courses to work. First, three databases built step by step, each lesson carrying on from the last; then three capstones, where you design everything yourself from a written specification.

**Project one: an online store's database**, from an empty file to the reports the owners read every Monday. Four lessons: the schema, loading the data, a revenue report, a customer report.

The shop has been running on spreadsheets. Its export is already here as `import_rows`: one row per order line, with the customer and product repeated on every line (you will load it next lesson). First, design where it will live. Four tables, each about one thing:

- `customers` — who buys
- `products` — what is sold
- `orders` — one purchase: who, when, what state it is in
- `order_items` — one product within one order: how many, at what price

Decisions worth making on purpose:

- **Money in integer cents.** `0.1 + 0.2` is not `0.3` in floating point, and sums of prices drift by fractions of a cent. Store `price_cents INTEGER`, and divide by 100.0 only for display.
- **The price charged lives on the order line** (`unit_cents`), separately from today's list price on `products`. Prices change; history must not.
- **Natural keys stay unique.** The database assigns each customer an `id`, but an email may still belong to only one customer, and a SKU to one product — say so with `UNIQUE`.
- **States are a closed list.** `CHECK (status IN (...))` stops `'Shipped '` and `'shiped'` from ever being stored.
- **One line per product per order.** A composite primary key `(order_id, product_id)`; buying two mugs is `qty = 2`, not two lines.
- **Children go with their parent.** Deleting an order should delete its lines (`ON DELETE CASCADE`), but deleting a product that has been sold should be refused (the default).

--- teach
The empty tables from last lesson are here, next to `import_rows`, the spreadsheet export. Loading it is `INSERT … SELECT`, one table at a time, **parents before children** so every foreign key has something to point at. Before writing any of it, **look at the data**. `SELECT * FROM import_rows` shows every problem you have to handle:

- **Emails with stray capitals and spaces**: `'BO@mail.com '` and `' ana@mail.com'`. Normalise with `lower(trim(email))` and group by that.
- **Statuses in mixed case**: `'SHIPPED'`, `'Paid'`. `lower(trim(status))`, or the `CHECK` you wrote will (rightly) refuse the row.
- **Prices as text in dollars.** Convert to cents — and watch floating point: `89.99 * 100` is `8998.999999999999`, and `CAST(… AS INTEGER)` truncates to 8998. **Round first**: `CAST(ROUND(CAST(price AS REAL) * 100) AS INTEGER)`.
- **An exact duplicate line** (order 1003's notebooks appear twice — the export double-counted). `SELECT DISTINCT` drops it; the primary key would refuse it anyway.
- **A product sold at two prices.** The headphones went for 89.99 and, on sale, 79.99. Each line keeps what it was charged; the product's list price is the highest price it has sold at.

Grouping by the normalised email gives one row per customer, but the name and country columns are neither grouped nor aggregated. Every row of a customer carries the same name, so `MIN(customer_name)` is an honest way to pick it — an aggregate, so the query is valid in any database.

Then the linking. `orders` needs a `customer_id`, which the export does not have — it has an email. Join back to the customers you just inserted on the normalised email to find the id. `order_items` finds its `product_id` the same way, through the SKU. The order's own id can simply be the export's `order_ref`.

Finish by reconciling: the total of the lines you loaded must equal the total in the export, minus the duplicate. Numbers that do not reconcile are a bug in the load, not a rounding curiosity.
--- task
Load `import_rows` into the four tables:

- one customer per normalised email (`lower(trim(email))`), with their name and country
- one product per SKU, with `price_cents` the highest price it sold at, in cents
- one order per `order_ref` (use it as the order `id`), linked to its customer, with the status lower-cased and trimmed
- one line per order and product, with `unit_cents` the price on that line in cents — the duplicate line loaded once
--- starter
INSERT INTO customers (email, name, country)
SELECT email, customer_name, country FROM import_rows;
--- solution
INSERT INTO customers (email, name, country)
SELECT lower(trim(email)), MIN(customer_name), MIN(country)
FROM import_rows
GROUP BY lower(trim(email))
ORDER BY MIN(order_ref);

INSERT INTO products (sku, name, category, price_cents)
SELECT sku, MIN(product_name), MIN(category), MAX(CAST(ROUND(CAST(price AS REAL) * 100) AS INTEGER))
FROM import_rows
GROUP BY sku
ORDER BY MIN(order_ref), sku;

INSERT INTO orders (id, customer_id, placed, status)
SELECT DISTINCT r.order_ref, c.id, r.placed, lower(trim(r.status))
FROM import_rows r
JOIN customers c ON c.email = lower(trim(r.email));

INSERT INTO order_items (order_id, product_id, qty, unit_cents)
SELECT DISTINCT r.order_ref, p.id, r.qty, CAST(ROUND(CAST(r.price AS REAL) * 100) AS INTEGER)
FROM import_rows r
JOIN products p ON p.sku = r.sku;
--- hint
Customers: `SELECT lower(trim(email)), MIN(customer_name), MIN(country) FROM import_rows GROUP BY lower(trim(email))`. Products the same way, grouped by SKU, with `MAX(...)` of the price in cents.
--- hint
Cents: `CAST(ROUND(CAST(price AS REAL) * 100) AS INTEGER)` — without `ROUND`, 89.99 becomes 8998.
--- hint
Orders and lines: `SELECT DISTINCT … FROM import_rows r JOIN customers c ON c.email = lower(trim(r.email))`, and `JOIN products p ON p.sku = r.sku` for the lines.

--- teach
The store is loaded. (Its tables are here with the data from last lesson.) Now the owners' first question: **where does the money come from, month by month?**

A report like this is a pipeline, and CTEs are its stages:

1. **Line revenue** — join items to orders and products, drop refunded orders, compute `qty * unit_cents` per line, and tag each line with its month and category.
2. **Aggregate** — sum per month and category.
3. **Compare** — each category's share of its month, with a window function: `SUM(revenue) OVER (PARTITION BY month)` puts the month's total on every row of that month, without collapsing them.

Keep the arithmetic in integer cents until the very end, then convert once: `ROUND(cents / 100.0, 2)`. Converting early and then summing reintroduces the floating-point drift you designed away.

Refunds: a refunded order is money you gave back. Excluding it is the simplest honest choice for a revenue report; a finance report would show it as a negative line instead. Either way, decide it explicitly and say so in the column name or the report title — silent inclusion is how two dashboards end up disagreeing.
--- task
Return revenue by month and category, excluding refunded orders: `month` (`'YYYY-MM'`), `category`, `revenue` (in dollars, rounded to 2 decimals) and `share` (the category's percentage of that month's revenue, rounded to 1 decimal). Sort by month, then revenue from highest to lowest.
--- starter
SELECT strftime('%Y-%m', o.placed) AS month, SUM(oi.qty * oi.unit_cents) AS revenue
FROM orders o
JOIN order_items oi ON oi.order_id = o.id
GROUP BY month;
--- solution
WITH lines AS (
  SELECT strftime('%Y-%m', o.placed) AS month, p.category, oi.qty * oi.unit_cents AS cents
  FROM order_items oi
  JOIN orders o   ON o.id = oi.order_id
  JOIN products p ON p.id = oi.product_id
  WHERE o.status <> 'refunded'
), totals AS (
  SELECT month, category, SUM(cents) AS cents
  FROM lines
  GROUP BY month, category
)
SELECT month, category,
       ROUND(cents / 100.0, 2) AS revenue,
       ROUND(100.0 * cents / SUM(cents) OVER (PARTITION BY month), 1) AS share
FROM totals
ORDER BY month, revenue DESC;
--- hint
First CTE: one row per line with its month, category and `qty * unit_cents`, refunded orders filtered out. Second: sum per month and category.
--- hint
`share` is `100.0 * cents / SUM(cents) OVER (PARTITION BY month)`. Convert cents to dollars only in the final `SELECT`.

The second Monday question: **who are our customers, and how much are they worth?** The tables are the same as last lesson, plus Fay, who signed up yesterday and has not ordered yet.

A customer report has to include **every** customer — including the ones who have not bought, because "signed up but never bought" is exactly who marketing wants to reach. That decides the shape: start from `customers` and LEFT JOIN everything else.

Three traps, each from an earlier course:

- **Condition placement.** Refunded orders must not count, but a customer whose only order was refunded must still appear. The status filter goes in the LEFT JOIN's `ON`.
- **Fan-out.** Joining orders to their lines makes one row per *line*, so `COUNT(o.id)` counts lines. Count orders with `COUNT(DISTINCT o.id)`, or pre-aggregate each order's total in a CTE first.
- **NULL is not zero.** A customer with no orders has `SUM(...)` = NULL. `COALESCE(…, 0)` makes the report say what it means.

Then a **segment**, so the numbers turn into action. A `CASE` on lifetime spend: big spenders are `'vip'`, anyone who has spent something is `'active'`, everyone else `'inactive'`. Write the boundaries into the task, and test the edges.
--- task
Return one row per customer (all of them): `email`, `orders` (their non-refunded orders), `spent` (total of those orders in dollars, rounded to 2 decimals; 0 if none), `last_order` (date of the latest non-refunded order as `'YYYY-MM-DD'`, or NULL) and `segment` — `'vip'` for spend of 100 dollars or more, `'active'` for any spend above 0, else `'inactive'`. Sort by spent, highest first, then email.
--- starter
SELECT c.email, COUNT(o.id) AS orders
FROM customers c
JOIN orders o ON o.customer_id = c.id
GROUP BY c.id;
--- solution
WITH order_totals AS (
  SELECT o.id, o.customer_id, o.placed, SUM(oi.qty * oi.unit_cents) AS cents
  FROM orders o
  JOIN order_items oi ON oi.order_id = o.id
  WHERE o.status <> 'refunded'
  GROUP BY o.id
), per_customer AS (
  SELECT c.email,
         COUNT(t.id) AS orders,
         COALESCE(SUM(t.cents), 0) AS cents,
         date(MAX(t.placed)) AS last_order
  FROM customers c
  LEFT JOIN order_totals t ON t.customer_id = c.id
  GROUP BY c.id
)
SELECT email, orders,
       ROUND(cents / 100.0, 2) AS spent,
       last_order,
       CASE WHEN cents >= 10000 THEN 'vip'
            WHEN cents > 0 THEN 'active'
            ELSE 'inactive' END AS segment
FROM per_customer
ORDER BY spent DESC, email;

--- hint

--- hint
The segment is a `CASE` on cents: `>= 10000` is vip, `> 0` is active.

--- teach
Last lesson finished the online store with a report on every customer. Now the second project begins: **product analytics** for an app. The app sends a small message every time a user does something, such as `visit` (opened the app), `signup` or `activate` (started really using it). Each message is an **event**: one thing one user did, at one moment. The team wants three numbers from them: daily active users, a signup funnel, and retention. That makes four lessons, and this first one gets the data in.

### The table

Events arrive in `raw_events` exactly as the phones and browsers sent them. It has three columns:

- `id`: the row's number, in the order the rows arrived;
- `received_at`: when the server got the message;
- `body`: the message itself, as [[JSON|json-text]] text.

Here are a few of the ten bodies:

```
id  body
1   {"event_id": "e1", "user_id": 1, "name": "visit", "ts": "2024-07-01 09:00:00"}
5   not json at all
7   {"event_id": "e5", "user_id": "3", "name": "Visit", "ts": "2024-07-02 08:00:00"}
10  {"user_id": 4, "name": "visit", "ts": "2024-07-03 09:00:00"}
```

Each good body has four keys: `event_id` (the event's own id, made by the app), `user_id`, `name` and `ts` (the time it happened). Row 9 also carries an extra `props` key, which this project does not need.

### The picture

Think of a post room. Letters pour in all day. A few are torn and unreadable. Some have no name on them. One was sent twice. Before anyone delivers anything, a sorter checks each letter, fixes what can be fixed, and puts only good ones in the pigeonholes. The pigeonholes are built so that a bad letter does not fit.

Loading raw data into a clean table is called **ingest**. It is the first step of every [[data pipeline|pipeline]]. The rule of an ingest step has two halves:

1. The **output table** has constraints strict enough that bad data **cannot** land in it.
2. The **query** does the cleaning that makes good data fit.

The clean table is `events`, with `event_id` as its primary key, and `user_id`, `name` and `ts` all `NOT NULL`. Now read the raw rows. They have five kinds of problem.

### Problem 1: a body that is not JSON

Row 5's body is `not json at all`. The functions that read JSON stop with an error when they meet it. This query tries to read every row's name:

```sql
SELECT body ->> '$.name' FROM raw_events;
```

```
Error: malformed JSON
```

One bad row, and the whole query fails. `->>`, from the lesson "JSON columns", reads one key out of the JSON as a plain value.

The fix is a new function. `json_valid(body)` gives 1 when `body` is well-formed JSON and 0 when it is not. It never errors, so you can test it first:

```sql
SELECT id, json_valid(body) AS ok FROM raw_events WHERE id IN (4, 5, 6);
```

| id | ok |
| --- | --- |
| 4 | 1 |
| 5 | 0 |
| 6 | 1 |

Put `json_valid(body)` first in the `WHERE`, and the bad row is dropped before anything else reads it.

### Problem 2: missing required fields

A key that is not in the JSON reads as NULL. Here are the ids of every valid row:

```sql
SELECT id, body ->> '$.event_id' AS event_id, body ->> '$.user_id' AS user_id
FROM raw_events
WHERE json_valid(body);
```

| id | event_id | user_id |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | e1 | 1 |
| 2 | e2 | 1 |
| 3 | e2 | 1 |
| 4 | e3 | 2 |
| 6 | e4 | NULL |
| 7 | e5 | 3 |
| 8 | e6 | 2 |
| 9 | e7 | 1 |
| 10 | NULL | 4 |

Row 6 has no `user_id`, and row 10 has no `event_id`. An event you cannot tie to a user, or cannot tell apart from a copy, is not usable. Skip both with `IS NOT NULL` tests in the `WHERE`. Test the event id yourself, even though it will be the primary key: in SQLite, [[a text primary key lets NULL in|null-key]]. A real pipeline would also [[count what it skipped|rejects]].

### Problem 3: the wrong type

Row 7 says `"user_id": "3"`, with quotes. That is the text `'3'`, not the number 3. `typeof(x)`, from "Constraints: rules the database enforces", shows the kind of value:

```sql
SELECT id,
       typeof(body ->> '$.user_id') AS raw_type,
       typeof(CAST(body ->> '$.user_id' AS INTEGER)) AS cast_type
FROM raw_events
WHERE id IN (2, 7);
```

| id | raw_type | cast_type |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | integer | integer |
| 7 | text | integer |

`CAST(… AS INTEGER)`, from "Problem solving: cohort retention", turns it into a whole number. Run it on every row: a value that is already a number stays as it is.

### Problem 4: the same thing written two ways

Row 7's name is `"Visit"` with a capital V, and every other row says `"visit"`. `lower()`, from "Working with text", makes every name small letters.

Row 8's time is `"2024-07-02T10:30:00Z"`, with a `T` between the date and the time and a `Z` at the end. Every other row uses the form `'2024-07-02 10:30:00'`. `datetime(x)` reads a time in [[either form|t-and-z]] and always writes it back in the plain one:

```sql
SELECT lower('Visit') AS name, datetime('2024-07-02T10:30:00Z') AS ts;
```

| name | ts |
| --- | --- |
| visit | 2024-07-02 10:30:00 |

Now every name and every time is written one way, so grouping and comparing work later.

### Problem 5: the same event twice

Rows 2 and 3 are both event `e2`, eight seconds apart. The phone lost signal, never heard back, and [[sent it again|retries]]. It is one signup, not two.

The event carries its own id, so the **id decides** what is a duplicate, not the row. Two parts do the work:

- `event_id` is the primary key of `events`, so the table cannot hold `e2` twice.
- `INSERT OR IGNORE`, from "Constraints: rules the database enforces", skips any row that breaks a rule, instead of stopping with an error. The first copy goes in. The later copy is skipped.

"First" must mean the copy that arrived first. So put `ORDER BY id` at the end of the `SELECT`, and rows go in in the order they came.

### Putting it together

In "Store 2: loading the export" you filled a table with `INSERT … SELECT`: an `INSERT` whose rows come from a query instead of a `VALUES` list. The ingest has the same shape:

```sql
INSERT OR IGNORE INTO events (event_id, user_id, name, ts)
SELECT …each field, cleaned…
FROM raw_events
WHERE …only rows you can use…
ORDER BY id;
```

- The `SELECT` list reads each key with `->>` and cleans it: `CAST` for the user id, `lower` for the name, `datetime` for the time.
- The `WHERE` keeps valid JSON with both ids present.
- The primary key and `OR IGNORE` drop the retry.

Ten raw rows go in and six clean events come out.

**Watch out:** with a plain `INSERT`, the retried `e2` breaks the primary key, and SQLite answers `UNIQUE constraint failed: events.event_id`. A failed statement changes nothing, so not one row is loaded, not even the good ones. Leave out `json_valid(body)` and the same happens with `malformed JSON`. One bad row must never block the good ones: filter what you can see, and let `OR IGNORE` catch the rest.

**JSON** (JavaScript Object Notation) is a way to write data as text: keys in quotes, a colon, then a value, all inside curly brackets. Nearly every app and website sends its data this way, because any programming language can read and write it. You queried JSON columns in the lesson "JSON columns". Spacecraft ground systems use it too: many mission-control tools pass commands and telemetry between programs as JSON messages, and they check every message the same way this lesson does before trusting it.
:::

Ingest is the front door: it takes whatever arrived and writes a table the later steps can trust. The next three lessons read only from `events`, never from `raw_events`, so they never have to worry about broken JSON again.

::: context null-key A primary key that lets NULL in
You might expect `PRIMARY KEY` to refuse NULL, as it does in most databases. In an ordinary SQLite table, only an `INTEGER PRIMARY KEY` is guaranteed never to be NULL. A `TEXT PRIMARY KEY` accepts NULL, and even accepts it twice, because NULL is never equal to anything, not even another NULL. It is an old bug that SQLite keeps so that older programs still work. So without the `IS NOT NULL` test, row 10 would land in `events` with no event id. Writing `event_id TEXT PRIMARY KEY NOT NULL` would close that door in the table too.
:::

::: context rejects Counting what you skipped
Skipping a bad row quietly is fine for a lesson, but a real pipeline also keeps a tally: 10 received, 6 loaded, 4 skipped, and why. Some keep the skipped rows in a separate table so someone can look at them later. If the tally suddenly jumps, say half of today's events have no `user_id`, it usually means a new version of the app has a bug. Ground stations do the same with telemetry: every frame that fails its check is counted, and a rising count is an early warning that something is wrong with the radio link.
:::

::: context t-and-z What the T and the Z mean
`2024-07-02T10:30:00Z` is the international standard way to write a time, called **ISO 8601**. The `T` only separates the date from the time. The `Z` means the time is in **UTC**, the world's reference clock (Z is said "Zulu" by pilots and mission controllers). The other rows here are in UTC too, so dropping the `Z` loses nothing. If a time carries an offset instead, like `+02:00`, `datetime()` converts it to UTC: `datetime('2024-07-02T12:30:00+02:00')` also gives `'2024-07-02 10:30:00'`.
:::

::: context retries Why phones send things twice
A phone sends an event and waits for the server to say "got it". If the signal drops, the reply never arrives, so the phone cannot tell whether the event was lost or only the reply. The safe choice is to send it again. That means the server must expect copies. An operation that gives the same result however many times it runs is called **idempotent**. Giving each event its own id and ignoring an id already seen makes this load idempotent: run it twice and `events` still holds six rows.
:::

### The picture

A shop door has a counter that clicks every time the door opens. At closing time it says 40. But one customer went in and out four times, so 40 clicks is not 40 customers. And on a day the shop was shut, the counter writes nothing at all, so a list of daily counts has no line for that day. This lesson fixes both.

### Two numbers per day

- **[[Daily active users|dau-word]]**, or **DAU**: how many *different* users did anything that day.
- **New users**: how many of them were seen that day for the very first time.

### Trap 1: activity is not users

Counting rows per day counts events, like the door counter:

```sql
SELECT date(ts) AS day, COUNT(*) AS dau
FROM events
GROUP BY day;
```

On 1 July this says 4. But the table above shows only users 1 and 2 that day. `COUNT(DISTINCT user_id)` counts each different user once:

```sql
SELECT date(ts) AS day, COUNT(DISTINCT user_id) AS dau
FROM events
GROUP BY date(ts);
```

| day | dau |
| --- | --- |
| 2024-07-01 | 2 |
| 2024-07-02 | 3 |
| 2024-07-03 | 3 |
| 2024-07-05 | 3 |
| 2024-07-06 | 2 |
| 2024-07-07 | 2 |
| 2024-07-08 | 1 |
| 2024-07-09 | 2 |
| 2024-07-10 | 1 |

`date(ts)` keeps only [[the day part|whose-day]] of the time, as in "Date arithmetic and date ranges".

### Trap 2: days with no events vanish

Look at the list again: it jumps from 3 July to 5 July. `GROUP BY` makes a row only for a day that has events, so [[a dead day disappears|missing-day]] instead of showing 0.

You met this in "Recursive CTEs: generating a series". The fix is a calendar: a recursive CTE with one row for every day, and the numbers joined onto it with `LEFT JOIN`. For the first week:

```sql
WITH RECURSIVE days(day) AS (
  SELECT '2024-07-01'
  UNION ALL
  SELECT date(day, '+1 day') FROM days WHERE day < '2024-07-07'
)
SELECT day FROM days;
```

That gives seven rows, 1 to 7 July, with 4 July included.

### New users: each user's first day

"New on a day" needs each user's **first-ever** day. That is a per-user `MIN`:

```sql
SELECT user_id, MIN(date(ts)) AS first_day
FROM events
GROUP BY user_id;
```

| user_id | first_day |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | 2024-07-01 |
| 2 | 2024-07-01 |
| 3 | 2024-07-02 |
| 4 | 2024-07-03 |
| 5 | 2024-07-05 |
| 6 | 2024-07-07 |

A day's new users are the users whose `first_day` is that day. So wrap this query and count its rows per `first_day`:

```sql
SELECT first_day AS day, COUNT(*) AS new_users
FROM (SELECT user_id, MIN(date(ts)) AS first_day FROM events GROUP BY user_id)
GROUP BY first_day;
```

| day | new_users |
| --- | --- |
| 2024-07-01 | 2 |
| 2024-07-02 | 1 |
| 2024-07-03 | 1 |
| 2024-07-05 | 1 |
| 2024-07-07 | 1 |

The `MIN` looks at **all** events, not only the week in the report. That matters: a user first seen before the report starts is [[never counted as new inside it|all-history]].

### One calendar, two separate counts

Now there are three pieces: the calendar, the DAU per day and the new users per day. Make each count its own CTE, grouped down to **one row per day**. Then join both onto the calendar:

```sql
FROM days d
LEFT JOIN active a ON a.day = d.day
LEFT JOIN firsts f ON f.day = d.day
```

Each count has at most one row per day, so every join matches [[one row to one row|one-to-one]], and nothing is repeated. The first week comes out like this:

| day | dau | new_users |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2024-07-01 | 2 | 2 |
| 2024-07-02 | 3 | 1 |
| 2024-07-03 | 3 | 1 |
| 2024-07-04 | NULL | NULL |
| 2024-07-05 | 3 | 1 |
| 2024-07-06 | 2 | NULL |
| 2024-07-07 | 2 | 1 |

The NULLs are days where a count had no row. `COALESCE(x, 0)`, from "Testing for NULL", turns each one into 0. A quiet day should read 0, not "unknown".

### Check it by hand

On 6 July, users 4 and 3 were active, so DAU is 2. Both were first seen earlier, so there are 0 new users. On 7 July, users 6 and 2 were active, and user 6 was brand new: 2 and 1. The table agrees.

**Watch out:** do not work out both numbers in one query over `events`. This looks tidy:

```sql
SELECT date(e.ts) AS day, COUNT(DISTINCT e.user_id) AS dau, COUNT(f.user_id) AS new_users
FROM events e
LEFT JOIN (SELECT user_id, MIN(date(ts)) AS first_day FROM events GROUP BY user_id) f
  ON f.user_id = e.user_id AND f.first_day = date(e.ts)
GROUP BY date(e.ts);
```

But it says 4 new users on 1 July, not 2. Each new user matches once **per event** they had that day, and users 1 and 2 had two events each. It is the fan-out from "Debugging: the join that double-counts". Counting in separate CTEs, one row per day each, avoids it.

::: context dau-word DAU, WAU and MAU
**DAU** is the number of different people who used a product on one day. Teams also count **WAU** (weekly) and **MAU** (monthly) the same way, over a longer window. A user active on all seven days of a week counts seven times in the week's daily numbers but only once in its WAU. Dividing DAU by MAU gives a rough "stickiness": the share of the month's users who show up on a typical day. Games and chat apps watch it closely, because it says whether people come back by habit.
:::

::: context whose-day Whose day is it?
`date(ts)` cuts the day at midnight in whatever clock `ts` is written in, here UTC. For a user in Tokyo, 9 hours ahead, an evening visit at 20:00 local time is 11:00 UTC the same day, but a morning visit at 07:00 local time is 22:00 UTC the day before. Big apps pick one clock for their daily numbers and write it on the chart, so nobody compares one team's Monday with another team's.
:::

::: context missing-day A missing day tells a lie
A chart draws what it is given. Give it the grouped rows and it places 3 July and 5 July side by side, and the day with no events is invisible. With the calendar, 4 July stays on the axis at 0, and anyone looking asks "what happened that day?". That question is often the most useful thing on the dashboard: an outage, a failed app release, a holiday.

::: context all-history Why "new" looks at all of history
Suppose the report covered only 5 to 7 July, and the `MIN` looked only at those days. User 1 was active on 5 July, and inside that window it is their earliest day, so they would be counted as new, although they have used the app since 1 July. Every report window would "discover" old users again. Taking each user's first day over all events, and only then picking the days to show, gives each user exactly one new day, ever.
:::

::: context one-to-one Why each count gets its own CTE
A join repeats a row once for every match. If both sides have at most one row per day, each day matches at most once, and the numbers stay as they were. That is why each count is grouped down to one row per day **before** it meets anything else. It is the same rule as pre-aggregating each order's total in "Store 4: the customer report": shrink each measure to the level of the report first, then join.
:::

=== sqlp-07 | Analytics 3: a signup funnel with a deadline
--- teach
Last lesson you counted who used the app each day. Now the team asks where people give up. In "Problem solving: a conversion funnel" you built an ordered funnel for a shop. This one is the app's **signup funnel**, visit → signup → activate, with two new things: a **deadline** on the last step, and percentages measured against the **first** step.

### The table

The same `events` table as last lesson. Here are each user's first visit, signups and activation:

| user_id | first visit | signup | activate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 07-01 09:00 | 07-01 09:05 | 07-02 08:00 |
| 2 | 07-01 10:00 | 07-02 11:00 | 07-05 11:00 |
| 3 | 07-02 09:00 | 07-02 09:02 | 07-03 15:00 |
| 4 | 07-03 18:00 | 07-03 18:10 | 07-10 10:00 |
| 5 | 07-05 11:00 | 07-05 11:30 | 07-09 09:00 |
| 6 | 07-07 22:00 | 07-07 21:00 | (none) |

All dates are July 2024. To **[[activate|activation]]** means to start really using the app after signing up.

### The picture

A shop hands you a coupon that says "valid for 72 hours". Use it on day two and you get the discount. Come back a week later and the coupon has run out, even though you did come back. The funnel's last step works like that coupon: activation only counts if it happens **within 72 hours of signing up**. A user who activates a week later is still a win, but not one the [[onboarding flow|onboarding]] can take credit for.

### Steps 1 and 2: as before

These work exactly as in "Problem solving: a conversion funnel". Each step is a CTE holding each user's time at that step:

- `s1`: each user's first `visit`, `MIN(ts)` grouped by user.
- `s2`: each user's first `signup` **after** their `s1` time. It joins `events` to `s1` and keeps `e.ts > s1.t`.

`s1` holds all six users. `s2` holds five. User 6 signed up at 21:00 and first visited at 22:00: they came in through an [[invite link|invite]], signed up, and only then looked around. In an ordered funnel their signup does not follow a visit, so they stop at step 1.

### Step 3 has two conditions

An activation counts only if it is:

1. **after** the signup, `e.ts > s2.t`, as before;
2. **no later than** 72 hours after it. That is new.

For the second condition you need the time between two moments. In "Sessionising events" you measured gaps with `unixepoch(ts)`, which turns a time into a whole number of seconds since [[1 January 1970|unix-time]]. Subtract two of them and you get the seconds in between, as an exact whole number.

72 hours is `72 * 3600` seconds, where `*` means "times" and 3600 is the number of seconds in one hour. That is 259,200 seconds. Here is every user's gap from signup to activation:

| user_id | seconds | hours | in time? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 82500 | about 22.9 | yes |
| 3 | 107880 | about 30.0 | yes |
| 2 | 259200 | exactly 72 | yes, on the line |
| 5 | 336600 | 93.5 | no |
| 4 | 575400 | about 159.8 | no |

So step 3 adds one line to the usual shape:

```sql
WHERE e.name = 'activate'
  AND e.ts > s2.t
  AND unixepoch(e.ts) - unixepoch(s2.t) <= 72 * 3600
```

`<=` means "less than or equal to". Users 1, 3 and 2 pass. Users 4 and 5 activated, but too late.

### Exactly on the line

User 2 signed up at 11:00 on 2 July and activated at 11:00 on 5 July: exactly 259,200 seconds. Is that "within 72 hours"? The team decided yes: "within" includes the boundary. So the test is `<=`, not `<`.

That is a decision, not a fact of nature. [[Write it down|boundary]] next to the report, so everyone reads the number the same way. Measuring in whole seconds is what lets the boundary be exact. A difference of `julianday` values is a fraction of a day, and as "Sessionising events" warned, turning it back into hours can land a hair off.

### Stacking the counts

As before, `UNION ALL` stacks one small row per step, each with its number, name and count:

| step | name | users |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | visit | 6 |
| 2 | signup | 5 |
| 3 | activate | 3 |

### Percent of the first step

In the shop funnel each step was measured against the step before, using `LAG`. This time the team wants each step against **step 1**: the share of all visitors who got this far. That is how an overall funnel chart reads.

`FIRST_VALUE(x)` is a window function that gives `x` from the **first** row of the window, on every row. With `OVER (ORDER BY step)`, the first row is step 1:

```sql
SELECT step, name, users,
       FIRST_VALUE(users) OVER (ORDER BY step) AS first_users,
       LAG(users) OVER (ORDER BY step) AS prev_users
FROM steps;
```

| step | name | users | first_users | prev_users |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | visit | 6 | 6 | NULL |
| 2 | signup | 5 | 6 | 6 |
| 3 | activate | 3 | 6 | 5 |

`first_users` is 6 on every row, so the percentage is `ROUND(100.0 * users / FIRST_VALUE(users) OVER (ORDER BY step), 1)`. Step 1 is 100.0, since it is measured against itself. Step 2 is 100.0 × 5 ÷ 6 = 83.3. Step 3 is 100.0 × 3 ÷ 6 = 50.0. Half the visitors [[activated in time|funnel-shape]].

**Watch out:** with `<` instead of `<=`, user 2 drops out and step 3 shows 2 users, 33.3%. Nothing errors, and the number looks believable. Boundaries are where funnel numbers quietly go wrong, so test the user who sits exactly on the line, as this lesson did with user 2.

::: context activation What "activation" means
Signing up only creates an account. **Activation** is the moment a new user first does the thing the app is for: sends a first message, uploads a first photo, finishes a first lesson. Product teams choose one event to stand for it, here `activate`. They care because users who activate early tend to stay, and users who sign up and do nothing tend to leave. That is why the signup funnel ends at activation, not at signup.
:::

::: context onboarding The onboarding flow
**Onboarding** is everything an app does to help a brand-new user get going: the welcome screens, the tips, the first-day emails. Its job is to turn a signup into an activation, fast. The 72-hour deadline measures exactly that. A user who comes back on their own after a week probably was not helped by the welcome screens, so counting them would make onboarding look better than it is.
:::

::: context invite An invite link
An **invite link** is a link one user sends another, often opening a signup page straight away. So the invited person signs up before they have "visited" in the funnel's sense. Nothing is broken, the same as the deep link in "Problem solving: a conversion funnel". An ordered funnel leaves them at step 1 because its question is how people move through the steps in order. A team that cares about invited users often builds a separate funnel for them.
:::

::: context unix-time Seconds since 1970
**Unix time** counts seconds from midnight UTC at the start of 1 January 1970, a date picked by the makers of the Unix operating system. `unixepoch('1970-01-01 00:00:00')` is 0, and `unixepoch('2024-07-02 11:00:00')` is 1719918000. Because it is one whole number, the difference of two moments is a plain subtraction, with no months or leap days to think about. Computers everywhere use it, and spacecraft often stamp telemetry with a similar count of seconds from their own chosen starting moment.
:::

::: context boundary Write the rule down
"Within 72 hours" can be read two ways, and both are reasonable. Engineers settle it once and write it down, in a comment beside the query or on the dashboard: "activation counts if it is at most 259,200 seconds after signup". Aerospace does the same with limits. A sensor reading "within limits" must say whether the limit itself counts, because a reading that sits exactly on it will happen sooner or later, and two teams reading the rule differently will disagree about whether to abort.
:::

::: context funnel-shape The funnel against its first step
Drawn against step 1, each bar shows the share of all visitors still in the funnel. The biggest drop here is from signup to activation: 5 users signed up, but only 3 activated in time. That is the step a team would look at first.

=== sqlp-08 | Analytics 4: day-N retention
--- teach
Last lesson you followed users from their first visit to activation. The last analytics question is the one that decides whether an app survives: **do people come back?** In "Problem solving: cohort retention" you answered it by calendar month. This lesson asks it day by day, and meets a trap that the monthly report did not have to face.

### The table

The same `events` table. Each user's signup day, and every day they did anything:

| user_id | signup day | active on (July 2024) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 07-01 | 01, 02, 03, 05, 08 |
| 2 | 07-02 | 01, 02, 05, 07 |
| 3 | 07-02 | 02, 03, 06, 09 |
| 4 | 07-03 | 03, 06, 10 |
| 5 | 07-05 | 05, 09 |
| 6 | 07-07 | 07 |

The data ends on 10 July.

### The picture

A gym signs up a new member. The owner wonders: did they come back the next day? Two days later? A week later? Now ask that about every new member at once, and count what share came back on each of those days.

### Day N

A user's **day 0** is the calendar day they signed up. **Day N** is the Nth calendar day after it. User 1 signed up on 1 July, so their day 1 is 2 July and their day 7 is 8 July.

**Day-N retention** asks, for N = 1, 2, … 7: of the users who signed up, what share were active on their day N? The day counts by the [[calendar, not by 24-hour periods|calendar-day]].

### Building it from pieces

Every piece is something you have used before. Each becomes one CTE.

**Piece 1: each user's signup day.** The date of their `signup` event. Taking `MIN(ts)` makes sure a user who somehow signed up twice gets one day:

```sql
signups AS (
  SELECT user_id, date(MIN(ts)) AS d0 FROM events WHERE name = 'signup' GROUP BY user_id
)
```

It gives the six users and the signup days in the table above.

**Piece 2: each user's active days.** `SELECT DISTINCT user_id, date(ts)` gives one row per user per day they did anything. User 1 had two events on 1 July, and `DISTINCT` keeps that day once.

**Piece 3: the numbers 1 to 7.** A recursive CTE that counts, as in "Recursive CTEs: a query that counts":

```sql
n(day) AS (
  SELECT 1 UNION ALL SELECT day + 1 FROM n WHERE day < 7
)
```

Each value in `n` is one N.

### Every user with every N

The report needs a yes-or-no for **every pair** of a user and an N. In "Design: choosing the joins for a report" you met `CROSS JOIN`, which pairs every row of one table with every row of the other. `n CROSS JOIN signups` gives 7 × 6 = 42 pairs.

### The date of day N

For each pair you need the date of that user's day N. `date(d0, '+3 days')` moves a date three days forward, as in "Date arithmetic and date ranges". But here the 3 is a number from `n`, not typed text. `||`, from "Building text: joining, replacing and LIKE", glues pieces of text together, so you can build the modifier:

```sql
SELECT '+' || 3 || ' days' AS modifier,
       date('2024-07-01', '+' || 3 || ' days') AS day_3;
```

| modifier | day_3 |
| --- | --- |
| +3 days | 2024-07-04 |

In the query, `date(s.d0, '+' || n.day || ' days')` is user `s`'s day N.

### Was the user active that day?

`EXISTS (…)` is 1 when its subquery finds a row and 0 when it does not, as in "Problem solving: cohort retention". Call the flag `hit`:

```sql
EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM active a
        WHERE a.user_id = s.user_id
          AND a.d = date(s.d0, '+' || n.day || ' days')) AS hit
```

Then `SUM(hit)` counts the yeses.

### Users who have not had day N yet

This is the trap. User 6 signed up on 7 July, so their day 7 is 14 July. The data ends on 10 July. Day 7 **has not happened yet** for user 6, so nobody knows whether they will come back then.

Counting them as "not retained" on day 7 treats "not yet known" as "no". Every recent signup would drag the later days down, for no real reason. So only users whose day N is on or before the last day of data are **eligible** for day N. The eligible users are the denominator: the number you divide by. Statisticians call data that stops before the answer arrives [[censored|censored]].

Find the last day of data from the data itself, not by typing `'2024-07-10'` into the query. [[A typed date goes stale|last-day]] the moment new events arrive.

```sql
last AS (SELECT MAX(date(ts)) AS d FROM events)
```

`last` has exactly one row. `CROSS JOIN last` adds its `d` column to every pair, without making more rows, because one row times one row is still one row. Then the pairs can keep only eligible ones:

```sql
WHERE date(s.d0, '+' || n.day || ' days') <= last.d
```

Here are the pairs for day 1 and day 7, after that filter:

| day | user_id | day N date | hit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 1 | 2024-07-02 | 1 |
| 1 | 2 | 2024-07-03 | 0 |
| 1 | 3 | 2024-07-03 | 1 |
| 1 | 4 | 2024-07-04 | 0 |
| 1 | 5 | 2024-07-06 | 0 |
| 1 | 6 | 2024-07-08 | 0 |
| 7 | 1 | 2024-07-08 | 1 |
| 7 | 2 | 2024-07-09 | 0 |
| 7 | 3 | 2024-07-09 | 1 |
| 7 | 4 | 2024-07-10 | 1 |

Everyone has had day 1. Only users 1 to 4 have had day 7; users 5 and 6 are left out of it.

### One row per N

Group the pairs by `day`:

- `COUNT(*)` counts the eligible users;
- `SUM(hit)` counts the retained ones;
- `ROUND(100.0 * SUM(hit) / COUNT(*), 1)` is the percentage.

Day 7 is 3 retained of 4 eligible: 75.0. Put all seven days together and you have the app's [[retention curve|retention-curve]].

**Watch out:** leave out the eligibility filter and day 7 reads 3 of 6, 50.0, instead of 75.0. Users 5 and 6 are counted as "did not come back" on a day that is still in the future. Days 4, 5 and 7 all come out too low, and the newest signups make the app look worse than it is.

::: context calendar-day Calendar days, not 24 hours
User 6 signed up at 21:00 on 7 July. Their day 1 is all of 8 July, which starts only three hours later. A user who signed up at 06:00 gets a day 1 that starts eighteen hours later. Counting in 24-hour periods instead would treat them equally, but it is harder to read on a chart, and "came back the next day" is what people mean. Like "calendar months" in "Problem solving: cohort retention", it is a choice, and the report should say which one it made.
:::

::: context censored Answers that have not arrived
When the data stops before the answer is known, statisticians call the data **censored**: it was cut off, not answered "no". Doctors meet it in medical trials, where a patient who joined last month cannot yet say how they are doing after a year. Engineers meet it when testing how long parts last: a pump still running when the test ends has not failed, it has only not failed *yet*. The fix is always the same as here: count someone only for the times you could actually observe.
:::

::: context last-day Let the data say when it ends
A date typed into a query is right on the day it is written. Tomorrow more events arrive, the real last day moves to 11 July, and the typed `'2024-07-10'` quietly leaves out a whole day of eligible users. `MAX(date(ts))` moves with the data, so the same query stays right every day it runs. Scheduled reports, the kind that rebuild a dashboard every morning, depend on this.
:::

::: context retention-curve The retention curve
Drawn as bars, the seven percentages make this app's day-N retention curve. The small numbers underneath are the eligible users for each day. With only six users the curve jumps about: one person more or less changes a bar by about 17 to 25 percentage points. Real apps have thousands of users per day, and their curves are smooth. A curve that levels off instead of falling to zero means some people have found a reason to stay.

--- task
Write one query that returns one row for each N from 1 to 7, with these columns:

1. `day`: N;
2. `eligible`: how many signed-up users have a day N on or before the last day in `events`;
3. `retained`: how many of those eligible users had any event on exactly their day N;
4. `pct`: `retained` as a percentage of `eligible`, rounded to 1 decimal place.

A user's day 0 is the date of their `signup` event, and day N is the Nth calendar day after it. Find the last day from the data with `MAX(date(ts))`, not by typing it. Sort by `day`.
--- starter
SELECT user_id, date(ts) AS signup_day
FROM events
WHERE name = 'signup';
--- solution
WITH RECURSIVE n(day) AS (
  SELECT 1 UNION ALL SELECT day + 1 FROM n WHERE day < 7
), signups AS (
  SELECT user_id, date(MIN(ts)) AS d0 FROM events WHERE name = 'signup' GROUP BY user_id
), active AS (
  SELECT DISTINCT user_id, date(ts) AS d FROM events
), last AS (
  SELECT MAX(date(ts)) AS d FROM events
), pairs AS (
  SELECT n.day, s.user_id,
         EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM active a
                 WHERE a.user_id = s.user_id AND a.d = date(s.d0, '+' || n.day || ' days')) AS hit
  FROM n
  CROSS JOIN signups s
  CROSS JOIN last
  WHERE date(s.d0, '+' || n.day || ' days') <= last.d
)
SELECT day, COUNT(*) AS eligible, SUM(hit) AS retained,
       ROUND(100.0 * SUM(hit) / COUNT(*), 1) AS pct
FROM pairs
GROUP BY day
ORDER BY day;
--- hint
Four CTEs first: `n`, the numbers 1 to 7; `signups`, each user's signup day `d0`; `active`, each user's distinct active days; and `last`, the one-row last day of data, `MAX(date(ts))`.
--- hint
A fifth CTE, `pairs`: `FROM n CROSS JOIN signups s CROSS JOIN last`, keeping only pairs where `date(s.d0, '+' || n.day || ' days') <= last.d`. Flag each pair with `EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM active a WHERE a.user_id = s.user_id AND a.d = …that same date…) AS hit`.
--- hint
Finish with `SELECT day, COUNT(*) AS eligible, SUM(hit) AS retained, ROUND(100.0 * SUM(hit) / COUNT(*), 1) AS pct FROM pairs GROUP BY day ORDER BY day`. Check day 7: 4 eligible, 3 retained, 75.0.

### A book is not a copy

Walk into a library and ask for *Dune*. The catalogue has one entry for it: the title, the author, the [[ISBN|isbn]]. On the shelf there may be three physical *Dune*s, each with its own barcode sticker. One is out with a member, one is on the shelf, one has coffee spilled on page 40.

So there are two different things here:

- A **book** is the title as a whole: one row for *Dune*, however many copies there are.
- A **copy** is one physical item you can hold, with its own barcode.

You lend copies, not books. This is the first [[modelling decision|type-and-instance]] of the project, and a classic one. Get it wrong, with a single `books` table and a `quantity` column, and you cannot say *which* copy is overdue or damaged. "Two of three are out" does not tell you who has which.

### Three new tables

- `books` — the title: ISBN (unique), title, author
- `copies` — one physical item: which book it is, and its barcode (unique)
- `loans` — one lending: which copy, which member, when it was lent, when it is due, when it came back

A copy points at its book. A loan points at a copy and at a member. Those pointers are foreign keys, from the lesson "Foreign keys", so remember that SQLite only checks them after `PRAGMA foreign_keys = ON;`.

A loan that is still out has no return date yet. So `returned_on` is **NULL while the copy is out**, and gets a date when it comes back. That one NULL is what the rest of this project leans on.

### Dates stored as text

The three dates are text like `'2024-07-15'`: year, month, day. As you saw in "Date arithmetic and date ranges", text in this shape sorts in time order. So `>` ("greater than") between two of these dates means "later than", and a rule can compare them.

### Rule 1: due after it starts

A loan must be due *after* the day it was lent:

```sql
CHECK (due_on > loaned_on)
```

This rule looks at two columns of the same row, so it goes at the end of the table, after the last column, as in "Constraints: rules the database enforces". A loan lent on 10 July and due on 1 July is refused with `CHECK constraint failed: due_on > loaned_on`.

### Rule 2: not returned before it was lent

```sql
CHECK (returned_on IS NULL OR returned_on >= loaned_on)
```

Read it in words: "either it has not come back yet, or it came back on or after the day it was lent". `OR` means at least one side must be true, and `>=` means "on or after". A loan lent on 10 July and returned on 9 July is refused. An open loan, with `returned_on` NULL, passes by the first half. [[The IS NULL half|is-null-or]] says that out loud.

### Rule 3: one open loan per copy

This is the interesting one. A copy cannot be in two people's bags at once, so it can be out on only one loan at a time.

A first try is `UNIQUE (copy_id)` at the end of `loans`. But that allows one loan per copy *ever*. The second time anybody borrows copy D-1, even long after it came back, the insert is refused. The library needs every old loan kept as [[history|loan-history]].

What you want is "unique among the loans that are still out".

### A partial index

In "Deduplicating: keep the latest row" you met a **unique index**: `CREATE UNIQUE INDEX` builds an index and refuses two rows with the same value in it. Add a `WHERE` to it and you get a **partial index**: an index that holds only the rows matching its condition.

```sql
CREATE UNIQUE INDEX one_open_loan_per_copy ON loans(copy_id) WHERE returned_on IS NULL;
```

- `CREATE UNIQUE INDEX one_open_loan_per_copy` makes a unique index with that name.
- `ON loans(copy_id)` says it indexes the `copy_id` column of `loans`.
- `WHERE returned_on IS NULL` says only open loans go into it.

Only [[the rows inside the index are compared|partial-picture]], so the uniqueness applies to open loans alone. Closed loans pile up freely as history.

### The rules at work

Say the library has *Dune* with copies 1 (D-1) and 2 (D-2), and copy 1 goes out to Ana: loan 1, lent on 1 July, due on 15 July. Now try these, one after another:

| you try | what happens |
| --- | --- |
| lend copy 1 to Bo on 2 July | refused: `UNIQUE constraint failed: loans.copy_id` |
| set loan 1's `returned_on` to 5 July | accepted, and loan 1 leaves the index |
| lend copy 1 to Bo on 6 July | accepted: it is now copy 1's only open loan |
| lend copy 9, which does not exist | refused: `FOREIGN KEY constraint failed` |
| a second book with Dune's ISBN | refused: `UNIQUE constraint failed: books.isbn` |

**Watch out:** write `WHERE returned_on IS NULL`, never `WHERE returned_on = NULL`. Anything compared with `= NULL` is unknown, not true, so that index holds no rows at all. SQLite creates it without an error, and then it refuses nothing: two open loans for the same copy go straight in.

::: context isbn The number on the back of a book
An **ISBN**, International Standard Book Number, names one edition of a book. Since 2007 it has 13 digits, and it is printed as the barcode on the back cover. Every copy of the same paperback *Dune* carries the same ISBN, 9780441013593, so it belongs on `books`. The library's own barcode sticker is different: the library prints one per copy, so it belongs on `copies`. Store the ISBN as text, not as a number. You never do arithmetic with it, and the older 10-digit form can start with a 0 or end in the letter X.
:::

::: context type-and-instance Part numbers and serial numbers
Engineers call this **type** versus **instance**: the design of a thing versus one real thing built to it. Aerospace lives by it. A *part number* names a design, such as a valve, and every identical valve shares it. A *serial number* names one physical valve, with its own test records and hours of use. If one valve fails an inspection, you ground that serial number, not every valve ever made. Books and copies are the same split.

::: context is-null-or Saying the NULL case out loud
Strictly, `CHECK (returned_on >= loaned_on)` alone would behave the same. In "Constraints: rules the database enforces" you saw that a `CHECK` only refuses a row when its condition is false. With `returned_on` NULL the comparison gives NULL, not false, so the open loan gets in anyway. Writing `returned_on IS NULL OR` spells out that an open loan is fine on purpose. The next person who reads the table does not have to remember how NULL behaves inside a `CHECK` to trust the rule.
:::

::: context loan-history Why old loans are kept
When a copy comes back, the loan is not deleted: it gets a `returned_on` date and stays. Those old rows answer real questions. Who had this copy before the page was torn? Which books are borrowed most, so the library buys more copies? How many loans ran late last year? Deleting them would throw that away. Some libraries do strip the member's name from old loans after a while, to protect readers' privacy, but they keep the loan itself for the counts. Mission logs work the same way: you add new rows and never overwrite the old ones.
:::

A partial index is also smaller than a full one, and a query whose `WHERE` repeats the condition, such as `WHERE returned_on IS NULL`, can use it to find the open loans fast.

### The question

The front desk and the website both ask the same thing: **for each book, how many copies are there, how many are out, and how many can be borrowed right now?**

Put the answer in a view named `book_availability`, as in "Views: saved queries". Then the desk, the website and the weekly report all use [[one definition of "available"|one-definition]].

### When is a copy out?

A copy is **out** when it has an **open loan**: a loan with `returned_on IS NULL`. Thanks to last lesson's partial index, a copy has at most one.

```sql
SELECT id, copy_id FROM loans WHERE returned_on IS NULL ORDER BY id;
```

| id | copy_id |
| --- | --- |
| 2 | 2 |
| 3 | 1 |
| 4 | 4 |
| 6 | 6 |

Copy 1 (D-1) has been lent twice. Loan 1 is closed, and loan 3 is the open one.

### Step 1: start from books

Build the query in the order of the tables. Start **from `books`**, then `LEFT JOIN` copies. As in "LEFT JOIN and NULL", every book stays, and a book with no copies gets NULLs on the copy side instead of disappearing:

```sql
SELECT b.title, c.barcode
FROM books b
LEFT JOIN copies c ON c.book_id = b.id;
```

| title | barcode |
| --- | --- |
| Dune | D-1 |
| Dune | D-2 |
| Dune | D-3 |
| Emma | E-1 |
| Ubik | U-1 |
| Ubik | U-2 |
| Kindred | NULL |

### Step 2: join only the open loans

Next, `LEFT JOIN` loans, with the open-loan condition **inside the `ON`**, joined with `AND`:

```sql
SELECT b.title, c.barcode, l.id AS loan_id
FROM books b
LEFT JOIN copies c ON c.book_id = b.id
LEFT JOIN loans l  ON l.copy_id = c.id AND l.returned_on IS NULL;
```

| title | barcode | loan_id |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dune | D-1 | 3 |
| Dune | D-2 | 2 |
| Dune | D-3 | NULL |
| Emma | E-1 | 4 |
| Ubik | U-1 | NULL |
| Ubik | U-2 | 6 |
| Kindred | NULL | NULL |

Still one row per copy. Each copy shows its open loan, or NULL when it is on the shelf.

### Why the condition belongs in ON

This is the rule from "LEFT JOIN: where the filter goes": a condition about the right-hand table goes in its `ON`. Here are the counts you get the other two ways:

| where `l.returned_on IS NULL` goes | Dune: copies, on loan | Ubik: copies, on loan |
| --- | --- | --- |
| in the `ON` (right) | 3, 2 | 2, 1 |
| in the `WHERE` | 3, 2 | 1, 1 |
| left out | 4, 3 | 2, 2 |

Put it in `WHERE` instead, and U-1 disappears. Its only loan came back, so its joined row has a date in `returned_on`, and the `WHERE` throws the whole row away. Ubik then shows 1 copy instead of 2.

Leave the condition out, and D-1 matches both of its loans, the closed one and the open one. It becomes two rows. Dune then shows 4 copies and 3 on loan. That is [[fan-out|at-most-one]], from "Debugging: the join that double-counts".

### Step 3: count

Now group by book and count:

- `COUNT(c.id)` counts the copies. `COUNT(column)` skips NULLs, so Kindred gets 0.
- `COUNT(l.id)` counts the open loans.
- `available` is the difference: `COUNT(c.id) - COUNT(l.id)`.

The finished view gives:

| book_id | title | copies | on_loan | available |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Dune | 3 | 2 | 1 |
| 2 | Emma | 1 | 1 | 0 |
| 3 | Ubik | 2 | 1 | 1 |
| 4 | Kindred | 0 | 0 | 0 |

### The rule that makes it correct

Because each copy has at most one open loan, this join cannot fan out: one copy never turns into two rows. The partial index you designed last lesson is what makes this query correct. And because a view runs again every time you use it, the numbers are [[always up to date|always-fresh]]: the moment a copy comes back, it counts as available.

**Watch out:** do not count with `COUNT(*)`. Kindred has no copies, but it still has one joined row full of NULLs, and `COUNT(*)` counts that row, so Kindred would show 1 copy. Count `c.id` and `l.id`.

::: context catalogue What "catalogued" means
A library's **catalogue** is its list of every title it holds, with the author, ISBN and subject, so people can search it. To **catalogue** a book is to add its entry to that list. That often happens before the physical copies arrive, or while they are still being labelled, which is exactly why *Kindred* has a row in `books` and none in `copies`. A good report shows it with 0 copies rather than leaving it out. A missing row reads as "we have never heard of this book", which is a different answer.
:::

::: context one-definition Why one shared definition matters
"Available" sounds obvious until you ask around. Does a copy waiting to be repaired count? A copy someone has reserved? If the desk, the website and the weekly report each write their own query, sooner or later they disagree, and a member is told online that a book is on the shelf when the desk says it is not. With one view, the rule lives in one place. When the library decides that copies in repair are not available, you change the view, and every screen and report changes with it.
:::

::: context at-most-one Why the join stays one row per copy
A join pairs each copy with every loan that matches its `ON`. With the open-loan condition in the `ON`, only open loans match, and the partial index allows at most one per copy. So each copy meets one loan, or none and gets NULLs. Without the condition, D-1 would meet its closed loan too.

Last lesson a view told the front desk which books can be borrowed. This lesson looks at the loans that should already have come back: the overdue report, with the fine each late loan has built up.

### The tables

The data is the same as last lesson. These are the four loans still out, with the member's name and the book's title filled in:

| loan | member | title | due_on |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | Bo | Dune | 2024-07-04 |

| 4 | Ana | Emma | 2024-07-15 |
| 6 | Ana | Ubik | 2024-04-15 |

### The rules

Every Monday the library emails members who have overdue books. Its rules:

- A loan is **overdue** when it is still out and its due date has passed.
- The fine is **25 cents per day** overdue, **capped at 10 dollars** (1000 cents) per loan. A **cap** is an upper limit: the fine grows each day until it reaches 1000, then [[stops growing|fine-cap]]. Nobody should owe more than a book is worth.

The fine is counted in whole cents, as in "Store 1: the schema", so every sum stays exact.

### Pick the report date

Think of a teacher marking homework late. "Late" only makes sense once you say *as of when*.

`date('now')`, from "Date arithmetic and date ranges", gives today's date. A report built on it is correct today and untestable forever: tomorrow it gives different numbers, and nobody can check yesterday's email. Reports that people check, compare and reproduce take the date as an [[input|report-date]]. Here the date is fixed: **as of 2024-07-15**.

### Which loans are overdue?

Two conditions, joined with `AND`: the loan is still out, and it was due **before** the report date. `<` means "less than", and for these dates that is "earlier than":

```sql
SELECT id, due_on FROM loans
WHERE returned_on IS NULL AND due_on < '2024-07-15'
ORDER BY id;
```

| id | due_on |
| --- | --- |
| 2 | 2024-07-04 |
| 3 | 2024-07-09 |
| 6 | 2024-04-15 |

Emma's loan 4 is not there. A book due on the 15th is not overdue on the 15th: the member has until the end of that day.

### Counting the days late

In "Date arithmetic and date ranges" you learned that `julianday(a) - julianday(b)` is the number of days between two dates:

```sql
SELECT julianday('2024-07-15') - julianday('2024-07-04') AS days,
       CAST(julianday('2024-07-15') - julianday('2024-07-04') AS INTEGER) AS whole_days;
```

| days | whole_days |
| --- | --- |
| 11.0 | 11 |

For whole dates like `'2024-07-15'` the difference is a whole number, but it is still a [[REAL|real-number]], a number with a decimal point. `CAST(… AS INTEGER)`, from "Problem solving: cohort retention", turns it into the integer 11 for display and arithmetic. Bo's loan is [[11 days overdue|overdue-timeline]].

### Capping the fine with MIN

Bo owes 11 × 25 = 275 cents. Ana's *Ubik* is 91 days late: 91 × 25 = 2275 cents, which is over the cap. The fine is the smaller of "days × 25" and 1000:

```sql
SELECT MIN(275, 1000) AS bo, MIN(2275, 1000) AS ana;
```

| bo | ana |
| --- | --- |
| 275 | 1000 |

In SQLite, `MIN` with two or more values inside the brackets is the ordinary "smaller of these values" function. It works on one row at a time. It is not the aggregate `MIN(x)` from the basics, which takes one column and finds the smallest value across many rows. The [[number of arguments|min-two-kinds]] decides which one you get. So the cap is `MIN(days * 25, 1000)`.

### The title is two joins away

A loan knows its `copy_id`, a copy knows its `book_id`, and the book knows its `title`. So the title is two joins away: loans → copies → books. The member's name is one more join, to `members`. This is the chain from "Joining three tables":

```sql
FROM loans l
JOIN members m ON m.id = l.member_id
JOIN copies c  ON c.id = l.copy_id
JOIN books b   ON b.id = c.book_id
```

### Sorting

The report lists the biggest fines first: `ORDER BY fine_cents DESC`. When two fines are equal, it sorts by member name, so the order is always the same: `ORDER BY fine_cents DESC, member`. You can sort by a column's `AS` name.

**Watch out:** `due_on <= '2024-07-15'`, "on or before", looks close enough, but it pulls in Emma, due that very day. She would appear on an overdue email with 0 days and a fine of 0 cents. Use `<`.

::: context fine-cap Why fines have a limit
A fine is meant to nudge people to bring books back, not to punish them forever. Without a cap, a book forgotten for a year would cost 365 × 25 = 9125 cents, more than 90 dollars, far more than a new copy. Many libraries have gone further and dropped overdue fines altogether. Chicago Public Library did so in 2019, and found that many people who had stayed away because of old fines came back. The cap in this project is 1000 cents per loan.
:::

::: context report-date The date as an input
When the date is an input, running the same report twice gives the same answer, and anyone can check it. In an app, the program passes the date in as a parameter, the `?` you met in "Indexes and EXPLAIN QUERY PLAN": `due_on < ?`. On Monday it passes that Monday's date. When a member phones to say an email was wrong, you rerun the report with the same date and see exactly what it saw. Engineers replay spacecraft telemetry the same way: every calculation takes the time as an input, so a past moment can be recomputed exactly.
:::

::: context real-number Why julianday gives a REAL
A **REAL** is SQLite's type for numbers with a decimal point, stored as floating point. `julianday` returns one because a date can carry a time of day, and a time is a fraction of a day: 18:00 adds 0.75. That is why even whole dates give 11.0 rather than 11. `CAST(… AS INTEGER)` drops the part after the point: 11.75 becomes 11. For this project's whole dates that changes nothing. With times, `'2024-07-15 18:00:00'` against a due date of 4 July would count 11 whole days, not 12.
:::

::: context min-two-kinds Two MINs, and other databases
SQLite looks at how many values you pass. One, as in `MIN(due_on)`, is the aggregate: it squeezes many rows into one answer, and in this report it would collapse all the overdue loans into a single row. Two or more, as in `MIN(2275, 1000)`, is the ordinary function, worked out row by row. `MAX` has the same two kinds. Other databases keep them apart by name: PostgreSQL and MySQL call the row-by-row versions `LEAST` and `GREATEST`, so there you would write `LEAST(days * 25, 1000)`.

--- teach
Last lesson's report showed fines building up while books are out. When a late book comes back, the fine becomes **real**: money the member now owes. This last library lesson records it by itself, at the moment of the return.

### The tables

`members`, `books`, `copies` and `loans` hold the same data as last lesson. There is one new table, `fines`, and it starts empty:

```sql
CREATE TABLE fines (
  loan_id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY REFERENCES loans(id),
  cents INTEGER NOT NULL CHECK (cents > 0)
);
```

- `loan_id` says which loan the fine is for. It is also the primary key, so each loan can have at most one fine.
- `cents` is the amount, and it must be more than 0.

### The picture

Think of a car park with a ticket barrier. Whichever exit you drive out of, the machine reads your ticket and works out what you owe. Nobody at the exit has to remember to charge you, and every exit charges by the same rule.

### Why a trigger

Returns are recorded in several places: the front desk, the self-service kiosk, and a librarian fixing a typo. Each is a different program. The fine must be recorded whichever one sets the return date.

"Whenever this column changes, do that" is a trigger, from "Triggers: reacting to changes". Here is its shape. The `…` is the amount, which comes a few steps below:

```sql
CREATE TRIGGER loan_returned
AFTER UPDATE OF returned_on ON loans
WHEN OLD.returned_on IS NULL AND NEW.returned_on > NEW.due_on
BEGIN
  INSERT INTO fines (loan_id, cents) VALUES (NEW.id, …);
END;
```

Take it one line at a time.

### Only when the return date is set

`AFTER UPDATE OF returned_on ON loans` means: after an `UPDATE` on `loans` that [[sets the returned_on column|update-of]]. An update that edits only the due date does not fire it.

### Only on the actual return

`OLD.returned_on IS NULL` looks at the row *before* the change. It is true only when the loan was still out. So the trigger fires on the return itself, and not when someone later corrects the date of a loan that had already come back.

### Only when late

`AND NEW.returned_on > NEW.due_on` looks at the row *after* the change. It is true only when the copy came back after its due date. A copy returned early, or on its due date, owes nothing. And the table's `CHECK (cents > 0)` would [[refuse a zero fine|zero-fine]] anyway.

### The amount

The fine uses the same rule as the report: 25 cents per day late, capped at 1000. The only change is the date. `NEW.returned_on`, the day the copy came back, replaces the fixed report date:

```sql
MIN(CAST(julianday(NEW.returned_on) - julianday(NEW.due_on) AS INTEGER) * 25, 1000)
```

For Bo's loan 2, due on 4 July and returned on 15 July, that is 11 days, so MIN(11 × 25, 1000) = MIN(275, 1000) = 275 cents.

### Watching it fire

Return Bo's *Dune* (loan 2) and Ana's *Ubik* (loan 6), both on 15 July, in one statement:

```sql
UPDATE loans SET returned_on = '2024-07-15' WHERE id IN (2, 6);
SELECT loan_id, cents FROM fines ORDER BY loan_id;
```

| loan_id | cents |
| --- | --- |
| 2 | 275 |
| 6 | 1000 |

The trigger fired once for each row. Ana's *Ubik* was 91 days late, so its fine is capped at 1000. Now return Ana's *Emma* (loan 4) on 15 July, its due date. It is on time, the `WHEN` is false, and `fines` still has two rows.

### A fine stays

A fine is money owed, so it is [[kept even if the loan record is edited later|ledger]]. Nothing in this design deletes a fine. And because `loan_id` is the primary key of `fines`, one loan can only ever be fined once.

**Watch out:** leave out `OLD.returned_on IS NULL` and every edit of a return date fires the trigger again. Say a librarian corrects loan 2's return from 15 July to 14 July. The trigger tries to insert a second fine for loan 2, the primary key refuses it with `UNIQUE constraint failed: fines.loan_id`, and the librarian's correction fails with it. Worse, correcting an old loan that came back late before the trigger existed, such as loan 5, would suddenly charge a fine for it.

::: context update-of What counts as setting a column
`UPDATE OF returned_on` fires when `returned_on` is one of the columns an `UPDATE` assigns in its `SET`, even if the new value equals the old one. `UPDATE loans SET returned_on = returned_on` fires it, and changes nothing. `UPDATE loans SET due_on = '2024-07-20'` does not fire it at all. So `UPDATE OF` narrows *which statements* wake the trigger, and the `WHEN` then decides, row by row, whether it does anything.

::: context zero-fine Why the late test matters twice
Without `NEW.returned_on > NEW.due_on`, returning Emma on her due date would make the trigger insert a fine of 0 cents. The `CHECK (cents > 0)` refuses it, and because a trigger runs inside the same statement as the change, the error cancels the whole `UPDATE`. The return itself would fail, and the desk could never check an on-time book back in. So the `WHEN` is not only tidier: it keeps on-time returns working. The `CHECK` stays as a second line of defence.
:::

::: context ledger Money is never quietly rewritten
Accountants keep a **ledger**: a list of every amount charged or paid, where old entries are never erased. A mistake is fixed by adding a new entry, such as a refund, so the history shows what happened and when. The `fines` table works the same way. The trigger only ever adds rows. And `fines.loan_id` references `loans(id)` with the default `ON DELETE` action, so with foreign keys switched on, a loan that has a fine cannot be deleted. The capstones that come next give you three more specifications to design this carefully, with no step-by-step lessons.
:::

--- solution
PRAGMA foreign_keys = ON;

CREATE TABLE expenses (
  id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY,
  paid_by INTEGER NOT NULL REFERENCES people(id),
  description TEXT NOT NULL,
  amount_cents INTEGER NOT NULL CHECK (amount_cents > 0)
);

CREATE TABLE shares (
  expense_id INTEGER NOT NULL REFERENCES expenses(id) ON DELETE CASCADE,
  person_id INTEGER NOT NULL REFERENCES people(id),
  share_cents INTEGER NOT NULL CHECK (share_cents >= 0),
  PRIMARY KEY (expense_id, person_id)
);

CREATE VIEW balances AS
WITH paid AS (
  SELECT paid_by AS person_id, SUM(amount_cents) AS cents FROM expenses GROUP BY paid_by
), owed AS (
  SELECT person_id, SUM(share_cents) AS cents FROM shares GROUP BY person_id
)
SELECT p.id AS person_id, p.name,
       COALESCE(pd.cents, 0) AS paid_cents,
       COALESCE(ow.cents, 0) AS owed_cents,
       COALESCE(pd.cents, 0) - COALESCE(ow.cents, 0) AS net_cents
FROM people p
LEFT JOIN paid pd ON pd.person_id = p.id
LEFT JOIN owed ow ON ow.person_id = p.id;

CREATE VIEW unbalanced_expenses AS
SELECT e.id AS expense_id, e.amount_cents,
       COALESCE(SUM(s.share_cents), 0) AS shared_cents
FROM expenses e
LEFT JOIN shares s ON s.expense_id = e.id
GROUP BY e.id
HAVING COALESCE(SUM(s.share_cents), 0) <> e.amount_cents;

--- hint

  staff_id INTEGER NOT NULL REFERENCES staff(id),
  client_id INTEGER NOT NULL REFERENCES clients(id),
  starts_at TEXT NOT NULL,
  ends_at TEXT NOT NULL,
  status TEXT NOT NULL DEFAULT 'booked' CHECK (status IN ('booked', 'cancelled')),
  CHECK (ends_at > starts_at)
);

CREATE INDEX idx_appointments_staff_start ON appointments(staff_id, starts_at);

CREATE VIEW double_bookings AS
SELECT a.staff_id, a.id AS first_id, b.id AS second_id
FROM appointments a
JOIN appointments b
  ON b.staff_id = a.staff_id
 AND a.id < b.id
 AND a.starts_at < b.ends_at
 AND b.starts_at < a.ends_at
WHERE a.status = 'booked' AND b.status = 'booked';

CREATE VIEW daily_schedule AS
SELECT staff_id,
       date(starts_at) AS day,
       COUNT(*) AS appointments,
       SUM((unixepoch(ends_at) - unixepoch(starts_at)) / 60) AS booked_minutes
FROM appointments
WHERE status = 'booked'
GROUP BY staff_id, date(starts_at);
--- hint
Case-insensitive uniqueness: a unique index on the expression `lower(email)`, as in the expert course's deduplication lesson. (Declaring the column `UNIQUE COLLATE NOCASE`, which makes it compare ignoring case, works too.)
--- hint
Overlap is a self-join: same staff, `a.id < b.id`, `a.starts_at < b.ends_at AND b.starts_at < a.ends_at`, both booked.
--- hint
Minutes: `(unixepoch(ends_at) - unixepoch(starts_at)) / 60`, summed per staff member and `date(starts_at)`.
